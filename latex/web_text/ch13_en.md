@epigraph Breiman2001a | Statistical Modeling: The Two Cultures
> There are two cultures in the use of statistical modeling to reach conclusions from data. One assumes that the data are generated by a given stochastic data model. The other uses algorithmic models and treats the data mechanism as unknown.

@text
The previous chapters built statistical models with a structure fixed in advance: distributions for returns, GARCH (generalised autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity) models for volatility, models for VaR (value at risk). Machine learning (ML) starts from the opposite direction: it picks a flexible prediction rule from the data and judges it by its accuracy on data it has not seen. The question of the chapter is whether these methods forecast financial risk and returns better than the models of [Chapters 7](ch7.html)–[10](ch10.html), and how such a gain is checked honestly.

The answer depends on the target. For the weekly volatility of the S&P 500, the lasso and a small neural network reduce the squared forecast error by 7.1% and 8.3% relative to the HAR (heterogeneous autoregressive) model, and the differences are significant. For Bitcoin, no model beats HAR. For the sign of tomorrow's return, no model is significantly better than the rule "always up", on any market studied. For VaR 1%, a linear quantile regression has the right violation rate on the S&P 500, while its flexible version runs short of data in the tail.

The difference between a real result and an apparent one lies in validation. A random split of the data produces an $R^2$ of 34.5% for a simulated random walk, which has nothing to predict; a walk-forward scheme gives a negative $R^2$ for the same data. The chapter therefore builds, before the models, the rules of evaluation: samples separated in time, simple benchmarks, tests of the difference between forecasts and a correction for the number of models tried.

# What Machine Learning Adds to Statistics

The section places machine learning in the history of statistics. It starts from the perceptron of 1958, presents Breiman's distinction between data modelling and algorithmic modelling, separates prediction from inference and fixes the notation of supervised learning: features, target, loss function and hyperparameters. It ends with a map of the areas of finance where these methods help and those where they struggle.

## The Perceptron, 1958 {h=9ce20987}

@Rosenblatt1958 described a machine that learns to classify images by adjusting its weights after each error. The perceptron receives a vector of inputs $x$, in the original version the signals of photocells, and outputs 1 if $w_0 + w^\top x > 0$ and 0 otherwise. The weights $w$ and the threshold $w_0$ are not fixed by the designer but learned from examples: when the machine makes a mistake, the weights move in the direction that would have corrected it.

The Mark I Perceptron, built at Cornell in 1960, had 400 photocells, and its weights were set by electric motors. The central idea outlived this mechanical implementation: a linear decision rule, with parameters estimated by repeatedly correcting errors, is the ancestor of the neuron in the modern networks presented in [Section 6](#sec-6).

Three later milestones mark the development of the field. In 1986, @RumelhartHintonWilliams1986 showed how back-propagation of the error trains networks with hidden layers. In 2001 came random forests [@Breiman2001b] and gradient boosting [@Friedman2001], the tree methods used in [Section 5](#sec-5). In 2024, the Nobel Prize in Physics was awarded to Hopfield and Hinton for their contributions to neural networks.

## Two Cultures of Statistical Modelling {h=379dae3c}

@Breiman2001a, a statistician at Berkeley, describes two ways of using data. In the data-modelling culture, the researcher assumes a stochastic model, for example the linear regression $y = x^\top\beta + \varepsilon$ or a GARCH model, estimates it and tests its parameters. The conclusions concern the parameters, and their validity depends on the assumed model being right.

In the algorithmic-modelling culture, the mechanism that generated the data is treated as unknown. The goal is a rule $\hat f$ that predicts $y$ well on new data, and a rule is judged by its prediction error, not by the plausibility of a model. Trees, random forests and neural networks belong to this culture.

The course has so far worked in the first culture, from [Chapter 2](ch2.html) to [Chapter 11](ch11.html): distributions, volatility models, tests of long memory. This chapter adds the second culture and asks under what conditions it brings a real gain on financial data, where noise is large and only one history is available.

## Prediction against Inference {h=74ec77d1}

Inference asks whether a coefficient $\beta_j$ differs from zero and how large the effect is, using standard errors and tests, as in [Chapter 6](ch6.html). An example from the course is the leverage effect: the asymmetry parameter of a GJR-GARCH (Glosten–Jagannathan–Runkle GARCH) model, from [Chapter 9](ch9.html), says whether price falls raise volatility more than rises.

Prediction asks how close the forecast $\hat y$ is to the value $y$ on data the model has never seen. Typical examples are next week's volatility and tomorrow's VaR 1%. The coefficients of a prediction model may be hard to interpret, and sometimes do not even exist as parameters with an economic meaning; only the error on new data matters.

The two goals can conflict. A biased estimator, such as ridge or the lasso of [Section 4](#sec-4), can have a smaller prediction error than the unbiased OLS (ordinary least squares) estimator, because it gains more by reducing variance than it loses through bias. Conversely, a model that predicts well does not prove a causal mechanism: it may exploit a stable correlation without saying anything about its cause.

## Supervised Learning: Notation and Loss (1/2) {h=b48a2081}

Supervised learning starts from pairs $(x_t, y_t)$, $t = 1, \dots, n$, where $t$ indexes the observations, for example days or weeks. The vector $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^p$ contains the features: $p$ numbers known at time $t$, such as past volatilities and returns. The target $y_t$ is the quantity we want to forecast, for example next week's volatility.

The condition "known at time $t$" is essential in finance. A feature that uses, even indirectly, information from after time $t$ makes the forecast look better than it could be in real time; [Section 3](#sec-3) treats this error in detail.

Problems are divided by the nature of the target. In regression, $y$ is a continuous variable, for example a volatility or a return. In classification, $y$ is a class: up or down, default or repayment, as in the scoring models of [Chapter 12](ch12.html). The methods of the chapter have versions for both types of problem.

## Supervised Learning: Notation and Loss (2/2) {h=0c617dea}

Learning means choosing, from a family of candidate rules $\mathcal{F}$, the rule $\hat f$ that minimises the average loss $\frac1n\sum_t L(y_t, f(x_t))$. The loss function $L(y, \hat y)$ gives the cost of the forecast $\hat y$ when $y$ is observed. The family $\mathcal{F}$ may be, for example, the set of all linear regressions or of all trees of depth 3; the richer the family, the more closely the chosen rule can adapt to the data.

The loss function must match the purpose of the forecast. The squared error $(y - \hat y)^2$, whose average is the MSE (mean squared error), is the default choice for regression. For variances, the course used QLIKE from [Chapter 8](ch8.html); for VaR, the quantile (pinball) loss from [Chapter 10](ch10.html); for probabilities, the log loss from [Chapter 12](ch12.html).

The parameters of a rule, such as coefficients or weights, are learned by estimation. A hyperparameter is chosen before estimation and controls the complexity of the rule: the depth of a tree, the penalty $\lambda$ in ridge regression, the number of neurons in a network. Choosing hyperparameters is itself a form of estimation and needs separate data, as the following sections show.

## Strengths and Limitations of Machine Learning in Finance {h=51e1395e}

Machine learning has a chance of success when the target has structure and the data are sufficient. Volatility is such a case: [Chapters 8](ch8.html) and [9](ch9.html) showed that it is persistent, so much of it can be forecast, and nonlinear effects are plausible. A second case is cross-sections with thousands of stocks and many characteristics, as in the case study of [Section 11](#sec-11).

The main difficulty is a low signal-to-noise ratio. Daily returns are almost unpredictable ([Chapter 7](ch7.html)), and a flexible model applied to them mostly learns noise. Markets change regimes, and crises bring situations absent from the training history, so the past is an imperfect training set for the future.

There is also a limit of principle: we have a single price history. The experiment cannot be repeated, the observations are dependent over time, and the real number of independent episodes, for example of crises, is small. These features explain why validation takes as important a place in the chapter as the models.

## @end

Machine learning adds to statistics the culture of algorithmic modelling: a prediction rule chosen from the data and judged by its error on new data. The ingredients are the features, the target, the loss function and the hyperparameters. In finance, the approach has a chance where the target has structure, as volatility does, and struggles where noise dominates, as with daily returns. [The next section](#sec-2) explains why flexibility has a cost.

# Overfitting, Bias and Variance

The section shows why a more flexible model is not automatically a better model. It distinguishes training error from test error, derives the decomposition of test error into bias, variance and noise, and illustrates the trade-off between them by a simulation with regression trees of different depths.

## Training Error and Test Error {h=48899c48}

The training error is the average loss on the data used for estimation; the test error is the average loss on new data generated by the same process. The first always falls when the model becomes more flexible, because a richer family of rules also contains the simpler rules. Training error therefore cannot be used to compare models of different complexity.

Overfitting occurs when the model learns, besides the signal, the noise of the training sample. The symptom is a small training error together with a large test error. The extreme case is a tree with one leaf per observation: it reproduces the training data exactly, so its training error is zero, but its forecasts for new observations are poor.

Underfitting is the opposite error: the model is too rigid to capture the signal. An example is a constant volatility forecast in a market with volatility clustering: it ignores exactly the structure that makes volatility predictable.

## The Bias–Variance Decomposition (1/2) {h=af514fa4}

Assume the data are generated by $y = f(x) + \varepsilon$, with $E[\varepsilon] = 0$ and $\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon) = \sigma^2$. The function $f$ is the true, unknown relation between $x$ and $y$, and $\varepsilon$ is the noise, unpredictable from $x$. The estimate $\hat f$ is obtained on a random training sample; another sample would give another estimate, so $\hat f(x_0)$ is a random variable.

For a new point $x_0$, with $y_0 = f(x_0) + \varepsilon_0$, the expected test error splits into three terms:

$$ E\big[(y_0 - \hat f(x_0))^2\big] = \big(E[\hat f(x_0)] - f(x_0)\big)^2 + \mathrm{Var}\big(\hat f(x_0)\big) + \sigma^2. $$ {#eq:bv}

The expectation is taken over all possible training samples and over the noise $\varepsilon_0$. The first term is the squared bias: the distance between the average estimate and the true value. The second is the variance: how much $\hat f(x_0)$ changes from one sample to another. The third is the noise, which does not depend on the model.

## The Bias–Variance Decomposition (2/2) {h=f4612d2b}

The derivation starts by writing the error at $x_0$ as a sum of three terms: $y_0 - \hat f = (f - E\hat f) + (E\hat f - \hat f) + \varepsilon_0$. Squaring and taking expectations, the three squares give the squared bias, the variance and $\sigma^2$. The cross terms have mean zero: the first term is a constant, $E[E\hat f - \hat f] = 0$, and $\varepsilon_0$ is independent of the training sample.

The decomposition @eq:bv explains the bias–variance trade-off. A rigid model has large bias and small variance: it gives almost the same forecast on any sample, but a systematically wrong one. A flexible model has small bias and large variance: it fits well on average, but its forecast depends strongly on the particular sample. The optimal complexity balances the two terms.

The third term, $\sigma^2$, is a floor: no model goes below it. For daily returns, $\sigma^2$ is almost all of the error, because the predictable part $f(x)$ is very small. In this situation, any extra variance brought by a flexible model shows up directly in the test error, and the possible gain from reducing bias is small.

## Bias and Variance of Regression Trees {h=f0588b3d}

The simulation makes the decomposition visible. The data are generated by $y = \sin(2\pi x) + \varepsilon$, with noise variance $\sigma^2 = 0.09$ and $n = 80$ observations, and the experiment is repeated on 300 training samples. The left panel shows a single sample and the trees of depth 1, 3 and 10 estimated on it; regression trees are defined in [Section 5](#sec-5).

The depth-1 tree has a single split and cannot follow the shape of the sine wave: its squared bias is 0.070 and its variance 0.031. The depth-10 tree follows every point: its squared bias is practically zero (0.000), but its variance rises to 0.090, as large as the noise variance. Between the two extremes, the test error is lowest at depth 3, at 0.132.

The minimum, 0.132, is only a little above the floor $\sigma^2 = 0.09$: in this simulation the signal is strong, and a model of medium complexity captures it almost completely. The chart shows the three components of @eq:bv as functions of depth and the U shape of the test error that results from their sum.

## Interpretation: the U-Shaped Test Error {h=59d1428d}

As depth increases, the training error falls from 0.172 to 0.002. The test error falls at first, while the reduction in bias dominates, and then rises, when the increase in variance becomes more important. The depth-10 tree has a training error close to zero and a test error of 0.180, larger than that of the depth-3 tree.

The practical consequence is that a model is never chosen by its training error: that would always favour the most flexible model. Complexity is a hyperparameter and must be chosen on data that were not used to estimate the parameters.

In finance, noise is much larger relative to signal than in this simulation. The minimum of the U curve then moves towards simpler models, so the best model is usually simpler than the depth-3 tree of the example.

## @end

Test error splits into squared bias, variance and noise, @eq:bv. Flexibility reduces bias and increases variance, hence the U shape of the test error, and the noise sets a floor that no model goes below. Complexity is therefore chosen on data not used for estimation, which requires a validation scheme suited to time series, the subject of [the next section](#sec-3).

# Validation for Time Series

The section sets the rules by which all the models of the chapter are evaluated. It defines the roles of the training, validation and test samples, the two errors that make a model look better than it is, look-ahead bias and leakage, and the validation schemes suited to time series. An experiment with a simulated random walk shows how large the effect of a wrong scheme can be.

## Training, Validation and Test Samples {h=ffa5c992}

Data have three roles. The training sample is used to estimate the parameters of each candidate model. The validation sample is used to choose the hyperparameters and the model. The test sample is used for a single measurement of the error of the finally chosen model; only this measurement honestly estimates performance on new data.

Any decision taken on the basis of the test sample turns it into a validation sample. Trying ten models on the test data and reporting the best is no longer testing but model selection, and the reported error is optimistic. [Section 10](#sec-10) quantifies this optimism for Sharpe ratios.

K-fold cross-validation (CV) splits the data into $K$ parts; each part is in turn the validation set, and the other $K - 1$ parts train the model. The scheme is valid for i.i.d. data. For time series, @BergmeirHyndmanKoo2018 show that it remains valid only under strict conditions, when no dependence is left in the model's errors.

## Look-Ahead Bias and Leakage {h=7722528a}

Look-ahead bias occurs when the forecast for day $t$ uses information that was not available at the close of day $t - 1$. The usual sources are features computed with future prices, for example a centred moving average or a full-sample mean, scaling or standardisation with full-sample statistics, data revised later, and using today's index membership for the past (survivorship).

Leakage is a related error: information about the test target reaches the training sample. The typical case is overlapping targets. The 21-day return starting on day $t$ and the one starting on day $t + 1$ share 20 days. If a random split puts day $t + 1$ in training and day $t$ in the test set, the model has seen almost all of the answer before being tested.

Both errors make a model look better than it can be in real time, and neither shows up in the training error or in code that runs without errors. They must be ruled out by the construction of the validation scheme, not discovered afterwards.

## Three Validation Schemes {h=cc3e5fc7}

The chart compares three schemes. Walk-forward validation trains the model on the past, tests it on the next block and then moves forward. In the expanding-window version, each step keeps the whole past in the training sample. The scheme mimics exactly the situation of an analyst forecasting in real time.

The second scheme is K-fold with purging and embargo, proposed by @LopezDePrado2018. Test blocks may also lie in the middle of the sample, but the training rows whose targets overlap the test block are removed (purging), together with a few rows after it (embargo). In this way, no training target contains information from the test period.

The third scheme, random K-fold, appears in the chart as a point of comparison: it shuffles the days and ignores time order. The experiment on the following slides shows what this scheme produces on overlapping targets.

## Walk-Forward Validation, Step by Step {h=cb1652c0}

For the S&P 500, the chapter uses data starting on 8 April 2008 and one fold for each calendar year from 2013, 14 folds in all. The model is re-estimated every January on all previous days, with an expanding window, and tested on every day of that year.

The target is the average variance over the next 5 days, so the last rows before the test year have targets that end in that year. Purging removes them: for 2013 the last 5 training rows, from 24 December 2012, are dropped from training. The 2013 fold thus trains on 1,188 days and tests on 252, and the 2026 fold trains on 4,458 days.

Hyperparameters are chosen inside each training window, by a walk-forward split of the training data only; the test year influences no choice. No embargo is needed, because the test block always comes after the training block and there are no training rows after the test period.

## Leakage in Practice: Random Folds on Overlapping Targets {h=88de86ec}

The experiment uses a random forest for the return over the next 21 days, with features built from past returns and volatilities. Performance is measured by the out-of-sample $R^2$ relative to the training mean, defined in [Section 7](#sec-7): a positive value means a smaller squared error than the mean, a negative one a larger error.

The first data set is a simulated random walk, in which future returns are independent of the past. Random 5-fold validation reports an $R^2$ of 34.5%, and walk-forward validation an $R^2$ of −26.1%. On the S&P 500, with 6,631 days, the two values are 36.9% and −38.0%.

The results on real data are almost identical to those on the random walk. The similarity shows that the large $R^2$ of the random folds reflects not a predictability of the S&P 500 but the validation scheme.

## Interpretation: a Forecast of Pure Noise with R² > 30% {h=b259752d}

The random walk has nothing to predict, yet the random folds report an $R^2$ of 34.5%. The explanation is leakage: the random forest memorises neighbouring days in the training set, whose targets share 20 of 21 returns with the test day. The forecast "guesses" the target because it has already seen almost all of it.

Walk-forward validation gives a negative $R^2$: the random forest learns the noise of the training years and does worse than the simple training mean. Purging, that is removing 21 days at the boundary between training and test, changes the result little, because in walk-forward only the rows at the boundary have targets overlapping the test period.

The resulting rule is simple: with overlapping targets or dependent data, time order is never shuffled. A large $R^2$ obtained with random folds on such data says nothing about the forecasting ability of the model.

## @end

Data have three roles, training, validation and test, and the test sample is used once. Look-ahead bias and leakage make a model look better than it can be in real time; on overlapping targets, random folds give an $R^2$ above 30% even for pure noise. All the empirical results of the chapter therefore use walk-forward validation with purging. [The next section](#sec-4) starts the series of models with penalised regression.

# Penalised Regression: Ridge and Lasso

The section starts from the instability of the OLS estimator when features are many and correlated. It introduces ridge regression and the lasso, which add a penalty on the coefficients, computes them in an example with a single feature and applies them to the volatility features of the S&P 500.

## OLS with Many Correlated Features {h=07c9467a}

The OLS estimator minimises the sum of squared errors and has the closed form

$$ \hat\beta = \arg\min_\beta \sum_t (y_t - x_t^\top\beta)^2 = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y, $$

where $X$ is the $n \times p$ matrix whose row $t$ is $x_t^\top$, and $y$ is the vector of targets. The estimator is unbiased, but its variance, $\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}$, with $\sigma^2$ the error variance, becomes very large when the features are many or strongly correlated, because the matrix $X^\top X$ becomes nearly singular.

Volatility features are a typical example: today's realised variance, this week's and this month's move together. OLS can then produce large coefficients of opposite signs that offset each other in sample; out of sample, where the correlations change slightly, the offsetting no longer works and the forecasts deteriorate.

Penalised regression minimises the squared error plus a penalty on the size of $\beta$. The penalty introduces a bias towards zero but reduces the variance of the estimator. In terms of the decomposition @eq:bv, a small bias is accepted for a large reduction in variance.

## Ridge Regression (1/2) {h=0d1a1627}

@HoerlKennard1970 proposed penalising the squared coefficients:

$$ \hat\beta^{\,ridge} = \arg\min_\beta \sum_t (y_t - x_t^\top\beta)^2 + \lambda\sum_j \beta_j^2 = (X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y. $$ {#eq:ridge}

The term $\sum_j \beta_j^2$ is large when the coefficients are large, and $\lambda \ge 0$ sets the strength of the penalty; $I$ is the $p \times p$ identity matrix. For $\lambda = 0$ we obtain OLS, and for $\lambda \to \infty$ all coefficients tend to zero. The parameter $\lambda$ is a hyperparameter and is chosen by validation.

The closed form in @eq:ridge has an important consequence. The matrix $X^\top X + \lambda I$ is invertible for any $\lambda > 0$, even if $X^\top X$ is not. Ridge therefore works even when there are more features than observations, a situation in which OLS has no unique solution.

## Ridge Regression (2/2) {h=ed73b6de}

The case of a single centred feature, with mean zero, shows the mechanism. With $S_{xx} = \sum_t x_t^2$ and $S_{xy} = \sum_t x_ty_t$,

$$ \hat\beta^{\,ridge} = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx} + \lambda} = \frac{S_{xx}}{S_{xx} + \lambda}\,\hat\beta^{\,OLS}, \qquad \hat\beta^{\,OLS} = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}. $$

The factor $S_{xx}/(S_{xx} + \lambda)$ lies in the interval $(0, 1]$ and is the fraction of the OLS coefficient that ridge keeps. Shrinkage is stronger when $\lambda$ is large relative to $S_{xx}$, that is, when the feature carries little information.

In the example on the slide, $S_{xx} = 100$ and $S_{xy} = 30$. The OLS coefficient is $30/100 = 0.30$, and the ridge coefficient with $\lambda = 50$ is $30/150 = 0.20$, with a shrinkage factor of 0.67. Ridge shrinks all coefficients towards zero but sets none of them exactly to zero: for any finite $\lambda$, the factor stays positive.

## The Lasso {h=d957aa84}

The lasso, proposed by @Tibshirani1996, replaces the squares by absolute values:

$$ \hat\beta^{\,lasso} = \arg\min_\beta \sum_t (y_t - x_t^\top\beta)^2 + \lambda\sum_j |\beta_j|. $$

The absolute value has a kink at zero, and the solution can stop at this point: some coefficients become exactly zero. Besides shrinkage, the lasso thus performs variable selection.

For a single feature, the solution is soft thresholding: $\hat\beta^{\,lasso} = \mathrm{sign}(S_{xy})\,\max(|S_{xy}| - \lambda/2,\, 0)/S_{xx}$, where $\mathrm{sign}(S_{xy})$ is the sign, $+1$ or $-1$. If $|S_{xy}| \le \lambda/2$, the coefficient is exactly zero. With the same data as for ridge and $\lambda = 20$, the coefficient is $(30 - 10)/100 = 0.20$; for $\lambda \ge 60$ the feature leaves the model.

In practice, the features are standardised before estimation, because the penalty depends on their units; the mean and standard deviation used for standardisation are computed on the training data only, otherwise look-ahead bias appears. The parameter $\lambda$ is chosen by walk-forward cross-validation. The elastic net combines the two penalties, $L_1$ and $L_2$.

## Ridge and Lasso Paths for the Volatility Features {h=07b61e03}

The chart shows the coefficient paths as functions of $\lambda$ for a real problem. The target is the log of the average variance proxy over the next 5 days for the S&P 500, in 2008–2012 (1,193 days), and the features are the 11 standardised features of [Section 7](#sec-7). For large $\lambda$ all coefficients are close to zero; as $\lambda$ falls, they approach the OLS values.

The value chosen by walk-forward cross-validation is $\lambda = 0.0079$. At this value, the lasso keeps 10 of the 11 features; the absolute return leaves the model. The coefficient on the weekly variance rises from +0.199 in OLS to +0.283 in the lasso.

The rise of the weekly coefficient seems to contradict the idea of shrinkage, but it is explained by correlations. When a correlated feature leaves the model or is shrunk, part of its weight passes to the remaining features. Shrinkage concerns the norm of the coefficient vector, not each coefficient separately.

Three of the features are exactly collinear: the absolute return is $|r| = r - 2\min(r, 0)$, so the matrix of the 11 features has rank 10. OLS therefore has no unique solution for the coefficients of $r$, $\min(r, 0)$ and $|r|$: any shift along the direction of this identity leaves the fit unchanged, and the values reported are the minimum-norm solution. The coefficients of the other eight features, including the weekly one, are unique. The lasso resolves the ambiguity in its own way, by setting the coefficient of $|r|$ to zero.

## Interpretation of the Paths {h=dad3c3b4}

In the left panel, ridge shrinks all coefficients smoothly and together, and correlated features share their weight. The negative return keeps its negative sign to the end: market falls raise future volatility, which corresponds to the leverage effect of [Chapter 9](ch9.html).

In the right panel, the lasso brings in the features one at a time as $\lambda$ falls. The weekly variance enters first, because it is the most informative feature on its own. At the chosen value of $\lambda$, the lasso keeps the HAR structure, with daily, weekly and monthly components, plus the negative return, with coefficient −0.112.

The lasso selects features but does not test them. A coefficient equal to zero is not the equivalent of a large p-value: it only shows that, at this $\lambda$, the feature does not improve the forecast enough to pay for its penalty. Among strongly correlated features, the lasso's choice can be unstable from one sample to another.

## @end

Ridge, @eq:ridge, and the lasso stabilise regression by penalising the coefficients: ridge shrinks all coefficients without setting any to zero, while the lasso sets some of them exactly to zero. On the volatility features of the S&P 500, the lasso recovers the HAR structure plus the leverage effect. Both remain linear models; [the next section](#sec-5) turns to trees, which capture nonlinearities and interactions.

# Trees, Random Forest and Gradient Boosting

The section builds regression trees, from the splitting rule to an example computed by hand and a tree estimated on S&P 500 volatility. It then shows the two ways of combining trees, the averaging of a random forest and the sequence of gradient boosting, and compares them on the volatility data.

## Regression Trees {h=aade49ab}

A regression tree splits the feature space into rectangles and predicts, in each rectangle, the mean of $y$ within it. The final rectangles are called leaves. Each split uses a feature $j$ and a threshold $s$: observations with $x_j \le s$ go to the left, those with $x_j > s$ to the right.

The tree is estimated greedily: at each node, the pair $(j, s)$ is chosen that minimises the sum of squared errors (SSE),

$$ \mathrm{SSE} = \sum_{\text{left}}(y - \bar y_L)^2 + \sum_{\text{right}}(y - \bar y_R)^2, $$

where $\bar y_L$ and $\bar y_R$ are the means of $y$ in the two groups, that is, the two forecasts. The procedure is repeated in each half until a stopping rule is met, for example a maximum depth or a minimum number of observations in a leaf.

Trees capture nonlinear effects and interactions without these being specified in advance, and they are insensitive to monotone transformations of the features, since they depend only on the order of the values. Their weak point is high variance: a small change in the data can change the first split and, through it, the whole tree, as in the simulation of [Section 2](#sec-2).

## Worked Example: One Split, Step by Step {h=5243d258}

The example uses six weeks. The feature $x$ is this week's volatility, in percent, with values 0.6, 0.8, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 2.4, and the target $y$ is next week's volatility, with values 0.7, 0.9, 0.8, 1.6, 2.1, 2.2. Without any split, the forecast is the mean $\bar y = 1.38$, and the SSE is 2.268.

The candidate thresholds are the midpoints between consecutive values of $x$: 0.70, 0.90, 1.25, 1.75, 2.20. For each threshold, the two means and the SSE are computed. The threshold 0.70 leaves a single observation on the left and gives an SSE of 1.708; the threshold 1.25 separates the first three weeks from the last three, with leaves 0.80 and 1.97 and an SSE of 0.227, the smallest value in the table.

The best split is therefore $s = 1.25$. It explains $1 - 0.227/2.268 = 0.90$ of the variation of the target: a single question, "is current volatility above 1.25%?", separates calm weeks from turbulent ones. A deeper tree would repeat the same search in each of the two groups.

The tree in the chart is estimated on the S&P 500 in 2008–2012, with target and features expressed as the log of variance (in %² per day). At each node, *value* is the mean of the target for the observations in that node, hence the tree's forecast if it stopped there.

The depth-2 tree has four leaves, hence four possible forecast levels. It is easy to read, but its forecast is a step function of current volatility; random forests and boosting, on the following slides, smooth these steps by combining many trees.

## Random Forest {h=c0f878f2}

A random forest [@Breiman2001b] averages $B$ deep trees, each grown on a bootstrap sample of the training data, a method called bagging (bootstrap aggregating); the bootstrap was introduced in [Chapter 2](ch2.html). In addition, only a random subset of features is tried at each split. This second source of randomness makes the trees less correlated with each other.

The effect of averaging is seen in a simple calculation. If each of the $B$ trees has variance $\sigma^2$ and the correlation between any two is $\rho$, then

$$ \mathrm{Var}(\text{average}) = \rho\sigma^2 + (1 - \rho)\frac{\sigma^2}{B}. $$ {#eq:rfvar}

The second term falls with $B$, the first does not. With $\rho = 0.3$ and $\sigma^2 = 1$, the variance is 1.000 for one tree, 0.370 for 10 trees and 0.302 for 300 trees, close to the floor $\rho\sigma^2 = 0.3$.

Two practical conclusions follow from @eq:rfvar. More trees do not cause overfitting; they only bring the variance closer to the floor. The floor itself falls if the trees are less correlated, for example by trying fewer features at each split. The chapter uses 300 trees, at least 20 observations per leaf and half of the features tried at each split.

## Gradient Boosting {h=b0ffebe7}

Gradient boosting [@Friedman2001] builds the model step by step, each new small tree learning what the earlier trees missed. It starts from the constant forecast $F_0 = \bar y$. At step $m$, a small tree $h_m$ is fitted to the residuals $y_t - F_{m-1}(x_t)$, and the model is updated:

$$ F_m = F_{m-1} + \nu\, h_m, \qquad 0 < \nu \le 1, $$

where $\nu$ is the learning rate. For squared loss, the residuals equal minus the gradient of the loss with respect to the forecast; hence the name of the method, which is gradient descent in the space of functions.

The two ensemble methods attack different terms of @eq:bv. A random forest uses deep trees, with small bias, in parallel, and averaging reduces their variance. Boosting uses small trees, with large bias, in sequence, and each step reduces the bias. This is why, in boosting, too many steps cause overfitting, while in a random forest they do not.

The method works with any differentiable loss, replacing the residuals by minus the gradient of that loss. With the quantile loss we obtain quantile gradient boosting, used for VaR in [Section 9](#sec-9).

The comparison uses the S&P 500, with training in 2008–2018 (2,698 days) and validation in 2019–2022 (1,008 days). The chart shows validation MSE as a function of depth, for the single tree and for the random forest, and as a function of the number of trees, for boosting. The dashed lines on the right are training MSE, and the dotted line is the HAR regression, with validation MSE 0.487.

The single tree reaches its minimum of 0.518 at depth 5, above HAR, and at depth 14 its error rises to 0.819. The random forest reaches 0.458 at depth 8 and stays at 0.465 at depth 14, below HAR at every depth in the chart.

## Interpretation: Averaging and Early Stopping {h=9bf93312}

The single tree overfits quickly, while the random forest is almost insensitive to depth: averaging removes most of the variance of the deep trees. In this validation period, the random forest also beats the HAR regression.

In boosting, the validation error reaches a minimum and then rises, while the training error keeps falling. With learning rate 0.3, the minimum is 0.457 after 13 trees, and after 500 trees the error climbs to 0.568. With learning rate 0.03, the minimum is 0.451 after 154 trees, and after 500 trees the error is 0.461. Small steps make the error less sensitive to the exact number of trees.

Early stopping, that is choosing the number of trees on the validation data, is therefore part of boosting estimation. The number of trees in boosting is a hyperparameter; the number of trees in a random forest is not, because more trees only reduce variance.

## @end

A regression tree splits the data by greedily chosen thresholds and has high variance. A random forest reduces variance by averaging deep, decorrelated trees, @eq:rfvar; gradient boosting reduces bias by sequentially adding small trees and needs early stopping. On S&P 500 volatility, the random forest and boosting have a validation error below that of the HAR regression, while the single tree does not. # Neural Networks

The section moves from the perceptron to the multilayer perceptron and explains activation functions, the number of parameters and training by gradient descent. It then applies networks in two Quantlets of the textbook: the neural-network ARCH model for GBP/USD and the forecast of the USD/JPY exchange rate level.

## From the Perceptron to the Multilayer Perceptron (1/2) {h=4810b54f}

The basic unit of a network is the neuron: $h = g(b + w^\top x)$. The neuron computes a weighted sum of the inputs $x$, with weights $w$ and constant $b$ (the neuron's intercept), and passes it through an activation function $g$. The result $h$ is the neuron's output.

The function $g$ is nonlinear and fixed in advance. Nonlinearity is necessary: a composition of linear functions is still linear, so a network of neurons without an activation function would be just a linear regression, however many layers it had.

The perceptron of [Section 1](#sec-1) uses a step function, with values 0 or 1. Modern networks use smooth or piecewise-linear functions, which allow the gradient to be computed: the logistic function $1/(1 + e^{-z})$, the hyperbolic tangent tanh and the ReLU (rectified linear unit), $\max(0, z)$.

## From the Perceptron to the Multilayer Perceptron (2/2) {h=a7d711a1}

A multilayer perceptron (MLP) with one hidden layer of $K$ neurons produces the forecast

$$ \hat y = c + \sum_{k=1}^{K} v_k\, g(b_k + w_k^\top x), $$ {#eq:mlp}

where $w_k$ and $b_k$ are the weights and constant of hidden neuron $k$, $v_k$ is the weight of this neuron in the output, and $c$ is the output constant. Model @eq:mlp is a linear regression in $K$ nonlinear features $g(b_k + w_k^\top x)$, but these features are not chosen by the analyst: they are learned from the data as well.

The number of parameters grows quickly. For 11 inputs and 16 neurons, the hidden layer has $11 \times 16 = 176$ weights plus 16 constants, that is 192 parameters, and the output has $16 + 1 = 17$; in total, 209 parameters.

The universal approximation theorem of @HornikStinchcombeWhite1989 shows that, given enough neurons, a single hidden layer approximates any continuous function on a compact set. The theorem guarantees flexibility, but says nothing about how much data is needed to estimate such a network without overfitting.

## A Small Network and Its Activation Functions {h=6d1a685b}

The left panel shows a network with three HAR inputs, the daily, weekly and monthly components of variance, five hidden neurons and a single output, the forecast. Each line of the diagram is an estimated weight; each hidden neuron combines the three inputs into a new feature.

The right panel compares the activation functions. The logistic and tanh functions saturate for large $|z|$: they become almost constant, so their derivative is almost zero and training slows down. The ReLU, $\max(0, z)$, is linear for $z > 0$ and does not saturate in that region, which is why networks with ReLU train faster.

The choice of activation function matters less for a small network than for a deep one. For volatility, it is more important that the inputs already contain the relevant information, as the HAR components do.

## Training a Network {h=7ef2205d}

The loss of a network is minimised by gradient descent: $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\nabla_\theta L$. The vector $\theta$ contains all weights and constants, $\nabla_\theta L$ is the gradient of the loss, and $\eta > 0$ is the learning rate, the step size. Each step moves $\theta$ in the direction in which the loss falls fastest.

Back-propagation [@RumelhartHintonWilliams1986] computes the gradient layer by layer, applying the chain rule from the output back to the inputs. An epoch is one full pass through the training data. Adam is a variant of gradient descent with adaptive steps.

The loss of a network is not convex, so different initial weights lead to different networks. The chapter fixes the random seeds and averages five networks, an ensemble. Against overfitting, the inputs and the target are standardised with training statistics, the weights get an $L_2$ penalty, as in ridge, and the number of epochs is fixed. With little data, the network must be small: here a single hidden layer with 16 neurons is enough.

## From Deep Blue to Deep Learning {h=9c53cb7d}

In 1997, IBM's (International Business Machines) Deep Blue computer beat the world chess champion. The success came mainly from search and from evaluation rules written by people, not from learning from data: Deep Blue belongs to classical artificial intelligence rather than to machine learning.

Deep learning, with networks of many layers trained on huge volumes of data, dominates image recognition and text processing today. Geoffrey Hinton, one of the authors of back-propagation, received the 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics for these contributions.

Conditions in finance are different. Price data are scarce compared with the millions of images in a training set, and the signal-to-noise ratio is low. Small networks are therefore the rule for financial forecasts, and the case study of [Section 11](#sec-11) shows that even there extra layers add little.

## Neural-Network ARCH: the Textbook Model (1/2) {h=5e856181}

The textbook @FrankeHardleHafner2019, ch. 19, writes the return as

$$ r_t = f(x_{t-1}) + s(x_{t-1})\,\varepsilon_t, $$

where $r_t$ is the return of day $t$, $x_{t-1}$ are the inputs known at the close of day $t - 1$, $f(x_{t-1})$ is the conditional mean, $s^2(x_{t-1})$ is the conditional variance, and $\varepsilon_t$ is a shock with mean 0 and variance 1. Both $f$ and $s^2$ are estimated by neural networks.

The ARCH (autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity) model of @Engle1982 is a special case, with $s^2(x) = \omega + \sum_i \alpha_i r_{t-i}^2$, $\omega > 0$ and $\alpha_i \ge 0$: the variance is a linear function of past squared returns. The network drops this form and lets the data determine how the variance depends on the past.

The NN-ARCH (neural-network ARCH) model thus keeps the mean-plus-volatility structure of [Chapter 9](ch9.html) and makes only the shape of the functions flexible. The question is whether this flexibility adds anything to ARCH and GARCH.

## Neural-Network ARCH: the Textbook Model (2/2) {h=af3362e6}

The textbook's SFEnnarch Quantlet applies the model to GBP/USD. The inputs $x$ are the last 3 returns of the exchange rate, the return on 10-year German bonds and the return on gold. Estimation has two steps: a network $\hat f$ for the mean, with residuals $e_t = r_t - \hat f(x_{t-1})$, then a network for $e_t^2$, which gives the conditional variance $\hat s^2(x_{t-1})$.

The network used, as in the Quantlet, is an RBF (radial basis function) network with 3 units. Unit $j$ computes $\exp(-\|x - c_j\|^2/s_j^2)$, where $c_j$ is its centre, obtained by clustering, $s_j$ is its width, and $\|x - c_j\|$ is the distance from $x$ to the centre. The unit is close to 1 when $x$ is near $c_j$ and close to 0 far from it; the network's output is a linear combination of the 3 units.

A technical problem arises in the second step: nothing in the network's construction forces $\hat s^2 > 0$. A network for the variance needs a floor imposed from outside, while GARCH guarantees positivity through the restrictions on its parameters.

## Neural-Network ARCH for GBP/USD {h=5ed01559}

The chart uses daily data from 7 January 2010, 4,048 days in all, and compares the annualised volatility given by the RBF network with that of a GARCH(1,1) estimated on the same data, with $\hat\alpha = 0.077$ and $\hat\beta = 0.892$.

On average, the two models agree: the mean volatility is 8.9% for the network and 8.8% for GARCH. Their paths differ, however: the correlation between them is only 0.45, and a small MLP estimated instead of the RBF network gives a similar correlation, 0.43.

The modest correlation shows that the two models do not say the same thing about when volatility is high. The next slide explains the difference through a specific episode.

## Interpretation: Neural-Network ARCH and GARCH {h=d7500476}

After the Brexit vote, the GARCH volatility peaks at 37.3% on 28 June 2016, while the network's reaches only 22.0%. The network sees only the last 3 returns: a large return raises its variance for 3 days, then the effect disappears. In the GARCH(1,1) model of [Chapter 9](ch9.html),

$$ \sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\,r_{t-1}^2 + \beta\,\sigma_{t-1}^2, $$

the term $\beta\,\sigma_{t-1}^2$, with $\sigma_{t-1}^2$ yesterday's conditional variance, carries the whole past with geometrically declining weights. Volatility clustering needs memory, not only flexibility.

The network for the mean explains 0.08% of the variation of returns in sample: exchange rates are almost a random walk, and the useful part of the NN-ARCH model is the variance, as with GARCH.

The lesson goes beyond this example. Good features, such as the HAR averages of [Section 7](#sec-7), which summarise the past over several horizons, matter more than the type of network. A flexible network with poor inputs cannot reconstruct information it does not receive.

## Forecasting an Exchange Rate Level: SFEnnjpyusd {h=f51745d0}

The textbook's SFEnnjpyusd Quantlet forecasts the yen–dollar rate from its last 3 values, with an RBF network with 3 units. The first 80% of the days are used for training, the last 20% for testing. The application in the chapter uses USD/JPY from 2000, with the split on 21 June 2021.

Forecasting the level has two pitfalls. First, a level series looks predictable because $P_t \approx P_{t-1}$, and any model that follows yesterday's value has a small error in absolute terms. The right benchmark is therefore the random walk $\hat P_t = P_{t-1}$ of [Chapter 7](ch7.html). Second, a network with bounded units cannot extrapolate. Test levels climb to 163.9 yen, while the training maximum was 134.9.

The original code rescales the test forecasts using the range of the test series, which is not known in real time: a case of look-ahead bias. The chapter's version uses the training range, the only one available at the time of the forecast.

## The RBF Network against the Random Walk {h=df9200d6}

The comparison uses the RMSE (root mean squared error), expressed in yen. On the training sample, the network has an RMSE of 2.64 and the random walk 0.66. On the test sample, the network has 27.56 and the random walk 0.89.

Out of sample, the network is 31 times worse than the rule "tomorrow = today". The huge difference comes from the second pitfall of the previous slide: when the rate leaves the training range, the RBF units approach zero and the forecast stays stuck below the observed levels.

The problem does not go away if the network forecasts returns instead of levels. On returns, the same network has an out-of-sample $R^2$ of −0.14%: there is no signal to learn, and a zero forecast is better.

# Application: Forecasting Realised Volatility

The first application compares the chapter's models on the target most favourable to them: next week's volatility. The section defines the target and the features, the HAR benchmark and three evaluation tools, out-of-sample $R^2$, QLIKE and the Diebold–Mariano test, then presents the results for the S&P 500 and Bitcoin, the forecasts in two crisis episodes and the importance of the features.

## Target and Features (1/2) {h=4b2f9fd9}

Without intraday data, daily variance is estimated from the open, high, low and close prices, with the proxy of [Chapter 8](ch8.html):

$$ v_t = o_t^2 + \tfrac12(u_t - d_t)^2 - (2\ln 2 - 1)c_t^2. $$

Here $o_t$ is the overnight log return, from the previous close to the open, and $u_t$, $d_t$, $c_t$ are the logs of the high, low and close relative to the open. The last two terms form the @GarmanKlass1980 estimator, which uses the day's range to reduce noise relative to the squared return.

The target is $y_t = \ln\big(\tfrac15\sum_{j=1}^{5} v_{t+j}\big)$, the log of realised variance over the next week, that is over the next five trading days. It is known only at the close of day $t + 5$, which makes the targets of neighbouring days overlap and calls for the purging of [Section 3](#sec-3). The log brings the distribution of the target closer to the Normal distribution and guarantees positive forecasts after applying the exponential function.

## Target and Features (2/2) {h=92a589f0}

The features are computed at the close of day $t$. The HAR set contains three features: $\ln v_t$, the log of the 5-day average of $v$ (the week) and the log of the 22-day average (the month). The extended set has 11 features: in addition, 4 lags of $\ln v_t$, from $\ln v_{t-1}$ to $\ln v_{t-4}$, the 66-day average, the return $r_t$, its negative part $\min(r_t, 0)$ and its absolute value $|r_t|$.

The feature $\min(r_t, 0)$ equals the return if it is negative and 0 otherwise. It lets falls and rises have different effects on future volatility, that is, the leverage effect. A linear model with $r_t$ and $\min(r_t, 0)$ has one slope for up days and another for down days.

The extended set gives flexible models the chance to find information beyond HAR: individual lags, a longer horizon and asymmetry. If a model beats HAR, these features and the nonlinearities between them are the possible sources of the gain.

## The HAR Benchmark (1/2) {h=23dd09d1}

The HAR model of @Corsi2009 explains the log of future variance by three components:

$$ y_t = \beta_0 + \beta_d\ln v_t + \beta_w\ln v_t^{(w)} + \beta_m\ln v_t^{(m)} + u_t, $$ {#eq:har}

where $v_t^{(w)}$ and $v_t^{(m)}$ are the averages of $v$ over the last 5 days and the last 22 days, $\beta_0$ is the constant, $\beta_d$, $\beta_w$, $\beta_m$ are the weights of the three horizons, and $u_t$ is the error. The model is estimated by OLS.

The three components correspond to market participants with different horizons: daily, weekly and monthly. With positive weights on all three, a volatility shock stays in the monthly average long after it has left the daily one, so it decays slowly.

This slow decay mimics the long memory of volatility of [Chapter 11](ch11.html), without the fractional parameters of a FIGARCH model. A linear model with four coefficients that reproduces this stylised fact is a hard benchmark to beat.

## The HAR Benchmark (2/2) {h=20577a8b}

The question of the section is whether the lasso, a random forest, gradient boosting or an MLP add anything over HAR out of sample. On intraday data, the large study of @ChristensenSiggaardVeliyev2023 finds modest gains, coming mostly from the extra features.

The evaluation uses the walk-forward scheme of [Section 3](#sec-3), with yearly re-estimation: for the S&P 500 the forecasts start in 2013, for Bitcoin in 2018. All models receive the same data and the same rules for choosing hyperparameters.

The models forecast the log of variance, $\hat y_t$, but evaluation by QLIKE requires a forecast of variance in levels. The transformation is $\hat h_t = \exp(\hat y_t + \hat s^2/2)$, where $\hat s^2$ is the residual variance in training. If the error in logs is approximately Normal, the variance has a log-Normal distribution and its mean is $\exp(\hat y_t + \hat s^2/2)$; $\exp(\hat y_t)$ alone would understate the mean variance.

## Evaluation (1/3): Out-of-Sample R² {h=08dff84e}

The out-of-sample $R^2$, proposed by @CampbellThompson2008, compares the squared error of the model with that of a benchmark forecast $\tilde y_t$:

$$ R^2_{OOS} = 1 - \frac{\sum_t (y_t - \hat y_t)^2}{\sum_t (y_t - \tilde y_t)^2}, $$ {#eq:r2oos}

where $\hat y_t$ is the model's forecast and the sums run over the test days only. In the chapter, the benchmark $\tilde y_t$ is either the training mean or the HAR forecast.

The measure is at most 1. A positive value means a smaller squared error than the benchmark's, a negative one a larger error. Unlike the in-sample $R^2$, $R^2_{OOS}$ can be negative, and negative values are common when flexible models are applied to noisy targets.

For the S&P 500, the squared error of the MLP is 0.397 and that of HAR is 0.433. The ratio is 0.917, so $R^2_{OOS} = 1 - 0.917 = +8.3\%$: the MLP reduces the squared error of the HAR forecasts by 8.3%.

## Evaluation (2/3): QLIKE {h=166f297f}

QLIKE (quasi-likelihood), introduced in [Chapter 8](ch8.html), is a loss function for variance forecasts:

$$ L_t = \frac{\mathrm{RV}_t}{\hat h_t} + \ln\hat h_t, $$ {#eq:qlike}

where $\mathrm{RV}_t$ is the realised variance over the next 5 days, the target in levels, and $\hat h_t$ is its forecast. @Patton2011 shows that QLIKE is robust to using a noisy proxy instead of the true variance: the ranking of models is not distorted by the noise of the proxy.

For a given $\mathrm{RV}_t$, the loss is smallest at $\hat h_t = \mathrm{RV}_t$, and a smaller average QLIKE indicates better forecasts. The loss is asymmetric, however. For $\mathrm{RV} = 1$, the forecast $\hat h = 0.5$ costs 1.307 and the forecast $\hat h = 2$ costs 1.193, although both are off by a factor of 2.

Underestimation is therefore penalised more than overestimation. The asymmetry matches the costs in risk management, where underestimated volatility leads to insufficient capital and VaR violations, that is, to the more costly error.

## Evaluation (3/3): the Diebold–Mariano Test {h=523602f5}

The @DieboldMariano1995 (DM) test asks whether two models $A$ and $B$ have the same expected loss. Starting from the difference of the losses on each day,

$$ d_t = L_t^{A} - L_t^{B}, \qquad \mathrm{DM} = \frac{\bar d}{\sqrt{\widehat{\mathrm{LRV}}/n}}, $$ {#eq:dm}

where $\bar d$ is the mean of $d_t$ and $n$ is the number of test days. Under the hypothesis of equal accuracy, the DM statistic is approximately standard Normal.

The denominator uses the long-run variance $\widehat{\mathrm{LRV}}$ of $d_t$, estimated by Newey–West with 5 lags. The correction is needed because the overlapping 5-day targets make the loss differences autocorrelated; the plain variance would understate the uncertainty of $\bar d$ and make the test too lenient.

The sign of the statistic gives the direction: a negative DM means that model $A$ has the smaller loss, a positive DM that $B$ is better. If $|\mathrm{DM}| > 1.96$, the difference is significant at the 5% level. In the following table, $A$ is each machine learning model and $B$ is HAR.

## Feature Importance: Permutation and Shapley Values {h=432b2583}

Permutation importance shuffles the values of one feature in the test data and measures how much the test MSE rises. A large rise shows that the model relies on that feature. Correlated features share their importance, however: if one is shuffled, the model can use another similar one, so each looks less important than the group.

Shapley values, from game theory, decompose each forecast: $\hat f(x) = \phi_0 + \sum_j \phi_j(x)$. Here $\phi_0$ is the average forecast, and $\phi_j(x)$ is the contribution of feature $j$ to this forecast: positive if the feature raises the forecast above the average, negative if it lowers it. The contribution $\phi_j$ is the average change in $\hat f$ when $j$ is added to each subset of the other features. SHAP (Shapley additive explanations), by @LundbergLee2017, offers fast versions for trees; for a linear model, $\phi_j = \beta_j(x_j - \bar x_j)$.

Both tools describe the model, not the economy. An important feature is one the model uses for forecasting, which does not mean that it causes future volatility.

# Application: the Sign of Tomorrow's Return

The second application is a classification problem: forecasting the direction of tomorrow's return. The section defines the target and the models, sets the right benchmark, the majority class, and a test against it, then compares the accuracy of the models for the S&P 500, the BET and Bitcoin.

## A Classification Problem {h=d2478858}

The target is $y_t = \mathbf{1}\{r_{t+1} > 0\}$, where $\mathbf{1}\{\cdot\}$ is the indicator function, equal to 1 if the statement is true and 0 otherwise; $y_t = 1$ means that tomorrow's return is positive. The features, computed at the close of day $t$, are the last 5 returns, the sums of returns over 5, 21 and 63 days and the volatility over 21 and 63 days.

The models are the logit regression of [Chapter 12](ch12.html) and random forest and gradient boosting classifiers, estimated with the walk-forward scheme and yearly re-estimation. The data are the S&P 500 and the BET (Bucharest Exchange Trading), the benchmark index of the Bucharest Stock Exchange (BVB), from 2008, and Bitcoin from 2018. The measures, defined in [Chapter 12](ch12.html), are accuracy, the share of days with the sign forecast correctly, and the AUC (area under the ROC curve), the area under the ROC (receiver operating characteristic) curve, for which 0.5 means no ranking ability.

Expectations are modest for theoretical reasons. Weak-form efficiency, from [Chapter 7](ch7.html), says that any reliable pattern in past returns would be exploited by trading and would disappear. A model that nevertheless finds such a pattern must be checked all the more carefully.

## The Majority-Class Baseline {h=c6c44f54}

The benchmark for classification is the majority class: always forecast the more frequent class in the training sample, here "up". For the S&P 500 since 2008, 54.4% of days are up days, so the rule "always up" is right on 54.4% of days. An accuracy of 53% looks like skill, being above 50%, but against this benchmark it is a loss.

The difference from the benchmark is tested with the statistic

$$ z = \frac{\widehat{\mathrm{acc}} - \mathrm{acc}_0}{\sqrt{\mathrm{acc}_0(1 - \mathrm{acc}_0)/n}}, $$

where $\widehat{\mathrm{acc}}$ is the model's accuracy, $\mathrm{acc}_0$ the benchmark's accuracy and $n$ the number of test days. If the model has no real edge, $z$ is approximately standard Normal. For the S&P 500, with $n = 4{,}705$, the standard error is 0.73 percentage points, so the 95% band around the benchmark has a width of ±1.4 points.

The random forest has an accuracy of 53.1%, with $z = -1.79$ (p 0.074), and boosting 52.1%, with $z = -3.19$ (p 0.001): boosting is significantly worse than the rule "always up". For forecasts of the returns themselves, the corresponding benchmark is the zero forecast or the historical mean [@WelchGoyal2008].

## Accuracy against the Baseline {h=ad2ed87d}

The chart shows, for each market and each model, accuracy minus the benchmark's accuracy, in percentage points. The grey band covers ±1.96 standard errors around zero: a model inside the band does not differ significantly from the rule "always up".

The AUC confirms the picture. For the logit and the random forest, the AUC is 0.511 and 0.507 on the S&P 500, 0.533 and 0.533 on the BET, 0.515 and 0.519 on Bitcoin. All values are very close to 0.5, that is, the models hardly rank the days by their probability of an up move.

The logit forecasts "up" on 86% of S&P 500 days. Its estimated probabilities are almost constant, slightly above 0.5, so the logit behaves almost like the benchmark. This explains why it loses little against the benchmark, while the flexible models lose more.

## Interpretation: Why Sign Prediction Mostly Fails {h=3fe9eb3c}

On no market is any model significantly better than the rule "always up", and the flexible models are worse than the logit. On the S&P 500, the differences from the benchmark are −0.6 points for the logit, −1.3 for the random forest and −2.3 for boosting; on Bitcoin, +0.1 for the logit and −0.9 for the random forest.

The explanation is the very low signal-to-noise ratio of daily returns. In terms of the decomposition @eq:bv, almost all of the error is noise. The flexible models find patterns in the noise of the training years, as in [Section 2](#sec-2), and apply them to years in which the patterns no longer exist; the logit stays close to the benchmark precisely because it barely moves.

Even a real edge of one percentage point would rarely remain profitable: a daily strategy based on the sign trades very often, and transaction costs would eat the gain. The documented gains of machine learning in return forecasting come from large cross-sections at monthly horizons, not from a single index for the next day, as the case study of [Section 11](#sec-11) shows.

## @end

No model, on any market, is significantly better than this rule; the AUC stays close to 0.5, and the flexible models are worse than the logit. # Application: VaR 1% by Quantile Methods

The third application forecasts VaR 1% directly as a conditional quantile. The section introduces quantile regression and the quantile loss, its version with boosted trees, and compares them by backtesting with historical simulation and with GARCH-t on the S&P 500 and Bitcoin.

## Quantile Regression (1/2) {h=2083f3fa}

The quantile regression of @KoenkerBassett1978 estimates the conditional quantile of order $\alpha$, $q_\alpha(x) = x^\top\beta_\alpha$. The quantile $q_\alpha(x)$ is the level below which $y$ lies with probability $\alpha$, conditional on $x$, and the coefficients $\beta_\alpha$ are specific to the level $\alpha$: the same feature can have different effects on the tail and on the centre of the distribution.

Estimation minimises the quantile loss. For an error $u = y - q$,

$$ \rho_\alpha(u) = u\,(\alpha - \mathbf{1}\{u < 0\}). $$ {#eq:pinball}

An observation below the quantile ($u < 0$) costs $(1 - \alpha)|u|$, and one above it costs $\alpha|u|$. For $\alpha = 0.5$ the two costs are equal and we obtain median regression.

With $\alpha = 0.01$, observations below the quantile are 99 times more expensive than those above it. The minimum of the average loss is reached when about 1% of the observations lie below $q$, which is exactly the property that defines the 1% quantile.

## Quantile Regression (2/2) {h=d2a99bd9}

With $\alpha = 0.01$ and $q = -\mathrm{VaR}$, the loss @eq:pinball becomes the average quantile loss of [Chapter 10](ch10.html): $(\alpha - I_t)(r_t + \mathrm{VaR}_t)$, where $I_t = 1$ if $r_t < -\mathrm{VaR}_t$, that is, if a violation occurs, and 0 otherwise. The same function thus serves both for estimating and for evaluating VaR forecasts.

VaR 1% for day $t + 1$ is $\widehat{\mathrm{VaR}}_t = -\hat q_{0.01}(x_t)$, with features $x_t$: the daily, weekly and monthly volatility, computed as square roots of the proxy, and the return $r_t$. No distribution of returns is assumed: the data choose how volatility translates into the 1% tail. CAViaR [@EngleManganelli2004] is a dynamic version of this idea, in which yesterday's quantile enters the equation for today's quantile.

Quantile gradient boosting (QGB) uses the same loss, with trees added by boosting as in [Section 5](#sec-5). It is more flexible than linear regression, but at the 1% level each leaf sees few observations from the tail, so the variance of the estimate is high.

## Backtest of VaR 1%: Quantile Methods, HS and GARCH-t (1/2) {h=70922a05}

The table compares four methods. QR is linear quantile regression, and QGB is quantile gradient boosting. The benchmarks are 500-day historical simulation (HS) and a GARCH-t re-estimated every January, both from [Chapter 10](ch10.html).

Column $x$ gives the number of violations, the days with $r_{t+1} < -\widehat{\mathrm{VaR}}_t$, among the $n$ test days, and the rate is $x/n$, with target 1%. "Kupiec p" is the p-value of the @Kupiec1995 test of the violation rate, and "ind. p" is the p-value of the @Christoffersen1998 independence test. The average quantile loss, multiplied by 100, measures the quality of the forecasts as a whole (lower is better), and "mean VaR" is the average VaR 1%, in percent.

On the S&P 500, QR has 28 violations, a rate of 0.81% and the smallest loss, 3.38; GARCH-t has 52 violations, a rate of 1.51% and a Kupiec p of 0.005. On Bitcoin, HS has 32 violations, a rate of 1.01%, and QGB has 47 violations, with a rate of 1.48%. Mean VaR ranges from 2.42% (GARCH-t) to 2.97% (HS) for the S&P 500 and from 8.52% (GARCH-t) to 10.46% (QR) for Bitcoin.

## Backtest of VaR 1%: Quantile Methods, HS and GARCH-t (2/2) {h=942cba4a}

On the S&P 500, with 3,448 test days from 2 January 2013, quantile regression has a violation rate consistent with 1% (0.81%) and the smallest quantile loss. Its violations are not fully independent: the p-value of the independence test is 0.020. GARCH-t is violated too often, on 1.51% of days, and the Kupiec test rejects it.

On Bitcoin, with 3,182 test days, historical simulation has the best rate, 1.01%. Quantile gradient boosting is rejected by the Kupiec test, with a rate of 1.48% and a p-value of 0.012: its flexibility does not make up for the small number of observations in the tail.

Reading the p-values follows [Chapter 10](ch10.html). A Kupiec p below 0.05 shows that the violation rate differs significantly from 1%. An ind. p below 0.05 shows that violations come in clusters, that is, VaR reacts too slowly to rises in volatility and lets several violations through on consecutive days.

## VaR 1% Forecasts in 2020 and 2025 {h=262cbe3c}

The chart shows, for the S&P 500, the next-day returns and minus VaR 1% for the four methods, in 2020 and 2025; the dots mark the violations of the VaR obtained by quantile regression.

QR and GARCH-t follow volatility: when volatility rises, their threshold drops within a few days. HS reacts slowly, because the 500-day window changes only as new days enter, one at a time, and an extreme day moves the 1% quantile only after several such days have entered the window.

The difference shows in 2020: HS was violated 10 times and QR 4 times, and the HS violations come in clusters, which explains the independence-test p-value below 0.001 in the table. A method that follows current volatility avoids exactly these runs of violations.

# Data Snooping and the Deflated Sharpe Ratio

The section quantifies the optimism that arises when the best of many strategies tried on the same data is reported. It computes the expected maximum of the Sharpe ratios of strategies without skill, illustrates it with 30 moving-average rules on the S&P 500 and introduces the deflated Sharpe ratio, which corrects for the number of trials.

## The Best of N Strategies (1/2) {h=2e15c2fd}

Data snooping means trying many models or rules on the same data and reporting the best [@White2000]. With enough trials, a rule without any skill looks excellent in sample, because the maximum of many noisy estimates lies systematically above their true value. @HarveyLiuZhu2016 show that hundreds of published return predictors would not survive a correction for multiple testing.

The Sharpe ratio is the mean excess return divided by its standard deviation ([Chapter 1](ch1.html)). For a strategy without skill, the annual Sharpe ratio estimated on $Y$ years of data is approximately $N(0, 1/Y)$. The zero mean expresses the lack of skill, and the variance $1/Y$ is the estimation error, which falls with the length of the sample.

Over 10 years, the standard deviation of the estimated Sharpe ratio is $1/\sqrt{10} = 0.316$. A strategy without skill thus has a reasonable chance of showing a Sharpe ratio of 0.3 or more by luck alone, and when many strategies are tried, the best of them will look much better.

## The Best of N Strategies (2/2) {h=00b8e359}

@BaileyLopezDePrado2014 give an approximation for the expected maximum of $N$ independent trials without skill:

$$ \mathrm{E}[\max] \approx \sigma\Big[(1 - \gamma)\,\Phi^{-1}\big(1 - \tfrac1N\big) + \gamma\,\Phi^{-1}\big(1 - \tfrac{1}{Ne}\big)\Big], $$ {#eq:emax}

where $\sigma$ is the standard deviation of the Sharpe ratios across trials, $1/\sqrt{Y}$ in the case above, $N$ is the number of trials, $\Phi^{-1}$ is the quantile function of the standard Normal distribution, $e \approx 2.718$, and $\gamma = 0.5772$ is the Euler–Mascheroni constant.

The term $\Phi^{-1}(1 - 1/N)$ grows roughly like $\sqrt{2\ln N}$. The advantage given by luck thus grows slowly with the number of trials, but without limit: ten times more trials always add something to the best apparent result.

For 10 years, with $\sigma = 0.316$ and $N = 100$, the formula gives $0.316\,[(1 - \gamma)\,2.326 + \gamma\,2.680] = 0.80$. An annual Sharpe ratio of 0.80 therefore appears, on average, by luck alone if the best of 100 strategies is chosen. In a simulation, the best of 10, 100 and 1,000 strategies without skill has an average Sharpe ratio of 0.48, 0.79 and 1.02.

## Selection Bias in Practice {h=98a29e79}

The left panel shows the best annual Sharpe ratio among $N$ strategies without skill, over 10 years, as a function of $N$: the curve rises slowly, as in @eq:emax. The right panel moves to real data: 30 moving-average (MA) rules on the S&P 500, each invested in the index or in cash according to its signal, evaluated in 2000–2014 and then in 2015–2026.

The best rule in sample, MA 50/200, has a Sharpe ratio of 0.66. In the following period, the same rule achieves 0.54, 22nd of 30, below the buy-and-hold strategy, which has 0.72.

The in-sample and out-of-sample Sharpe ratios have a correlation of 0.04: the ranking of the rules in the first period says practically nothing about their ranking in the second. Selecting the best rule mostly selected the luck of the first period.

## The Deflated Sharpe Ratio (1/2) {h=01e9cd6c}

The deflated Sharpe ratio (DSR) of @BaileyLopezDePrado2014 is the probability that the true Sharpe ratio exceeds what the best of $N$ trials would show by luck:

$$ \mathrm{DSR} = \Phi\Big(\frac{(\widehat{SR} - SR_0)\sqrt{T - 1}}{\sqrt{1 - \hat\gamma_3\widehat{SR} + \frac{\hat\gamma_4 - 1}{4}\widehat{SR}^2}}\Big). $$ {#eq:dsr}

Here $\widehat{SR}$ is the daily Sharpe ratio of the chosen strategy, $T$ is the number of days, and $SR_0$ is the expected maximum from @eq:emax, computed with $\sigma$ equal to the standard deviation of the $N$ Sharpe ratios tried. $\hat\gamma_3$ is the skewness and $\hat\gamma_4$ the kurtosis of daily returns, with value 3 for the Normal distribution; $\Phi$ is the standard Normal distribution function.

The DSR is a probability in the interval $(0, 1)$. A value above 0.95 indicates a Sharpe ratio that is significant after correcting for the $N$ trials. The denominator measures the precision of the estimate $\widehat{SR}$: negative skewness and heavy tails, common in financial strategies, enlarge it and reduce the DSR, because a Sharpe ratio estimated from such returns is less precise.

## The Deflated Sharpe Ratio (2/2) {h=1c3009e1}

The step-by-step calculation uses the best MA rule, with $N = 30$ rules tried and $T = 3{,}769$ days. The daily Sharpe ratio is $\widehat{SR} = 0.0416$, that is $0.0416 \times \sqrt{252} = 0.66$ per year; the skewness of daily returns is $\hat\gamma_3 = -0.44$ and the kurtosis $\hat\gamma_4 = 12.0$.

The luck threshold is $SR_0 = 0.0105\,[(1 - \gamma)\,1.834 + \gamma\,2.249] = 0.0219$ per day, that is 0.35 per year. The argument of $\Phi$ in @eq:dsr is $z = 1.20$, so DSR $= \Phi(1.20) = 0.88$. Without the correction for $N$, with $SR_0 = 0$, we obtain the probabilistic Sharpe ratio (PSR), equal to 0.994.

Taken alone, the rule looks significant: the PSR is well above 0.95. After correcting for the 30 trials, the DSR falls to 0.88, below the 0.95 threshold, and the Sharpe ratio is no longer significant. The result in 2015–2026, 22nd of 30, confirms this conclusion.

## @end

The best of $N$ strategies without skill has a positive Sharpe ratio that grows with $N$, @eq:emax: over 10 years, about 0.80 for 100 trials. On the S&P 500, the best of 30 moving-average rules ends up 22nd in the following period, and the DSR, @eq:dsr, shows that its Sharpe ratio is not significant after the correction. The number of trials must therefore be reported together with the result. [The next section](#sec-11) presents a paper in which machine learning passes these tests.

# Case Study: Gu, Kelly and Xiu (2020)

The section presents the reference paper of the field, which compares machine learning methods for the monthly returns of US stocks. It describes the data and the estimation scheme, the evaluation, the statistical and economic results and their limits.

## Empirical Asset Pricing via Machine Learning {h=10750979}

@GuKellyXiu2020 ask which methods best predict the monthly return of individual US stocks. The data cover almost 30,000 stocks over 1957–2016. The features are 94 firm characteristics, interacted with 8 macroeconomic variables, plus 74 industry dummy variables: 920 features in all.

The estimation scheme is of the walk-forward type: 18 years of training (1957–1974), 12 years of validation (1975–1986) and 30 years of testing (1987–2016). The models are re-estimated once a year; the training window grows and the validation window moves forward, so that each forecast uses only earlier data.

The paper compares OLS, penalised and dimension-reduction regressions, random forests, boosted trees and neural networks with 1 to 5 hidden layers, denoted NN1–NN5, where NN$k$ has $k$ hidden layers. It is thus the same family of models as in this chapter, applied to a problem with far more data.

## Evaluation in the Paper {h=0b093433}

The authors use

$$ R^2_{OOS} = 1 - \frac{\sum_{(i,t)}(r_{i,t+1} - \hat r_{i,t+1})^2}{\sum_{(i,t)} r_{i,t+1}^2}, $$

where $r_{i,t+1}$ is the return of stock $i$ in month $t + 1$, $\hat r_{i,t+1}$ is its forecast, and the sums run over all stock–month pairs in the test period. The benchmark is a zero forecast, not the historical mean as in @eq:r2oos. The authors show that the historical mean of an individual stock is so noisy that it is easy to beat, so the zero benchmark is more demanding.

The models are compared with Diebold–Mariano tests between all pairs, adapted to a panel of stocks: the same tool as in [Section 7](#sec-7). A Bonferroni correction is applied for the 12 comparisons, in which the significance level is divided by the number of comparisons, a simple form of protection against the data snooping of [Section 10](#sec-10).

Economic value is measured through a portfolio. Each month, stocks are sorted into deciles by their forecast; the top decile is bought and the bottom decile sold. The portfolios are value-weighted, and for each one the Sharpe ratio, the drawdown and the turnover, the share of the portfolio traded monthly, are reported.

## Results: Predictive R² and Economic Value {h=f5e751fd}

The left panel reproduces the monthly $R^2_{OOS}$ from Table 1 of the paper. OLS with all 920 features has −3.46%: the unpenalised estimator with so many features learns noise. OLS-3, with three classic predictors, size, book-to-market and momentum, has 0.16%. The random forest reaches 0.33%, and the NN3 network 0.40%, or 0.70% for the largest 1,000 stocks.

The right panel turns to economic value, from Table 7 of the paper: the annual Sharpe ratio of the value-weighted decile spread portfolio. It is 0.61 for OLS-3, 0.98 for the random forest and 1.35 for NN4.

The contrast between the two panels is the main lesson of the paper. A monthly $R^2$ below 1% looks negligible, but applied to a cross-section of thousands of stocks, every month, it ranks the stocks well enough to more than double the Sharpe ratio of a long–short strategy relative to the linear model with three predictors.

## Interpretation and Limits {h=cff1b584}

The paper finds that nonlinear models, trees and networks, are superior to linear ones, because they capture interactions between firm characteristics. All methods agree on the main signals: price trends, that is momentum and short-term reversal, liquidity and volatility. Shallow networks are enough: 3–4 layers do better than 5, and deep learning does not help for monthly returns.

A monthly $R^2_{OOS}$ below 1% is, in this context, an important result. The comparison with [Section 8](#sec-8) is instructive: there, no model beat the rule "always up" for the sign of a single index on the next day.

The results also have limits. Portfolios built on the networks turn over more than 100% of their value each month, and transaction costs reduce the gains. The evidence comes from a cross-section of thousands of stocks, where the edge is obtained by ranking stocks relative to each other; it says little about timing the market for a single index.

Shallow networks are enough, and high turnover reduces the gains after costs. [The next section](#sec-12) poses an open question for the Romanian market.

## An Open Question {h=bbb978d7}

The question is whether machine learning volatility forecasts help on the Bucharest Stock Exchange, where liquidity is low and intraday data are scarce. The results so far are mixed: on the S&P 500, the lasso gains +7.1% over HAR, while on Bitcoin there is no significant gain (+2.0%, p 0.520).

An AI assistant can help in three stages. For the literature, it can draw up a list of studies on HAR and machine learning for emerging and frontier markets. ## What to Check {h=f7d6ef2b}

The first check concerns time order. Folds are not shuffled, features use only data up to the close of day $t$, and the last training targets do not reach into the test period. The third check concerns calendars. The BVB closes before the New York exchange, so an S&P 500 feature from the same calendar day was not known at the BVB close; it must come from the previous New York close. This is a form of look-ahead bias easily introduced by code that aligns series by date.

The last checks concern reporting. # Summary

The results of the chapter come down to a few statements about when machine learning helps in finance, a set of formulas and three self-check questions.

## Key Takeaways {h=2ba3fac7}

Machine learning is judged by its error on new data, and the bias–variance trade-off sets how flexible a model should be. Time series need walk-forward validation with purging: random folds on overlapping targets simulate false predictability, with an $R^2$ above 30% even for a random walk.

Ridge and the lasso shrink coefficients, a random forest averages trees, boosting adds small trees in sequence, and neural networks learn nonlinear features. For volatility, HAR is hard to beat, and the gains are a few percent; for the sign of returns, no model gains over the rule "always up".

For VaR 1%, linear quantile regression works, while flexible quantile models lack data in the tail. The number of trials is reported, the Sharpe ratio is deflated, and every model is compared with a simple benchmark.

## Key Formulas {h=19425578}

The table collects the formulas of the chapter. The bias–variance decomposition, @eq:bv, explains the U shape of test error. Ridge, @eq:ridge, has the form $S_{xy}/(S_{xx} + \lambda)$ for a single feature, and the lasso gives the soft threshold $\mathrm{sign}(S_{xy})\max(|S_{xy}| - \lambda/2, 0)/S_{xx}$. The variance of a random forest average, @eq:rfvar, has a floor $\rho\sigma^2$ that does not fall with the number of trees, and boosting updates the model by $F_m = F_{m-1} + \nu h_m$.

The multilayer perceptron, @eq:mlp, is a linear regression in learned nonlinear features. The HAR benchmark, @eq:har, combines three horizons of volatility, and $R^2_{OOS}$, @eq:r2oos, compares the error of a model with that of the benchmark on the test days.

The quantile loss, @eq:pinball, both estimates and evaluates VaR forecasts. The deflated Sharpe ratio, @eq:dsr, corrects the Sharpe ratio for the number of trials and for the skewness and kurtosis of returns.

## Check Yourself {h=079b0c3c}

The first question describes a model with a training $R^2$ of 80% and an out-of-sample $R^2$ of −5%. The diagnosis is overfitting: the model has learned the noise of the training sample and loses to the benchmark on new data.

The second question asks why random K-fold is wrong for a 21-day return target. Neighbouring targets overlap, so the test answers reach the training set; the right scheme is walk-forward with purging.

The third question describes a classifier that is right on 53% of days, in a market that rose on 54% of them. The classifier is not useful, because the rule "always up" is right on 54% of days. The next chapter, [Chapter 14](ch14.html), treats crypto assets.
