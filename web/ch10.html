A risk manager has to answer two questions every day: how much can the portfolio lose tomorrow, and was yesterday's answer right? Value at Risk (VaR) answers the first with a quantile of the loss distribution, expected shortfall (ES) with the average loss beyond that quantile, and backtesting answers the second by comparing the forecasts with the losses that actually occurred. Since 1996 these three tools have also been the language of bank regulation: the Basel rules set market-risk capital from a bank's own VaR or ES and raise it when the model fails its backtest.

The chapter builds the tools on daily data from 2000 to 2026 and judges them in two storms, the financial crisis of 2008 and the COVID-19 crash of 2020. On the S&P 500, the historical VaR 1% is 3.46% and the ES 2.5% is 3.76%, while a Normal distribution fitted to the same returns gives 2.80% and 2.81%. From September 2008 to March 2009, a rolling Normal VaR 1% of the S&P 500 was exceeded on 21 of 146 days, where a correct model would have produced about 1.5 exceptions; a VaR built on a GARCH volatility forecast was exceeded on 5 days.

The chapter therefore combines the two previous strands of the course: the tails of Chapter 5 and the volatility forecasts of Chapter 9. It shows that the choice of tail distribution matters less than tracking today's volatility, that correlation understates the risk of joint crashes, and that one year of data separates a good risk model from a bad one only roughly.

Measuring Tail Risk

The section tells how a single daily number came to summarise the risk of a bank. It starts with the request of J.P. Morgan's chairman for a daily report, follows VaR from the RiskMetrics document of 1994 to the Basel capital rules of 1996 and 2019, and introduces the two crises, 2008 and 2020, in which every method of the chapter is tested.

The 4:15 Report and RiskMetrics

Slide 5 in the PDF

Around 1990, Dennis Weatherstone, the chairman of J.P. Morgan, asked for a single number, delivered every day at 4:15 pm after the close of trading, that would say how much the bank could lose over the next day. The bank's trading desks held positions in bonds, currencies, equities and derivatives, and no individual report could add them up. The answer became the Value at Risk (VaR): a loss that is exceeded only with a small probability over a given horizon.

In October 1994 the bank published its methodology and the data needed to apply it free of charge, under the name RiskMetrics; the fourth edition of the technical document is J.P. Morgan/Reuters (1996). The method was deliberately simple: Normal returns, a volatility estimated by an exponentially weighted moving average (EWMA, the estimator of Chapter 8) and a VaR at a tail probability of 5% over one day.

The publication turned VaR into a common language. Banks, regulators and fund managers could now state their market risk in the same units, a loss in currency at a given probability, and compare positions that had nothing else in common. The simplicity that made the method popular also contains its two weak points, which the chapter examines in turn: the Normal distribution has too thin a tail, and a quantile says nothing about the size of the losses beyond it.

  • Around 1990: J.P. Morgan's chairman, Dennis Weatherstone, asks for one number, every day at 4:15 pm, summarising how much the bank could lose the next day

  • October 1994: the methods and data are published free of charge as RiskMetrics J.P. Morgan/Reuters (1996)

From RiskMetrics to Basel

Slide 6 in the PDF

The Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BCBS), hosted by the Bank for International Settlements (BIS) in Basel, sets the international standards for bank capital (history of the Basel Committee). In 1996 it allowed banks to compute the capital for market risk with their own internal VaR models (BCBS, 1996a). The rule uses VaR 1% over 10 days, multiplied by a factor of at least 3, and the factor increases when the model fails its backtest, that is, when actual losses exceed the VaR too often (BCBS, 1996b).

In 2019 the Fundamental Review of the Trading Book (FRTB) replaced VaR 1% with ES 2.5% as the basis of the capital charge (BCBS, 2019); the current rule is chapter MAR33 of the Basel Framework (BCBS, 2023b). The change answers the main criticism of VaR: a quantile ignores how bad the losses beyond it are, while ES averages them.

Backtesting did not move to ES. Under chapter MAR32 of the Basel Framework (BCBS, 2023a), banks still count the days on which the loss exceeded the VaR forecast, at the level of the bank and of each trading desk. The reason is statistical and appears in the Coherent Risk Measures section: a VaR forecast can be checked against realised losses on its own, while an ES forecast can only be checked together with a VaR forecast.

In brief

  • The BCBS (Basel Committee on Banking Supervision) sits at the BIS (Bank for International Settlements) in Basel history of the Basel Committee
    • 1996: banks may compute market-risk capital with their own VaR models BCBS (1996a)
    • rule: VaR 1% over 10 days, times a multiplier of at least 3; the multiplier grows when the model fails the backtest BCBS (1996b)
  • 2019: the FRTB (Fundamental Review of the Trading Book) BCBS (2019)
    • capital from ES 2.5% instead of VaR 1% (MAR33, BCBS (2023b)); backtesting still counts VaR exceptions (MAR32, BCBS (2023a))

VaR and ES: Definitions

The section defines VaR and ES as functions of the distribution of the profit and loss, computes them first on a toy sample of 100 days and then on 26 years of S&P 500 returns, derives the closed forms for the Normal and the Student-t distributions, and examines the rule that scales a one-day VaR to ten days.

Losses, Horizon and Level

Slide 8 in the PDF

A risk measure needs three ingredients. The first is the random variable \(X\): the profit and loss (P&L) or the return of a position over a horizon \(h\); in this chapter \(h\) is one day and \(X\) is the daily log return in percent. The loss is \(L = -X\), a positive number when the position loses money. Both conventions appear in the literature, and most errors in practice come from mixing them.

The second ingredient is the level \(\alpha\), the tail probability, for example \(\alpha = 1\%\) or \(\alpha = 2.5\%\). The course writes VaR 1% and ES 2.5%: the number in the name is always the probability of the tail. Regulatory texts and software often speak of "VaR 99%", naming the complementary confidence level; the two names describe the same number, but the course uses only the tail probability, so that a smaller number always means a more extreme loss.

The third ingredient is the quantile. The quantile of order \(\alpha\) of \(X\) is \(q_\alpha(X) = \inf\{x : P(X \le x) > \alpha\}\), the definition of Chapter 2 that also works for discrete distributions. For a continuous distribution function \(F\) it is simply \(q_\alpha = F^{-1}(\alpha)\). For small \(\alpha\) the quantile of returns is a negative number: the return below which the worst \(\alpha\) of the days fall.

In brief

  • \(X\): the P&L (profit and loss) or the return of a position over a horizon \(h\) (here one day, in %)
    • the loss is \(L = -X\): a positive number when the position loses money
  • The level \(\alpha\) is the probability of the tail: \(\alpha = 1\%\) or \(\alpha = 2.5\%\)
    • we write VaR 1% and ES 2.5%: the number in the name is always the tail probability
  • \(q_\alpha(X) = \inf\{x : P(X \le x) > \alpha\}\): the \(\alpha\)-quantile of \(X\) (Chapter 2)
    • for a continuous distribution function \(F\): \(q_\alpha = F^{-1}(\alpha)\), a negative number for small \(\alpha\)

Value at Risk

Slide 9 in the PDF

The Value at Risk at level \(\alpha\) is the negative of the \(\alpha\)-quantile of the P&L:

\[\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(X) = -q_\alpha(X).\]
(10.1)

The minus sign turns the quantile into a positive loss. The definition implies \(P(L > \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha) \le \alpha\): the loss exceeds the VaR with probability at most \(\alpha\). A daily VaR 1% of 3% therefore says that a loss larger than 3% occurs on about one day in a hundred. In money, a position worth \(W\) has a VaR of \(W \times \mathrm{VaR}(\%)/100\).

What VaR does not say matters as much. It says nothing about the size of the losses beyond it: two positions with the same 1% quantile, one of which loses 4% on its bad days and the other 40%, have the same VaR. And VaR is not the maximum loss. By construction it is exceeded on about a fraction \(\alpha\) of the days; a VaR that is never exceeded is not a prudent VaR but a wrong one, as the backtests of the Backtesting section show.

In brief

  • Definition: \(\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(X) = -q_\alpha(X)\)
    • the loss that is exceeded with probability \(\alpha\): \(P(L > \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha) \le \alpha\)
    • the minus sign makes VaR a positive loss; a VaR of 3% means “a loss of more than 3% happens on 1 day in 100”
  • What VaR does not say
    • how large the loss is on the bad days: a loss of 4% and a loss of 40% beyond VaR give the same VaR
    • VaR is not the maximum loss: by construction it is exceeded on about \(\alpha\) of the days
  • In money: a position of value \(W\) has \(\mathrm{VaR} = W \times \mathrm{VaR}(\%)/100\)

Expected Shortfall

Slide 10 in the PDF

Expected shortfall answers the question VaR leaves open: how large is the loss on the bad days? It is defined as the average of the VaR over all levels below \(\alpha\):

\[\mathrm{ES}_\alpha(X) = -\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_0^\alpha q_u(X)\,du,\]
(10.2)

where the integration variable \(u\) runs over the tail levels from 0 to \(\alpha\). For a continuous distribution the definition reduces to \(\mathrm{ES}_\alpha = -E[X \mid X \le q_\alpha(X)]\): the average loss on the worst \(\alpha\) of the days. The same quantity appears under other names, conditional VaR (CVaR), tail VaR (TVaR) or expected tail loss; for continuous distributions they coincide.

Two properties follow from the definition. Because the quantiles \(q_u\) for \(u < \alpha\) all lie below \(q_\alpha\), their average does too, so \(\mathrm{ES}_\alpha \ge \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha\) at the same level. And ES looks inside the tail: two distributions with the same VaR but different tails, one ending abruptly and one with rare catastrophic losses, have different ES. The integral form (10.2) is the general definition; it remains valid for discrete distributions, where the conditional expectation can give a different and less well-behaved number.

In brief

  • Definition: \(\mathrm{ES}_\alpha(X) = -\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\displaystyle\int_0^\alpha q_u(X)\,du\): the average of the VaRs at all levels below \(\alpha\)
    • for a continuous distribution: \(\mathrm{ES}_\alpha = -E[X \mid X \le q_\alpha(X)]\), the average loss on the worst \(\alpha\) of the days
    • \(u\): the integration variable, a tail level running from 0 to \(\alpha\)
    • other names: CVaR (conditional VaR), TVaR (tail VaR), expected tail loss
  • Properties
    • \(\mathrm{ES}_\alpha \ge \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha\) at the same level \(\alpha\)
    • ES looks inside the tail: two distributions with the same VaR but different tails have different ES

Worked Example: VaR and ES from 100 Days

Slide 11 in the PDF

With a sample, the quantile is replaced by an order statistic. A position has 100 daily returns, and the five worst are −6.1%, −4.8%, −4.2%, −3.9% and −3.5%. The empirical quantile of level \(\alpha\) is the \(k\)-th smallest return, with \(k = \lceil n\alpha \rceil\), where \(\lceil\cdot\rceil\) rounds up to the next integer.

For VaR 1%, \(k = \lceil 100 \times 0.01 \rceil = 1\): the empirical quantile is the worst return, and \(\mathrm{VaR}_{1\%} = 6.1\%\). A single observation decides this estimate, so with 100 days it is very imprecise: a different sample of 100 days would almost surely have a different worst day. For ES 2.5%, \(k = \lceil 2.5 \rceil = 3\), and the estimate is the average of the three largest losses, \(\mathrm{ES}_{2.5\%} = (6.1 + 4.8 + 4.2)/3 = 5.03\%\), while \(\mathrm{VaR}_{2.5\%}\) is the third largest loss, 4.2%.

Translated into money, a position of 1 million lei has a VaR 1% of 61,000 lei: on one day in a hundred, the loss is larger. The example also shows why ES at 2.5% and VaR at 1% are natural partners: ES 2.5% uses three observations where VaR 1% uses one, and its value, 5.03%, lies between the worst and the third-worst loss.

  • A position has 100 daily returns; the five worst are \(-6.1\), \(-4.8\), \(-4.2\), \(-3.9\), \(-3.5\) (in %)

  • VaR 1%: \(k = \lceil 100 \times 0.01 \rceil = 1\), so \(\mathrm{VaR}_{1\%} = 6.1\%\)
    • one observation decides VaR 1%: with 100 days the estimate is very imprecise
  • ES 2.5%: \(k = \lceil 2.5 \rceil = 3\); the average of the three worst losses
    • \(\mathrm{ES}_{2.5\%} = (6.1 + 4.8 + 4.2)/3 = 5.03\%\); \(\mathrm{VaR}_{2.5\%} = 4.2\%\)
  • A position of 1 million RON: VaR 1% = 61 000 RON; on one day in a hundred the loss is larger

                        The Normal Distribution: Closed Forms

                        Slide 13 in the PDF

                        If \(X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)\), both measures have closed forms. With \(z_\alpha = \Phi^{-1}(\alpha)\) the standard Normal quantile, where \(\Phi\) and \(\varphi\) are the distribution function and the density of \(N(0, 1)\),

                        \[\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha = -(\mu + \sigma z_\alpha), \qquad \mathrm{ES}_\alpha = -\mu + \sigma\,\frac{\varphi(z_\alpha)}{\alpha}.\]
                        (10.3)

                        The ES formula rests on the tail mean of the standard Normal, \(E[Z \mid Z \le z] = -\varphi(z)/\Phi(z)\). The derivation uses \(\varphi'(z) = -z\varphi(z)\): integrating \(t\varphi(t)\) from \(-\infty\) to \(z\) gives \(-\varphi(z)\), and dividing by \(P(Z \le z) = \Phi(z) = \alpha\) gives the conditional mean.

                        The constants are worth knowing: \(z_{0.01} = -2.326\), \(z_{0.025} = -1.960\), \(\varphi(z_{0.025}) = 0.0584\), and the ES factor at 2.5% is \(\varphi(z_{0.025})/0.025 = 2.338\). With \(\mu = 0\), the ratio of ES 2.5% to VaR 1% is \(2.338/2.326 = 1.005\): for Normal returns the two measures are practically equal. This is why the FRTB chose ES 2.5% to replace VaR 1%: for Normal data the capital charge stays about the same, and for heavy-tailed data, where the ES 2.5% exceeds the VaR 1%, it rises.

                        In brief

                        • \(X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)\), \(z_\alpha = \Phi^{-1}(\alpha)\) (\(\Phi\), \(\varphi\): distribution function and density of \(N(0,1)\))
                          • \(\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha = -(\mu + \sigma z_\alpha)\),   \(\mathrm{ES}_\alpha = -\mu + \sigma\,\dfrac{\varphi(z_\alpha)}{\alpha}\)
                          • derivation: \(E[Z \mid Z \le z] = -\varphi(z)/\Phi(z)\) because \(\varphi'(z) = -z\varphi(z)\)
                        • Numbers: \(z_{0.01} = -2.326\); \(z_{0.025} = -1.960\), \(\varphi(z_{0.025}) = 0.0584\), \(\varphi(z_{0.025})/0.025 = 2.338\)
                          • ES 2.5% / VaR 1% \(= 2.338/2.326 = 1.005\) (with \(\mu = 0\)): for Normal returns the two measures are almost equal
                          • this is why ES 2.5% replaced VaR 1% in Basel: about the same size for Normal data, larger for heavy tails

                        Worked Example: Normal VaR and ES of the S&P 500

                        Slide 14 in the PDF

                        The sample moments of the daily S&P 500 returns since 2000 are \(\hat\mu = 0.025\%\) and \(\hat\sigma = 1.21\%\). Inserted into (10.3), they give the Normal VaR 1% \(= -(0.025 - 2.326 \times 1.21) = -0.025 + 2.822 = 2.80\%\) and the Normal ES 2.5% \(= -0.025 + 2.338 \times 1.21 = -0.025 + 2.836 = 2.81\%\).

                        The data tell a different story. Historical simulation gives VaR 1% = 3.46% and ES 2.5% = 3.76%. The Normal VaR 1% is 19% too small, because the Normal tail is too thin (Chapter 2). The Normal ES 2.5% is even further from the data, about a quarter below the historical value, because the Normal tail has nothing like the extreme days that the historical average includes.

                        Two parameters, a mean and a standard deviation, are fitted to the centre of the distribution, where most of the observations are; they cannot also describe the tail. The methods of the following sections take one of two routes: they change the distribution (Student-t, Cornish–Fisher, EVT) or they change the data used (historical simulation, volatility filtering).

                        In brief

                        • Sample moments of the daily returns since 2000: \(\hat\mu = 0.025\), \(\hat\sigma = 1.21\) (in %)

                        The Student-t Distribution: Closed Forms

                        Slide 15 in the PDF

                        The Student-t distribution of Chapter 2 has a power-law tail. Write \(X = m + s\,T\) with \(T \sim t_\nu\), a location \(m\), a scale \(s\) and \(\nu\) degrees of freedom; let \(t_\alpha = t_\nu^{-1}(\alpha)\) be the quantile and \(f_\nu\) the density of \(t_\nu\). Then

                        \[\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha = -(m + s\,t_\alpha), \qquad \mathrm{ES}_\alpha = -m + s\,\frac{f_\nu(t_\alpha)}{\alpha}\,\frac{\nu + t_\alpha^2}{\nu - 1}, \quad \nu > 1.\]
                        (10.4)

                        The condition \(\nu > 1\) is needed for the mean, and hence the ES, to exist. The scale \(s\) is not the standard deviation: the variance of \(X\) is \(s^2\nu/(\nu - 2)\), so a standardised t with variance 1 has \(s = \sqrt{(\nu - 2)/\nu}\). This standardisation reappears in the GARCH-t model of the conditional VaR section.

                        Fitted to the S&P 500 by maximum likelihood, the distribution has location \(\hat m = 0.068\), scale \(\hat s = 0.703\) and \(\hat\nu = 2.67\) degrees of freedom. The quantile is \(t_{0.01} = -5.023\), so the VaR 1% \(= -(0.068 + 0.703 \times (-5.023)) = 3.46\%\), exactly the historical value. The ES factor is 5.720, so the ES 2.5% \(= -0.068 + 0.703 \times 5.720 = 3.95\%\), above the historical 3.76%. With \(\nu < 3\) the fitted tail is very heavy: it matches the 1% quantile, but beyond it the t distribution places more mass than the data show.

                        In brief

                        • \(X = m + s\,T\), \(T \sim t_\nu\) (location \(m\), scale \(s\), \(\nu\) degrees of freedom, Chapter 2); \(t_\alpha = t_\nu^{-1}(\alpha)\), \(f_\nu\) the density of \(t_\nu\)
                          • \(\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha = -(m + s\,t_\alpha)\),   \(\mathrm{ES}_\alpha = -m + s\,\dfrac{f_\nu(t_\alpha)}{\alpha}\,\dfrac{\nu + t_\alpha^2}{\nu - 1}\) (for \(\nu > 1\))
                          • the variance is \(s^2\nu/(\nu - 2)\): a standardised t (variance 1) has \(s = \sqrt{(\nu - 2)/\nu}\)
                        • Worked example: S&P 500, maximum likelihood: \(\hat m = 0.068\), \(\hat s = 0.703\), \(\hat\nu = 2.67\)
                          • \(t_{0.01} = -5.023\): VaR 1% \(= -(0.068 + 0.703 \times (-5.023)) = 3.46\%\)
                          • ES factor \(5.720\): ES 2.5% \(= -0.068 + 0.703 \times 5.720 = 3.95\%\)
                        • Interpretation: with \(\nu < 3\) the fitted tail is very heavy: VaR 1% matches the data, ES 2.5% is above the historical \(3.76\%\)

                        From One Day to Ten Days

                        Slide 16 in the PDF

                        Regulatory VaR has a 10-day horizon, while data and models are daily. The square-root-of-time rule bridges the gap: \(\mathrm{VaR}^{(h)} \approx \sqrt{h}\,\mathrm{VaR}^{(1)}\). The rule is exact for i.i.d. Normal returns with mean zero, because the \(h\)-day return is then Normal with standard deviation \(\sigma\sqrt{h}\) and every quantile scales by \(\sqrt{h}\). Basel 1996 asked for a 10-day VaR, usually obtained from the one-day VaR by the factor \(\sqrt{10} = 3.162\).

                        For the S&P 500, the historical one-day VaR 1% is 3.46%, and the rule gives \(3.162 \times 3.46 = 10.93\%\) over 10 days. The historical VaR 1% of overlapping 10-day returns is 10.37%, somewhat smaller: the ratio is 3.00, below \(\sqrt{10}\). The difference is not an accident of the sample but the result of three features of real returns.

                        The first is heavy tails. The sum of 10 heavy-tailed returns is closer to Normal than each of them (the central limit theorem, CLT), so its kurtosis is lower and the ratio of its 1% quantile to the daily one is below \(\sqrt{10}\). The second is volatility clustering (Chapter 9): after a quiet day volatility tends to rise back to its long-run level, so the 10-day risk is more than \(\sqrt{10}\) times today's risk; after a turbulent period it tends to fall, and the 10-day risk is less. The third is autocorrelation. The variance of a 10-day sum is \(\sigma^2\bigl(10 + 2\sum_{k=1}^{9}(10-k)\rho_k\bigr)\), so a negative first-order autocorrelation lowers it and a positive one raises it. The S&P 500 has \(\hat\rho_1 = -0.10\): consecutive days partly offset each other, which adds to the effect of the heavy tails. The BET has \(\hat\rho_1 = +0.11\) (the non-synchronous trading of its constituents), and its ratio is 3.86, above \(\sqrt{10}\). The rule is a convention of regulation, not a property of the data.

                        In brief

                        • Square-root-of-time rule: \(\mathrm{VaR}^{(h)} \approx \sqrt{h}\,\mathrm{VaR}^{(1)}\)
                          • exact for i.i.d. Normal returns with mean 0: the \(h\)-day return has standard deviation \(\sigma\sqrt{h}\)
                          • Basel 1996: 10-day VaR, usually scaled from one day with \(\sqrt{10} = 3.162\)
                        • S&P 500, historical simulation
                          • one day: 3.46%; scaled: \(3.162 \times 3.46 = 10.93\%\)
                          • VaR 1% of overlapping 10-day returns: 10.37%
                        • Why the rule fails in general
                          • heavy tails: a sum of 10 heavy-tailed returns is closer to Normal (CLT, central limit theorem), so the ratio of quantiles is below \(\sqrt{10}\)
                          • autocorrelation: \(\mathrm{Var}(r_{t+1}+\dots+r_{t+10}) = \sigma^2\bigl(10 + 2\sum_{k=1}^{9}(10-k)\rho_k\bigr)\); S&P 500: \(\hat\rho_1 = -0.10\), ratio \(3.00\); BET: \(\hat\rho_1 = +0.11\), ratio \(3.86 > \sqrt{10}\)
                          • volatility clustering: after a calm day the 10-day risk is larger than \(\sqrt{10}\) times today's, after a storm smaller (Chapter 9)

                        Section summary

                        VaR (10.1) is a quantile of the loss and ES (10.2) the average loss beyond it. On the S&P 500 since 2000 the historical values are VaR 1% = 3.46% and ES 2.5% = 3.76%; the Normal distribution gives 2.80% and 2.81%, while a Student-t matches the VaR and overshoots the ES. The next section asks which properties a good risk measure should have and finds that VaR lacks one of them.

                        Coherent Risk Measures

                        The section judges VaR and ES by two theoretical criteria. The first, coherence, asks whether a risk measure behaves sensibly when positions are scaled or combined; the four axioms of Artzner, Delbaen, Eber and Heath define it, and a counterexample with two bonds shows that VaR can violate it. The second, elicitability, asks whether forecasts of the measure can be compared with outcomes, and here the ranking of the two measures reverses.

                        1999: Four Axioms for a Risk Measure

                        Slide 17 in the PDF

                        Artzner et al. (1999) define a risk measure \(\rho\) as a map from a loss \(L\) to the amount of capital that makes the position acceptable to a regulator. They call \(\rho\) coherent if it satisfies four axioms. Monotonicity: if \(L_1 \le L_2\) in every state of the world, then \(\rho(L_1) \le \rho(L_2)\). Translation invariance: \(\rho(L + c) = \rho(L) + c\) for a certain amount \(c\), so adding a sure loss raises the capital by exactly that amount. Positive homogeneity: \(\rho(\lambda L) = \lambda\rho(L)\) for \(\lambda > 0\), so doubling a position doubles its capital. Subadditivity: \(\rho(L_1 + L_2) \le \rho(L_1) + \rho(L_2)\).

                        The first three axioms are almost uncontroversial. Subadditivity carries the economic content: merging two positions never creates risk, so diversification never adds risk. It has a practical consequence for a bank: the sum of the capital computed separately for each division is an upper bound for the capital of the whole bank, and the capital can be allocated division by division. A measure that is not subadditive could make a merged bank look riskier than its two halves, and it would reward splitting a portfolio artificially to lower the capital.

                        The photograph shows Freddy Delbaen, one of the four authors, in 1997. The axioms were published in 1999 in Mathematical Finance and changed the question about risk measures from "which number is intuitive?" to "which properties must the number have?".

                        In brief

                        • Artzner et al. (1999): a risk measure \(\rho\) maps a loss \(L\) to the capital that makes it acceptable; \(\rho\) is coherent if
                          • monotonicity: \(L_1 \le L_2\) always \(\Rightarrow \rho(L_1) \le \rho(L_2)\)
                          • translation invariance: \(\rho(L + c) = \rho(L) + c\) for a sure amount \(c\)
                          • positive homogeneity: \(\rho(\lambda L) = \lambda\rho(L)\), \(\lambda > 0\)
                          • subadditivity: \(\rho(L_1 + L_2) \le \rho(L_1) + \rho(L_2)\)
                        • Subadditivity: diversification never adds risk; a bank can add the capital of its desks as an upper bound

                        Counterexample: Two Bonds, Step by Step

                        Slide 18 in the PDF

                        Two independent bonds each lose 100 if the issuer defaults, which happens with probability 4%, and 0 otherwise; the level is \(\alpha = 5\%\). For one bond, \(P(L > 0) = 4\% \le 5\%\), so the 5% quantile of the loss is 0 and \(\mathrm{VaR}_{5\%} = 0\) for each bond. A risk manager using VaR would see no risk in either bond.

                        For the portfolio of both bonds, the loss is 200 if both default, with probability \(0.04^2 = 0.16\%\), and 100 if exactly one defaults, with probability \(2 \times 0.04 \times 0.96 = 7.68\%\). Now \(P(L > 0) = 7.84\% > 5\%\), so the VaR of the portfolio is 100. Since \(\mathrm{VaR}_{5\%}(L_1 + L_2) = 100 > 0 + 0\), VaR penalises diversification: holding both bonds appears riskier than the sum of holding each.

                        ES 5% is the average of the worst 5% of the outcomes. For one bond, the worst 5% consist of the 4% of default states with loss 100 and 1% of the states with loss 0, so \(\mathrm{ES}_{5\%} = (4\% \times 100 + 1\% \times 0)/5\% = 80\). For the portfolio, the worst 5% consist of the 0.16% with loss 200 and 4.84% with loss 100, so \(\mathrm{ES}_{5\%} = (0.16\% \times 200 + 4.84\% \times 100)/5\% = 103.2\). Since \(103.2 \le 80 + 80\), ES is subadditive here, and it sees the default risk of each bond, which VaR ignores entirely.

                        In brief

                        • Two independent bonds; each loses 100 with probability 4% (default) and 0 otherwise; level \(\alpha = 5\%\)
                          • one bond: \(P(L > 0) = 4\% \le 5\%\), so \(\mathrm{VaR}_{5\%} = 0\) for each
                        • Both bonds: \(P(L = 200) = 0.16\%\), \(P(L = 100) = 7.68\%\), so \(P(L > 0) = 7.84\% > 5\%\)
                          • \(\mathrm{VaR}_{5\%}(L_1 + L_2) = 100 > 0 + 0\): VaR punishes diversification
                        • ES 5% (the average of the worst 5% of outcomes)
                          • one bond: \((4\% \times 100 + 1\% \times 0)/5\% = 80\)
                          • both: \((0.16\% \times 200 + (5 - 0.16)\% \times 100)/5\% = 103.2 \le 80 + 80\): subadditive

                        ES Is Coherent; VaR Only Sometimes

                        Slide 19 in the PDF

                        ES satisfies all four axioms (Acerbi & Tasche, 2002). The reason is visible in (10.2): ES is an average of quantiles over the tail, and averages respect sums. More precisely, ES can be written as the largest expected loss over a set of reweighted scenarios, and the maximum of a sum is never larger than the sum of the maxima.

                        VaR satisfies the first three axioms; subadditivity can fail. It fails for discrete losses, such as credit losses and the bonds of the previous example, and for extremely heavy tails, with a tail index below 1 (Chapter 5), where the mean loss is infinite. It holds for elliptical distributions such as the Normal and the Student-t, and Daníelsson et al. (2013) show that in practice it holds for most equity returns, whose tail index lies well above 1.

                        ES pays for coherence with a stronger requirement: it needs a finite mean loss, while VaR exists for every distribution. For daily equity returns, whose tail index is around 3, this is not a constraint; for some operational or insurance losses it can be.

                        In brief

                        • ES satisfies all four axioms Acerbi & Tasche (2002)
                          • reason: ES is an average of quantiles over the tail, and averaging respects sums
                        • VaR satisfies the first three; subadditivity can fail
                          • fails for discrete losses (credit, the bonds above) and for extremely heavy tails (tail index below 1, Chapter 5)
                          • holds for elliptical distributions (Normal, Student-t) and, in practice, for most equity returns Daníelsson et al. (2013)
                        • ES needs a finite mean of the losses; VaR always exists

                        Elicitability: Can a Forecast Be Scored?

                        Slide 20 in the PDF

                        A statistic is elicitable if some loss function, or scoring function, is minimised in expectation by its true value (Gneiting, 2011). The mean is elicitable through the squared error, the median through the absolute error. Elicitability matters because it allows competing forecasts to be ranked: the forecaster with the smaller average score is the better one, and no forecaster can lower the expected score by reporting anything other than the true value.

                        VaR is elicitable. The scoring function is the quantile, or pinball, loss

                        \[S(v, x) = (\alpha - \mathbf{1}\{x < -v\})(x + v),\]
                        (10.5)

                        where \(v\) is the VaR forecast, \(x\) the realised return and \(\mathbf{1}\{\cdot\}\) equals 1 if the event occurs and 0 otherwise. Its expectation is minimised where \(P(X < -v) = \alpha\), that is at \(v = \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha\). Two VaR models can therefore be compared by the average quantile loss of their forecasts, as the Backtesting section does.

                        ES alone is not elicitable (Gneiting, 2011): no scoring function is minimised in expectation by the true ES. The pair (VaR, ES), however, is jointly elicitable (Fissler & Ziegel, 2016). The consequence is practical: forecasts of ES are tested together with forecasts of VaR, as in the ES backtest at the end of the Backtesting section. Coherence favours ES, elicitability favours VaR, and the Basel rules use both: ES for capital, VaR exceptions for the backtest.

                        In brief

                        • A statistic is elicitable if some loss (scoring) function is minimised, on average, by its true value Gneiting (2011)
                          • the mean: squared error; the median: absolute error
                        • VaR is elicitable: the quantile (pinball) loss \(S(v, x) = (\alpha - \mathbf{1}\{x < -v\})(x + v)\)
                          • \(v\): the VaR forecast; \(x\): the realised return; \(\mathbf{1}\{\cdot\}\): 1 if the event occurs, 0 otherwise
                          • two VaR models can be compared by their average quantile loss
                        • ES alone is not elicitable Gneiting (2011); the pair (VaR, ES) is Fissler & Ziegel (2016)
                          • consequence: ES forecasts are tested together with VaR forecasts (Section 7)

                        Section summary

                        ES is coherent; VaR is not always subadditive and can penalise diversification, as the two-bond example shows with a portfolio VaR of 100 against individual VaRs of 0. VaR is elicitable through the pinball loss (10.5), while ES is elicitable only together with VaR. The next section turns from properties to estimation.

                        Estimating VaR and ES

                        Slide 21 in the PDF

                        Historical Simulation

                        Slide 22 in the PDF

                        Historical simulation is the simplest estimator. Take the last \(n\) returns \(r_{t-n+1}, \dots, r_t\) and sort them, \(r_{(1)} \le \dots \le r_{(n)}\). Then

                        \[\widehat{\mathrm{VaR}}_\alpha = -r_{(k)}, \quad k = \lceil n\alpha \rceil, \qquad \widehat{\mathrm{ES}}_\alpha = -\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k} r_{(i)}.\]
                        (10.6)

                        No distribution is assumed: the empirical distribution of the window is the forecast. For the S&P 500 since 2000, \(n = 6{,}714\). The VaR 1% uses \(k = \lceil 67.14 \rceil = 68\), and the 68th worst day is a loss of 3.46%. The ES 2.5% is the average of the worst \(\lceil 167.85 \rceil = 168\) days, 3.76%.

                        The window length \(n\) is a trade-off. A short window of 250 days reacts to recent events, but its VaR 1% is the third worst return, \(k = \lceil 2.5 \rceil = 3\), and rests on three observations. A long window, here all the data, gives a stable estimate that does not react to today's volatility at all.

                        A specific artefact of a fixed window is the ghost effect. A crash day changes the VaR when it enters the window, which is reasonable, and changes it again, abruptly, \(n\) days later when it leaves the window, although nothing happened on that day. With a 500-day window, a crash stays in the estimate for about two years, as the rolling forecasts of the conditional VaR section show.

                        • Algorithm: take the last \(n\) returns \(r_{t-n+1}, \dots, r_t\), sort them: \(r_{(1)} \le \dots \le r_{(n)}\)

                          • no distribution is assumed: the empirical distribution of the window is the forecast

                        How Precise Is a Historical VaR?

                        Slide 23 in the PDF

                        A VaR estimate is a statistic and has a sampling error. The bootstrap of Chapter 2 measures it: resample the \(n\) returns with replacement 2000 times, recompute VaR and ES on each resample, and read the 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles of the 2000 values as a 95% interval.

                        With all 6,714 S&P 500 days, the VaR 1% of 3.46% has the 95% interval [3.18%, 3.66%], and the ES 2.5% of 3.76% has [3.46%, 4.07%]. With the last 500 days only, the VaR 1% is 2.75% with the interval [1.99%, 4.96%], and the ES 2.5% is 2.94% with [2.14%, 3.82%]. With two years of data, the interval for the VaR 1% is almost three percentage points wide, because the estimate rests on the five worst days, \(k = \lceil 500 \times 0.01 \rceil = 5\). The interval of the ES is narrower, since an average of 13 observations varies less than a single order statistic.

                        The intervals set a scale for every comparison that follows. A difference between two methods that is smaller than the bootstrap interval does not show that one method is better; it may be sampling noise. The resampling treats the days as independent, so with volatility clustering the true uncertainty is, if anything, larger.

                        The Cornish–Fisher Expansion

                        Slide 24 in the PDF

                        Cornish & Fisher (1938) correct the Normal quantile \(z = z_\alpha\) with the skewness \(S\) and the excess kurtosis \(K\), the kurtosis coefficient minus 3:

                        \[z_{CF} = z + \frac{(z^2 - 1)S}{6} + \frac{(z^3 - 3z)K}{24} - \frac{(2z^3 - 5z)S^2}{36}, \qquad \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha = -(\mu + \sigma z_{CF}).\]
                        (10.7)

                        The expansion is a series in the higher cumulants around the Normal quantile. At \(z_{0.01} = -2.326\), a negative skewness \(S\) and a positive excess kurtosis \(K\) both push the quantile further into the left tail, so the VaR rises above the Normal value.

                        For the S&P 500, \(S = -0.35\) and \(K = 10.7\). The kurtosis term alone is \((z^3 - 3z)K/24 \approx -2.50\), larger than the Normal quantile itself, and the corrected quantile is \(z_{CF} = -5.03\), which gives a VaR 1% of 6.08%, against the historical 3.46%. The correction has overshot by more than the Normal method undershot.

                        The expansion is a small-correction device: it is accurate when the distribution is close to Normal and the correction terms are small compared with \(z\). With an excess kurtosis above 10, the terms are no longer small and the expansion lies far above the correct value. The method is useful only when \(|S|\) and \(K\) are moderate, for example for monthly returns or for diversified portfolios.

                        In brief

                        • Cornish & Fisher (1938): correct the Normal quantile \(z = z_\alpha\) with the skewness \(S\) and the excess kurtosis \(K\) (kurtosis minus 3)
                          • \(z_{CF} = z + \dfrac{(z^2 - 1)S}{6} + \dfrac{(z^3 - 3z)K}{24} - \dfrac{(2z^3 - 5z)S^2}{36}\),   \(\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha = -(\mu + \sigma z_{CF})\)
                          • negative \(S\) and positive \(K\) push the quantile further into the left tail
                        • S&P 500: \(S = -0.35\), \(K = 10.7\), \(z_{0.01} = -2.326\)
                          • \(z_{CF} = -5.03\), so VaR 1% \(= 6.08\%\), against the historical \(3.46\%\)
                        • Interpretation: the expansion is a small correction around the Normal; with an excess kurtosis above 10 it overshoots badly
                          • use it only when \(|S|\) and \(K\) are moderate (for example monthly returns or diversified portfolios)

                        EVT: Peaks over Threshold (Chapter 5)

                        Slide 25 in the PDF

                        The peaks-over-threshold (POT) method of Chapter 5 models only the tail. With the losses \(L = -r\) and a threshold \(u\) set at the 90% quantile of the losses, as in McNeil & Frey (2000), the excesses \(L - u\) above \(u\) follow approximately a GPD with shape \(\xi\) and scale \(\beta\). The tail estimator then gives

                        \[\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha = u + \frac{\beta}{\xi}\Big[\Big(\frac{n\alpha}{N_u}\Big)^{-\xi} - 1\Big], \qquad \mathrm{ES}_\alpha = \frac{\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha + \beta - \xi u}{1 - \xi},\]
                        (10.8)

                        where \(n\) is the number of days and \(N_u\) the number of excesses. The ratio \(n\alpha/N_u\) compares the target tail probability with the share of days above the threshold; a shape \(\xi > 0\) means a heavy, Pareto-type tail.

                        For the S&P 500, \(u = 1.26\%\), \(N_u = 672\) excesses (10.0% of the days), \(\hat\xi = 0.164\) and \(\hat\beta = 0.820\). For VaR 1%, \(n\alpha/N_u = 0.0999\) and \(0.0999^{-\hat\xi} = 1.461\); with \(\hat\beta/\hat\xi = 4.988\), the VaR 1% is \(1.26 + 4.988 \times (1.461 - 1) = 3.56\%\). The ES 2.5% is 3.77%. Both are close to the historical values, 3.46% and 3.76%.

                        At 1% and 2.5% the EVT and historical estimates agree because both are driven by the same few hundred tail observations. EVT adds value further out: it smooths the tail with a two-parameter model and extrapolates beyond the worst observed day, which makes it the method for VaR 0.1% and for stress tests.

                        In brief

                        • Losses \(L = -r\); threshold \(u\) = the 90% quantile of the losses McNeil & Frey (2000); excesses \(L - u\) above \(u\) follow a GPD (generalised Pareto distribution) with shape \(\xi\) and scale \(\beta\)
                          • \(\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha = u + \dfrac{\beta}{\xi}\Big[\Big(\dfrac{n\alpha}{N_u}\Big)^{-\xi} - 1\Big]\),   \(\mathrm{ES}_\alpha = \dfrac{\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha + \beta - \xi u}{1 - \xi}\) (\(N_u\): number of excesses)

                                    Conditional VaR: GARCH and Filtered Historical Simulation

                                    The section combines the volatility forecasts of Chapter 9 with the tail models of the previous section. A VaR forecast becomes the product of tomorrow's volatility and a quantile of the standardised innovation. The section computes such forecasts for six series, evaluates rolling forecasts from 2005 to 2026, and compares conditional and unconditional methods in the crises of 2008 and 2020.

                                    Why Condition on Today's Volatility?

                                    Slide 29 in the PDF

                                    Volatility clusters (Chapters 8 and 9): calm days follow calm days and turbulent days follow turbulent days. An unconditional VaR averages over both regimes, so it is too high in quiet years and too low at the start of a crisis, exactly when it matters most.

                                    The conditional model writes tomorrow's return as \(r_{t+1} = \mu + \sigma_{t+1}z_{t+1}\), where \(\sigma_{t+1}\) is the volatility forecast made today and \(z\) is an i.i.d. innovation with mean 0 and variance 1. Because the only time-varying part is the scale, the risk measures follow from those of \(z\):

                                    \[\mathrm{VaR}_{t+1} = -(\mu + \sigma_{t+1}\,q_\alpha(z)), \qquad \mathrm{ES}_{t+1} = -\mu + \sigma_{t+1}\,\mathrm{ES}_\alpha(z),\]
                                    (10.9)

                                    where \(q_\alpha(z)\) and \(\mathrm{ES}_\alpha(z)\) are the quantile and the ES of the standardised innovation. The model has two ingredients: a forecast of \(\sigma_{t+1}\) and a distribution for \(z\).

                                    The chapter uses a GARCH(1,1) model (Bollerslev, 1986) for the volatility and two variants for \(z\). GARCH-t assumes a standardised Student-t, with \(\nu\) estimated jointly with the GARCH parameters. Filtered historical simulation (FHS), proposed by Hull & White (1998) and by Barone-Adesi, Giannopoulos & Vosper (1999), takes the empirical distribution of the standardised residuals \(\hat z_t = (r_t - \hat\mu)/\hat\sigma_t\): the volatility comes from the model, the shape of the tail from the data.

                                    In brief

                                    • Volatility clustering (Chapters 8 and 9): a calm day is followed by calm days, a storm by storms
                                      • an unconditional VaR is too high in calm years and too low at the start of a crisis
                                    • Conditional model: \(r_{t+1} = \mu + \sigma_{t+1}z_{t+1}\), \(z\) i.i.d. with mean 0 and variance 1
                                      • \(\mathrm{VaR}_{t+1} = -(\mu + \sigma_{t+1}\,q_\alpha(z))\),   \(\mathrm{ES}_{t+1} = -\mu + \sigma_{t+1}\,\mathrm{ES}_\alpha(z)\)
                                      • \(q_\alpha(z)\), \(\mathrm{ES}_\alpha(z)\): the quantile and the ES of the standardised innovation \(z\)
                                      • two ingredients: the volatility forecast \(\sigma_{t+1}\) and the distribution of \(z\)
                                    • Two choices for the distribution of \(z\)
                                      • GARCH-t: standardised Student-t with \(\nu\) estimated with the GARCH(1,1) Bollerslev (1986)
                                      • FHS (filtered historical simulation): the empirical distribution of the standardised residuals \(\hat z_t = (r_t - \hat\mu)/\hat\sigma_t\) Hull & White (1998); Barone-Adesi, Giannopoulos & Vosper (1999)

                                            Portfolio VaR and Dependence

                                            The Variance–Covariance Method

                                            Slide 37 in the PDF

                                            With the weight vector \(w\), the vector of mean returns \(\mu\) and the covariance matrix \(\Sigma\), the portfolio has mean \(\mu_p = w^\top\mu\) and variance

                                            \[\sigma_p^2 = w^\top\Sigma w = w_1^2\sigma_1^2 + w_2^2\sigma_2^2 + 2w_1w_2\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2,\]
                                            (10.10)

                                            where \(\rho\) is the correlation of the two assets. If the returns are jointly Normal, the portfolio return is Normal too and \(\mathrm{VaR}_p = -(\mu_p + z_\alpha\sigma_p)\); this is the RiskMetrics method (J.P. Morgan/Reuters, 1996), which scales to hundreds of assets because it needs only a covariance matrix.

                                            Step by step: \(w_1\sigma_1 = 0.712\), \(w_2\sigma_2 = 0.614\) and the covariance term is \(2 \times 0.5 \times 0.5 \times 0.369 = 0.185\), where \(0.369 = \rho\sigma_1\sigma_2\); hence \(\sigma_p^2 = 0.712^2 + 0.614^2 + 0.185 = 1.068\) and \(\sigma_p = 1.033\). With \(\mu_p = 0.055\), the VaR 1% is \(-(0.055 - 2.326 \times 1.033) = 2.35\%\). The weighted sum of the two Normal VaRs is \(0.5 \times 3.23 + 0.5 \times 2.82 = 3.03\%\), so the diversification benefit is 22% of the risk. With \(\rho = 1\) it would be zero: the volatilities would simply add.

                                            The Euler contributions \(w_i(\Sigma w)_i/\sigma_p\), where \((\Sigma w)_i\) is the \(i\)-th component of the vector \(\Sigma w\), add up exactly to \(\sigma_p\), so they split the portfolio risk among the assets. The BET contributes 56% and the S&P 500 44%: the BET, with the higher volatility, carries more than half of the risk despite its equal weight.

                                            • Portfolio: \(\mu_p = w^\top\mu\), \(\sigma_p^2 = w^\top\Sigma w = w_1^2\sigma_1^2 + w_2^2\sigma_2^2 + 2w_1w_2\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2\) (\(\Sigma\): covariance matrix)

                                                    Correlation Is Not Tail Dependence

                                                    Slide 39 in the PDF

                                                    Pearson's correlation measures linear co-movement over all days, and ordinary days, which are the vast majority, dominate it (Embrechts, McNeil & Straumann, 2002). It also depends on the marginal distributions and can change under monotone transformations of the returns. Rank correlations avoid this by using only the order of the returns: for the BET and the S&P 500, Kendall's \(\hat\tau = 0.100\) and Spearman's rank correlation is 0.15.

                                                    Tail dependence asks directly about joint extremes. With \(U_i = F_i(X_i)\) the returns transformed to a uniform scale by their distribution functions \(F_i\), the lower tail dependence coefficient is

                                                    \[\lambda_L = \lim_{q \to 0} P(U_1 \le q \mid U_2 \le q),\]
                                                    (10.11)

                                                    where \(q\) is a small probability and \(\lambda_L \in [0, 1]\). In words: the probability that the BET has one of its worst days, given that the S&P 500 has one of its worst days. Under independence the conditional probability equals \(q\) and tends to zero.

                                                    In the data, the conditional probability is 0.22 at \(q = 5\%\) and 0.19 at \(q = 1\%\), far above the values 0.05 and 0.01 that independence would give. A joint crash, both indices below their own 1% quantile on the same day, occurs on 0.19% of the days, nineteen times the 0.01% expected under independence. A correlation of 0.21 does not reveal this.

                                                    In brief

                                                    • Pearson correlation measures linear co-movement over all days; it is dominated by ordinary days Embrechts, McNeil & Straumann (2002)
                                                      • rank correlations (Spearman, Kendall's \(\tau\)) use only the order of the returns: Kendall \(\hat\tau = 0.100\), Spearman \(0.15\)
                                                    • Lower tail dependence: \(\lambda_L = \lim_{q \to 0} P(U_1 \le q \mid U_2 \le q)\), \(U_i = F_i(X_i)\) (the returns on a uniform scale)
                                                      • \(q\): a small probability; \(F_i\): the distribution function of asset \(i\); \(\lambda_L \in [0, 1]\)
                                                      • the chance that BET has one of its worst days given that the S&P 500 has one of its worst days
                                                      • data: \(q = 5\%\): \(0.22\); \(q = 1\%\): \(0.19\); under independence both would equal \(q\)
                                                    • Joint crash (both below their own 1% quantile): 0.19% of the days, against 0.01% under independence

                                                    Copulas: a Light Introduction

                                                    Slide 40 in the PDF

                                                    Sklar's theorem states that every joint distribution function can be written as

                                                    \[F(x_1, x_2) = C(F_1(x_1), F_2(x_2)),\]
                                                    (10.12)

                                                    where \(F_1\) and \(F_2\) are the marginal distributions, each asset on its own, and \(C\) is a copula, a joint distribution of uniform variables on \([0,1]^2\) (McNeil, Frey & Embrechts, 2015). For continuous marginals the copula is unique. The theorem separates the two parts of a multivariate model: the copula contains all of the dependence, and the marginals contain all of the individual tails.

                                                    The separation gives a practical recipe. The marginals can be empirical or fitted (Student-t, EVT), whichever describes each asset best. The dependence is estimated on the pseudo-observations \(\hat U_{i,t} = \mathrm{rank}(X_{i,t})/(n + 1)\), the ranks rescaled to the open interval \((0, 1)\), so that the copula estimate does not depend on the marginal model.

                                                    To compute a portfolio VaR, simulate pairs \((U_1, U_2)\) from the copula, map them back to returns with the quantile functions \(F_i^{-1}\), compute the portfolio return and read VaR and ES from the simulated distribution. The textbook treatment is Franke, Härdle & Hafner (2019), chapter 17.

                                                    In brief

                                                    • Sklar's theorem: every joint distribution function can be written as \(F(x_1, x_2) = C(F_1(x_1), F_2(x_2))\) McNeil, Frey & Embrechts (2015)
                                                      • \(F_1\), \(F_2\): the marginal distributions (each asset alone); \(C\): the copula, a joint distribution of uniforms on \([0,1]^2\)
                                                      • the copula holds all the dependence, the margins all the individual tails
                                                    • Practical recipe
                                                      • margins: empirical or fitted (Student-t, EVT); dependence: a copula fitted to the pseudo-observations \(\hat U_{i,t} = \mathrm{rank}(X_{i,t})/(n + 1)\)
                                                      • simulate \((U_1, U_2)\) from the copula, map back with \(F_i^{-1}\), compute the portfolio return, read VaR and ES
                                                    • Textbook: Franke, Härdle & Hafner (2019), Ch. 17

                                                    Gaussian and t Copulas

                                                    Slide 41 in the PDF

                                                    The Gaussian copula is the dependence structure of a bivariate Normal distribution with correlation \(\rho\). Its tail dependence is \(\lambda_L = 0\) for every \(\rho < 1\): in the limit, extreme days become independent, and joint crashes are rare. This is the copula implicit in the variance–covariance method.

                                                    The t copula is the dependence structure of a bivariate Student-t with correlation \(\rho\) and \(\nu\) degrees of freedom (Demarta & McNeil, 2005). Its tail dependence is positive:

                                                    \[\lambda_L = 2\,t_{\nu+1}\Big(-\sqrt{(\nu + 1)(1 - \rho)/(1 + \rho)}\Big),\]
                                                    (10.13)

                                                    where \(t_{\nu+1}(\cdot)\) is the Student-t distribution function with \(\nu + 1\) degrees of freedom. A smaller \(\nu\) or a larger \(\rho\) gives a larger \(\lambda_L\): crashes occur together across assets even when the overall correlation is modest.

                                                    For the BET and the S&P 500, the correlation parameter of both copulas is estimated from Kendall's tau through \(\rho = \sin(\pi\hat\tau/2) = 0.157\), a relation valid for all elliptical copulas, and the t copula has \(\hat\nu = 4.50\) by maximum likelihood. The log-likelihood is 92.1 for the Gaussian copula and 219.5 for the t copula; the likelihood-ratio statistic, LR = 254.9, is far above the 5% critical value of \(\chi^2(1)\), 3.84, so the t copula fits clearly better. Its implied tail dependence by (10.13) is \(\lambda_L = 0.096\), against 0 for the Gaussian copula.

                                                    In brief

                                                    • Gaussian copula: the dependence of a bivariate Normal with correlation \(\rho\); \(\lambda_L = 0\) for \(\rho < 1\)
                                                      • extreme days become independent in the limit: joint crashes are rare
                                                    • t copula: the dependence of a bivariate Student-t with \(\rho\) and \(\nu\) Demarta & McNeil (2005)
                                                      • \(\lambda_L = 2\,t_{\nu+1}\Big(-\sqrt{(\nu + 1)(1 - \rho)/(1 + \rho)}\Big) > 0\): crashes cluster across assets
                                                      • \(t_{\nu+1}(\cdot)\): the distribution function of a Student-t with \(\nu + 1\) degrees of freedom; a smaller \(\nu\) or a larger \(\rho\) gives a larger \(\lambda_L\)
                                                    • Fit on BET and S&P 500: \(\rho = \sin(\pi\hat\tau/2) = 0.157\) (valid for both copulas); \(\hat\nu = 4.50\) by maximum likelihood
                                                      • log-likelihood: Gaussian \(92.1\), t \(219.5\); LR \(= 254.9\), far above \(\chi^2_{0.95}(1) = 3.84\): the t copula wins

                                                        Copula VaR: the Simulation Step by Step

                                                        Slide 43 in the PDF

                                                        The simulation has four steps. First, draw \((Z_1, Z_2)\) from a bivariate Normal with correlation \(\rho\).                                                         The Idea: the Hit Sequence

                                                        Slide 45 in the PDF

                                                        An exception, or exceedance, is a day on which the loss exceeded the VaR forecast, \(r_t < -\mathrm{VaR}_t\). The hit sequence records them: \(I_t = \mathbf{1}\{r_t < -\mathrm{VaR}_t\}\). The VaR must be the forecast made one day before, with no information from the future; a VaR estimated on a sample that includes day \(t\) will be exceeded less often than a real forecast.

                                                        If the VaR at level \(\alpha\) is correct, the hits \(I_t\) are i.i.d. Bernoulli(\(\alpha\)) (Christoffersen, 1998). This statement contains two separate properties. Unconditional coverage, \(P(I_t = 1) = \alpha\), means that the number of exceptions is right on average. Independence means that an exception today does not make an exception tomorrow more likely: the timing of exceptions carries no information.

                                                        Under both properties, the number of exceptions in \(n\) days, \(x = \sum_t I_t\), follows a \(\mathrm{Binomial}(n, \alpha)\) distribution, with mean \(n\alpha\) and variance \(n\alpha(1 - \alpha)\). Every test of the section compares the observed hit sequence with this benchmark.

                                                        In brief

                                                        • Exception (hit): a day with \(r_t < -\mathrm{VaR}_t\); \(I_t = \mathbf{1}\{r_t < -\mathrm{VaR}_t\}\)
                                                          • the VaR must be the forecast made the day before: no look-ahead
                                                        • A correct VaR at level \(\alpha\): the \(I_t\) are i.i.d. Bernoulli(\(\alpha\)) Christoffersen (1998)
                                                          • unconditional coverage: \(P(I_t = 1) = \alpha\): the right number of exceptions
                                                          • independence: an exception today does not make one tomorrow more likely
                                                        • Number of exceptions in \(n\) days: \(x = \sum_t I_t \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(n, \alpha)\), mean \(n\alpha\), variance \(n\alpha(1 - \alpha)\)

                                                                            Kupiec's POF Test

                                                                            Slide 47 in the PDF

                                                                            The proportion-of-failures (POF) test of Kupiec (1995) tests unconditional coverage, \(H_0\): \(P(I_t = 1) = \alpha\). With \(x\) exceptions in \(n\) days and the observed rate \(\hat\pi = x/n\), the likelihood-ratio statistic compares the binomial likelihood at the rate \(\alpha\) with the likelihood at the rate \(\hat\pi\):

                                                                            \[LR_{uc} = -2\big[(n - x)\ln(1 - \alpha) + x\ln\alpha - (n - x)\ln(1 - \hat\pi) - x\ln\hat\pi\big].\]
                                                                            (10.14)

                                                                            Under \(H_0\), \(LR_{uc}\) follows asymptotically a \(\chi^2(1)\) distribution, as every likelihood-ratio test with one restriction (Chapter 6). It is never negative, because \(\hat\pi\) maximises the likelihood, and the hypothesis is rejected at 5% when \(LR_{uc} > 3.84\).

                                                                            Step by step, with \(n = 250\) and \(x = 7\), so \(\hat\pi = 0.028\): \(\ln L_0 = 243\ln 0.99 + 7\ln 0.01 = -34.68\) and \(\ln L_1 = 243\ln 0.972 + 7\ln 0.028 = -31.93\). Then \(LR_{uc} = -2(-34.68 - (-31.93)) = 5.50\), with p-value 0.019: the model is rejected at 5%.

                                                                            The test has low power with one year of data. In 250 days, any number of exceptions from 1 to 6 is accepted at 5%, so a model whose true rate is 2% passes the test with probability 76%. The test is two-sided: too few exceptions also lead to rejection, because an excessively prudent VaR wastes capital; with 250 days, however, only a year with no exception is rejected on that side.

                                                                            In brief

                                                                            • POF (proportion of failures) Kupiec (1995): \(H_0\): \(P(I_t = 1) = \alpha\); \(\hat\pi = x/n\)
                                                                              • \(LR_{uc} = -2\big[(n - x)\ln(1 - \alpha) + x\ln\alpha - (n - x)\ln(1 - \hat\pi) - x\ln\hat\pi\big] \sim \chi^2(1)\) under \(H_0\)
                                                                              • \(x\): exceptions in \(n\) days; \(\hat\pi\): the observed rate; \(LR_{uc}\) compares the likelihood under the rate \(\alpha\) with that under \(\hat\pi\)
                                                                              • \(LR_{uc} \ge 0\); a likelihood-ratio test (Chapter 6); reject at 5% if \(LR_{uc} > 3.84\)
                                                                            • Step by step: \(n = 250\), \(x = 7\), \(\hat\pi = 0.028\)
                                                                              • \(\ln L_0 = 243\ln 0.99 + 7\ln 0.01 = -34.68\); \(\ln L_1 = 243\ln 0.972 + 7\ln 0.028 = -31.93\)
                                                                              • \(LR_{uc} = -2(-34.68 - (-31.93)) = 5.50\), p \(= 0.019\): rejected at 5%
                                                                            • Low power with one year of data
                                                                              • in 250 days, any count from 1 to 6 is accepted at 5%: a model with a true rate of 2% passes with probability 76%

                                                                            Christoffersen's Independence Test

                                                                            Slide 49 in the PDF

                                                                            The Kupiec test counts exceptions but ignores when they happen. Christoffersen (1998) tests their timing by counting transitions: \(n_{ij}\) is the number of days with \(I_{t-1} = i\) and \(I_t = j\). The rate of exceptions after a day without an exception is \(\hat\pi_{01} = n_{01}/(n_{00} + n_{01})\), and after a day with an exception \(\hat\pi_{11} = n_{11}/(n_{10} + n_{11})\). The null hypothesis \(H_0\): \(\pi_{01} = \pi_{11}\) says that an exception today does not change the chance of an exception tomorrow. The likelihood ratio compares a single rate \(\hat\pi\) with the two transition rates:

                                                                            \[LR_{ind} = -2\ln\frac{(1 - \hat\pi)^{n_{00} + n_{10}}\hat\pi^{n_{01} + n_{11}}}{(1 - \hat\pi_{01})^{n_{00}}\hat\pi_{01}^{n_{01}}(1 - \hat\pi_{11})^{n_{10}}\hat\pi_{11}^{n_{11}}},\]
                                                                            (10.15)

                                                                            asymptotically \(\chi^2(1)\) under \(H_0\). Conditional coverage combines both properties: \(LR_{cc} = LR_{uc} + LR_{ind}\) is asymptotically \(\chi^2(2)\), and a model is rejected at 5% if \(LR_{cc} > 5.99\).

                                                                            For the HS VaR 1% of the S&P 500 over 5,460 days, the transition counts are \(n_{00} = 5313\), \(n_{01} = 69\), \(n_{10} = 69\) and \(n_{11} = 8\). The exception rate after a quiet day is \(\hat\pi_{01} = 1.28\%\), after an exception \(\hat\pi_{11} = 10.4\%\): an exception is eight times more likely the day after another exception. The statistic \(LR_{ind} = 19.43\) has a p-value below 0.001; the exceptions of HS cluster.

                                                                            The test detects first-order dependence, exceptions on consecutive days. Clustering spread over a week, with quiet days in between, shows up only weakly in the transition counts, so the test is a minimal check of independence, not a complete one.

                                                                            In brief

                                                                            • Count the transitions: \(n_{ij}\) = number of days with \(I_{t-1} = i\) and \(I_t = j\); \(\hat\pi_{01} = \dfrac{n_{01}}{n_{00} + n_{01}}\), \(\hat\pi_{11} = \dfrac{n_{11}}{n_{10} + n_{11}}\)
                                                                              • \(\hat\pi_{01}\): the rate of exceptions after a day without one; \(\hat\pi_{11}\): after a day with one
                                                                              • \(H_0\): \(\pi_{01} = \pi_{11}\) (an exception today does not change the chance of one tomorrow) Christoffersen (1998)
                                                                              • \(LR_{ind} = -2\ln\dfrac{(1 - \hat\pi)^{n_{00} + n_{10}}\hat\pi^{n_{01} + n_{11}}}{(1 - \hat\pi_{01})^{n_{00}}\hat\pi_{01}^{n_{01}}(1 - \hat\pi_{11})^{n_{10}}\hat\pi_{11}^{n_{11}}} \sim \chi^2(1)\)
                                                                            • Conditional coverage: \(LR_{cc} = LR_{uc} + LR_{ind} \sim \chi^2(2)\); reject at 5% if \(LR_{cc} > 5.99\)
                                                                            • Example: S&P 500, HS VaR 1%, 5 460 days: \(n_{00} = 5313\), \(n_{01} = 69\), \(n_{10} = 69\), \(n_{11} = 8\)
                                                                              • \(\hat\pi_{01} = 1.28\%\), \(\hat\pi_{11} = 10.4\%\): after an exception the next one is 8 times more likely
                                                                              • \(LR_{ind} = 19.43\) (p \(<\) 0.001): the exceptions cluster

                                                                                          Backtesting ES: Acerbi and Székely

                                                                                          Slide 56 in the PDF

                                                                                          ES alone is not elicitable, but it can be tested together with VaR (Acerbi & Székely, 2014). Their second test statistic is

                                                                                          \[Z_2 = \frac{1}{T\alpha}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{r_t I_t}{\mathrm{ES}_t} + 1, \qquad I_t = \mathbf{1}\{r_t < -\mathrm{VaR}_t\}, \quad \alpha = 2.5\%,\]
                                                                                          (10.17)

                                                                                          where \(T\) is the number of test days and \(\mathrm{VaR}_t\), \(\mathrm{ES}_t\) the forecasts for day \(t\). Each exception adds \(r_t/\mathrm{ES}_t\), a negative number close to −1 if the loss is as large as the forecast ES. If VaR and ES are both correct, the expected sum is \(-T\alpha\) and \(E[Z_2] = 0\). A negative \(Z_2\) means that the losses beyond the VaR are larger than the forecast ES, or that exceptions are too frequent, or both.

                                                                                          The distribution of \(Z_2\) under the null depends on the forecast distributions, so its p-value is obtained by simulation: draw a return from each day's forecast distribution, recompute \(Z_2\), repeat 1000 times, and compare the observed value with the simulated ones. The values and p-values are the last column of the two backtest tables.

                                                                                          For the S&P 500, the DAX and the BET, every method has a negative \(Z_2\) with a p-value below 0.05: all four ES 2.5% forecasts are too small for the equity indices. FHS comes closest, with \(Z_2\) between −0.18 and −0.20, because it takes the tail of \(z\) from the data; the Normal method is furthest, between −0.83 and −1.15. For Bitcoin, HS (p = 0.224) and FHS (\(Z_2 = 0.12\), p = 0.834) are not rejected.

                                                                                          In brief

                                                                                          • ES is not elicitable alone, but it can be tested together with VaR Acerbi & Székely (2014)

                                                                                            • \(T\): the number of test days; \(\mathrm{VaR}_t\), \(\mathrm{ES}_t\): the forecasts for day \(t\); each exception adds \(r_t/\mathrm{ES}_t\)

                                                                                          Case Study: VaR Models at Commercial Banks

                                                                                          Slide 57 in the PDF

                                                                                          Berkowitz & O'Brien (2002) were the first to study the VaR forecasts actually reported by large US banks, comparing them with the banks' daily trading P&L. The design is a backtest on real regulatory data: each bank's VaR 1% against its realised P&L, with a simple GARCH model of each bank's own P&L as a benchmark.

                                                                                          The banks' VaRs were conservative on average, with fewer exceptions than the nominal rate, but the exceptions that did occur came in clusters. The GARCH benchmark produced lower VaRs with comparable coverage: a simple model of the P&L series tracked changes in volatility better than the banks' structural models.

                                                                                          The same pattern appears in the data of this chapter: the window-based VaR is either too high in quiet periods or too slow in turbulent ones, while the conditional VaR adapts. A further line of work forecasts the quantile directly, without a model for the whole distribution: the CAViaR models of Engle & Manganelli (2004) specify an autoregression for the quantile itself and estimate it with the quantile loss (10.5).

                                                                                          An Open Question

                                                                                          Slide 58 in the PDF

                                                                                          Does the tail dependence between the BVB and world markets jump in crises and then revert? The chapter's results point in that direction. The BET–S&P 500 correlation was 0.20 in a quiet year and 0.71 in the spring of 2020; a constant t copula gives a tail dependence of \(\lambda_L = 0.096\); and a portfolio VaR built on a constant copula stays below the historical VaR (Portfolio VaR section).

                                                                                          What to Check

                                                                                          Slide 60 in the PDF

                                                                                          Several errors are common in AI-generated risk code. The first is the VaR convention: "VaR 1%" is the loss exceeded with probability 1%, a positive number; an answer that uses the complementary probability, or reports a negative VaR, mixes conventions. The second is data alignment: prices must be aligned on common trading days before returns are computed, and the BVB and New York trade at different hours.

                                                                                          The third concerns the copula itself. Pseudo-observations are the ranks divided by \(n + 1\), not by \(n\): otherwise the largest observation sits exactly at 1, where the quantile transforms \(\Phi^{-1}\) and \(t_\nu^{-1}\) are infinite and the copula log-likelihood is undefined. Tail dependence estimated from 250 days rests on a few joint bad days, so its uncertainty must be reported, for example with the bootstrap of Chapter 2.

                                                                                          Overlapping windows share most of their data, so consecutive estimates are not independent evidence: a rolling series that crosses a threshold several times is one finding, not several. Finally, every reference must exist: the DOI of each cited paper has to be checked, because invented references with plausible titles are a known failure of language models.

                                                                                          In brief

                                                                                          • The VaR convention: “VaR 1%” is the loss exceeded with probability 1%, a positive number; an answer that names the complementary probability or reports a negative VaR mixes conventions
                                                                                          • Prices joined on common days before computing returns; the BVB and New York trade at different hours
                                                                                          • Pseudo-observations: ranks divided by \(n + 1\), not by \(n\) (otherwise the copula density is infinite at 1)
                                                                                          • Tail dependence from 250 days rests on a handful of joint bad days: report its uncertainty (bootstrap, Chapter 2)
                                                                                          • Overlapping windows: consecutive estimates are not independent evidence
                                                                                          • References: every cited paper must exist; check the DOI

                                                                                          Key Takeaways

                                                                                          Slide 62 in the PDF

                                                                                          VaR 1% is the negative of the 1% quantile, (10.1): the loss exceeded on one day in a hundred. ES 2.5%, (10.2), is the average loss on the worst 2.5% of the days. ES is coherent; VaR can penalise diversification, as in the two-bond example. VaR is elicitable, while ES is elicitable only jointly with VaR.

                                                                                          On daily data, the Normal VaR 1% is 14–24% too small on all six series. Historical simulation, the Student-t and EVT agree at 1%, and Cornish–Fisher breaks down when the kurtosis is large. Conditional VaR, GARCH-t and FHS, tracks turbulent periods and passes tests that the window-based methods fail, above all the independence of exceptions.

                                                                                          For portfolios, correlation rises in crises, and tail dependence, captured by the t copula, matters for joint crashes. Backtesting combines four tools: the Kupiec test for the rate of exceptions, the Christoffersen test for their clustering, the Basel traffic light for regulation and the \(Z_2\) statistic for ES.

                                                                                          Check Yourself

                                                                                          Slide 64 in the PDF

                                                                                          The first question gives a division's report "VaR 1% = −2.4%". The sign is wrong: −2.4% is the quantile \(q_{0.01}\) of the returns, and by (10.1) the VaR 1% is \(-q_{0.01} = 2.4\%\), a positive loss.

                                                                                          The second question describes a model with 3 exceptions in 250 days, all in the same week. The Kupiec test does not detect the problem, because 3 is close to the expected 2.5. The Christoffersen independence test can detect it if the exceptions fall on consecutive days, because these produce transitions from an exception to an exception; the test looks only at day-to-day transitions, so three exceptions on Monday, Wednesday and Friday would leave it blind; and with only three exceptions in a year its power is limited, which is why the test is applied to long samples.

                                                                                          The third question asks whether two portfolios with the same VaR 1% can have different ES 2.5%. They can: ES depends on the whole tail beyond the quantile, VaR on a single point. The next chapter, Chapter 11, turns to the fractal market hypothesis and long memory.
