The previous chapters built statistical models with a structure fixed in advance: distributions for returns, GARCH (generalised autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity) models for volatility, models for VaR (value at risk). Machine learning (ML) starts from the opposite direction: it picks a flexible prediction rule from the data and judges it by its accuracy on data it has not seen. The question of the chapter is whether these methods forecast financial risk and returns better than the models of Chapters 7–10, and how such a gain is checked honestly.

The answer depends on the target. For the weekly volatility of the S&P 500, the lasso and a small neural network reduce the squared forecast error by 7.1% and 8.3% relative to the HAR (heterogeneous autoregressive) model, and the differences are significant. For Bitcoin, no model beats HAR. For the sign of tomorrow's return, no model is significantly better than the rule "always up", on any market studied. For VaR 1%, a linear quantile regression has the right violation rate on the S&P 500, while its flexible version runs short of data in the tail.

The difference between a real result and an apparent one lies in validation. A random split of the data produces an \(R^2\) of 34.5% for a simulated random walk, which has nothing to predict; a walk-forward scheme gives a negative \(R^2\) for the same data. The chapter therefore builds, before the models, the rules of evaluation: samples separated in time, simple benchmarks, tests of the difference between forecasts and a correction for the number of models tried.

What Machine Learning Adds to Statistics

The section places machine learning in the history of statistics. It starts from the perceptron of 1958, presents Breiman's distinction between data modelling and algorithmic modelling, separates prediction from inference and fixes the notation of supervised learning: features, target, loss function and hyperparameters. It ends with a map of the areas of finance where these methods help and those where they struggle.

The Perceptron, 1958

Slide 6 in the PDF

Rosenblatt (1958) described a machine that learns to classify images by adjusting its weights after each error. The perceptron receives a vector of inputs \(x\), in the original version the signals of photocells, and outputs 1 if \(w_0 + w^\top x > 0\) and 0 otherwise. The weights \(w\) and the threshold \(w_0\) are not fixed by the designer but learned from examples: when the machine makes a mistake, the weights move in the direction that would have corrected it.

The Mark I Perceptron, built at Cornell in 1960, had 400 photocells, and its weights were set by electric motors. The central idea outlived this mechanical implementation: a linear decision rule, with parameters estimated by repeatedly correcting errors, is the ancestor of the neuron in the modern networks presented in Section 6.

Three later milestones mark the development of the field. In 1986, Rumelhart, Hinton & Williams (1986) showed how back-propagation of the error trains networks with hidden layers. In 2001 came random forests (Breiman, 2001b) and gradient boosting (Friedman, 2001), the tree methods used in Section 5. In 2024, the Nobel Prize in Physics was awarded to Hopfield and Hinton for their contributions to neural networks.

In brief

  • Rosenblatt (1958): a machine that learns to classify images by adjusting weights after each error
    • the perceptron: output \(1\) if \(w_0 + w^\top x > 0\), else \(0\)
    • \(x\): the inputs (photocell signals); \(w\): their weights, learned from examples; \(w_0\): the threshold term; \(w^\top x = \sum_j w_jx_j\)
    • the Mark I Perceptron (Cornell, 1960): 400 photocells, weights set by electric motors
  • Three later milestones
    • 1986: back-propagation trains networks with hidden layers Rumelhart, Hinton & Williams (1986)
    • 2001: random forests Breiman (2001b) and gradient boosting Friedman (2001)
    • 2024: Nobel Prize in Physics to Hopfield and Hinton for neural networks

Two Cultures of Statistical Modelling

Slide 7 in the PDF

Breiman (2001a), a statistician at Berkeley, describes two ways of using data. In the data-modelling culture, the researcher assumes a stochastic model, for example the linear regression \(y = x^\top\beta + \varepsilon\) or a GARCH model, estimates it and tests its parameters. The conclusions concern the parameters, and their validity depends on the assumed model being right.

In the algorithmic-modelling culture, the mechanism that generated the data is treated as unknown. The goal is a rule \(\hat f\) that predicts \(y\) well on new data, and a rule is judged by its prediction error, not by the plausibility of a model. Trees, random forests and neural networks belong to this culture.

The course has so far worked in the first culture, from Chapter 2 to Chapter 11: distributions, volatility models, tests of long memory. This chapter adds the second culture and asks under what conditions it brings a real gain on financial data, where noise is large and only one history is available.

In brief

  • Breiman (2001a), a statistician at Berkeley, describes two cultures
    • data modelling: assume a stochastic model (the linear regression \(y = x^\top\beta + \varepsilon\), GARCH), estimate it, test its parameters
    • algorithmic modelling: treat the mechanism as unknown; find a rule \(\hat f\) that predicts \(y\) well on new data
  • This course used the first culture in Chapters 2–11
    • this chapter adds the second, and asks when it pays off in finance

Prediction against Inference

Slide 8 in the PDF

Inference asks whether a coefficient \(\beta_j\) differs from zero and how large the effect is, using standard errors and tests, as in Chapter 6. An example from the course is the leverage effect: the asymmetry parameter of a GJR-GARCH (Glosten–Jagannathan–Runkle GARCH) model, from Chapter 9, says whether price falls raise volatility more than rises.

Prediction asks how close the forecast \(\hat y\) is to the value \(y\) on data the model has never seen. Typical examples are next week's volatility and tomorrow's VaR 1%. The coefficients of a prediction model may be hard to interpret, and sometimes do not even exist as parameters with an economic meaning; only the error on new data matters.

The two goals can conflict. A biased estimator, such as ridge or the lasso of Section 4, can have a smaller prediction error than the unbiased OLS (ordinary least squares) estimator, because it gains more by reducing variance than it loses through bias. Conversely, a model that predicts well does not prove a causal mechanism: it may exploit a stable correlation without saying anything about its cause.

In brief

  • Inference: is \(\beta_j \ne 0\)? how large is the effect? (standard errors, tests, Chapter 6)
    • example: does the leverage effect exist (GJR-GARCH, Chapter 9)?
  • Prediction: how close is \(\hat y\) to \(y\) on data the model has never seen?
    • example: next week's volatility; tomorrow's VaR 1%
    • the coefficients may be uninterpretable; what matters is the error on new data
  • The two goals can conflict
    • a biased estimator (ridge, lasso) can predict better than the unbiased OLS
    • a model that predicts well does not prove a causal mechanism

Supervised Learning: Notation and Loss (1/2)

Slide 9 in the PDF

Supervised learning starts from pairs \((x_t, y_t)\), \(t = 1, \dots, n\), where \(t\) indexes the observations, for example days or weeks. The vector \(x_t \in \mathbb{R}^p\) contains the features: \(p\) numbers known at time \(t\), such as past volatilities and returns. The target \(y_t\) is the quantity we want to forecast, for example next week's volatility.

The condition "known at time \(t\)" is essential in finance. A feature that uses, even indirectly, information from after time \(t\) makes the forecast look better than it could be in real time; Section 3 treats this error in detail.

Problems are divided by the nature of the target. In regression, \(y\) is a continuous variable, for example a volatility or a return. In classification, \(y\) is a class: up or down, default or repayment, as in the scoring models of Chapter 12. The methods of the chapter have versions for both types of problem.

In brief

  • Data \((x_t, y_t)\), \(t = 1, \dots, n\)
    • \(t\): the index of the observation (a day, a week); \(n\): the number of observations
    • \(x_t \in \mathbb{R}^p\): the features, \(p\) numbers known at time \(t\) (past volatilities, past returns)
    • \(y_t\): the target, the quantity we want to forecast (next week's volatility)
  • Two types of problems
    • regression: \(y\) continuous (volatility, return)
    • classification: \(y\) a class (up/down, default; Chapter 12)

Supervised Learning: Notation and Loss (2/2)

Slide 10 in the PDF

Learning means choosing, from a family of candidate rules \(\mathcal{F}\), the rule \(\hat f\) that minimises the average loss \(\frac1n\sum_t L(y_t, f(x_t))\). The loss function \(L(y, \hat y)\) gives the cost of the forecast \(\hat y\) when \(y\) is observed. The family \(\mathcal{F}\) may be, for example, the set of all linear regressions or of all trees of depth 3; the richer the family, the more closely the chosen rule can adapt to the data.

The loss function must match the purpose of the forecast. The squared error \((y - \hat y)^2\), whose average is the MSE (mean squared error), is the default choice for regression. For variances, the course used QLIKE from Chapter 8; for VaR, the quantile (pinball) loss from Chapter 10; for probabilities, the log loss from Chapter 12.

The parameters of a rule, such as coefficients or weights, are learned by estimation. A hyperparameter is chosen before estimation and controls the complexity of the rule: the depth of a tree, the penalty \(\lambda\) in ridge regression, the number of neurons in a network. Choosing hyperparameters is itself a form of estimation and needs separate data, as the following sections show.

In brief

  • Learning = choose \(\hat f\) in a family \(\mathcal{F}\) that minimises the average loss \(\frac1n\sum_t L(y_t, f(x_t))\)
    • \(f(x_t)\): the forecast of a rule \(f\) for observation \(t\); \(L(y, \hat y)\): the cost of forecasting \(\hat y\) when \(y\) is observed
    • \(\mathcal{F}\): the candidate rules (all linear regressions, all trees of depth 3); \(\hat f\): the rule chosen from the data
  • Losses used in this course
    • squared error \((y - \hat y)^2\) (MSE: mean squared error); QLIKE for variances (Chapter 8)
    • quantile (pinball) loss for VaR (Chapter 10); log loss for probabilities (Chapter 12)
  • A hyperparameter is chosen before the fit
    • examples: tree depth, penalty \(\lambda\), number of neurons
    • the parameters (coefficients, weights) are learned by the fit

Where ML Helps in Finance, and Where It Struggles

Slide 11 in the PDF

Machine learning has a chance of success when the target has structure and the data are sufficient. Volatility is such a case: Chapters 8 and 9 showed that it is persistent, so much of it can be forecast, and nonlinear effects are plausible. A second case is cross-sections with thousands of stocks and many characteristics, as in the case study of Section 11.

The main difficulty is a low signal-to-noise ratio. Daily returns are almost unpredictable (Chapter 7), and a flexible model applied to them mostly learns noise. Markets change regimes, and crises bring situations absent from the training history, so the past is an imperfect training set for the future.

There is also a limit of principle: we have a single price history. The experiment cannot be repeated, the observations are dependent over time, and the real number of independent episodes, for example of crises, is small. These features explain why validation takes as important a place in the chapter as the models.

In brief

  • Helps: targets with structure and enough data
    • volatility is persistent (Chapters 8–9): a lot to predict, nonlinear effects possible
    • cross-sections with thousands of stocks and many characteristics (the case study)
  • Struggles: a low signal-to-noise ratio
    • daily returns are close to unpredictable (Chapter 7): a flexible model fits the noise
    • markets change (regimes, crises): the past is an imperfect training set
    • one price history: we cannot repeat the experiment, and the observations are dependent

Section summary

Machine learning adds to statistics the culture of algorithmic modelling: a prediction rule chosen from the data and judged by its error on new data. The ingredients are the features, the target, the loss function and the hyperparameters. In finance, the approach has a chance where the target has structure, as volatility does, and struggles where noise dominates, as with daily returns. The next section explains why flexibility has a cost.

Overfitting, Bias and Variance

The section shows why a more flexible model is not automatically a better model. It distinguishes training error from test error, derives the decomposition of test error into bias, variance and noise, and illustrates the trade-off between them by a simulation with regression trees of different depths.

Training Error and Test Error

Slide 12 in the PDF

The training error is the average loss on the data used for estimation; the test error is the average loss on new data generated by the same process. The first always falls when the model becomes more flexible, because a richer family of rules also contains the simpler rules. Training error therefore cannot be used to compare models of different complexity.

Overfitting occurs when the model learns, besides the signal, the noise of the training sample. The symptom is a small training error together with a large test error. The extreme case is a tree with one leaf per observation: it reproduces the training data exactly, so its training error is zero, but its forecasts for new observations are poor.

Underfitting is the opposite error: the model is too rigid to capture the signal. An example is a constant volatility forecast in a market with volatility clustering: it ignores exactly the structure that makes volatility predictable.

In brief

  • Training error: the average loss on the data used for the fit; test error: on new data from the same process
    • the training error always falls as the model becomes more flexible
  • Overfitting: the model learns the noise of the training sample
    • symptom: a small training error and a large test error
    • a tree with one leaf per observation has zero training error and predicts badly
  • Underfitting: the model is too rigid to capture the signal
    • example: a constant forecast of volatility in a world with volatility clustering

The Bias–Variance Decomposition (1/2)

Slide 13 in the PDF

Assume the data are generated by \(y = f(x) + \varepsilon\), with \(E[\varepsilon] = 0\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon) = \sigma^2\). The function \(f\) is the true, unknown relation between \(x\) and \(y\), and \(\varepsilon\) is the noise, unpredictable from \(x\). The estimate \(\hat f\) is obtained on a random training sample; another sample would give another estimate, so \(\hat f(x_0)\) is a random variable.

For a new point \(x_0\), with \(y_0 = f(x_0) + \varepsilon_0\), the expected test error splits into three terms:

\[E\big[(y_0 - \hat f(x_0))^2\big] = \big(E[\hat f(x_0)] - f(x_0)\big)^2 + \mathrm{Var}\big(\hat f(x_0)\big) + \sigma^2.\]
(13.1)

The expectation is taken over all possible training samples and over the noise \(\varepsilon_0\). The first term is the squared bias: the distance between the average estimate and the true value. The second is the variance: how much \(\hat f(x_0)\) changes from one sample to another. The third is the noise, which does not depend on the model.

In brief

  • Model: \(y = f(x) + \varepsilon\), \(E[\varepsilon] = 0\), \(\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon) = \sigma^2\)
    • \(f\): the true, unknown relation between \(x\) and \(y\); \(\varepsilon\): the noise, unpredictable from \(x\), with variance \(\sigma^2\)
    • \(\hat f\): the estimate of \(f\) from a random training sample; another sample would give another \(\hat f\)
  • Expected test error at a new point \(x_0\), with \(y_0 = f(x_0) + \varepsilon_0\):
    \[ E\big[(y_0 - \hat f(x_0))^2\big] = \underbrace{\big(E[\hat f(x_0)] - f(x_0)\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\mathrm{Var}\big(\hat f(x_0)\big)}_{\text{variance}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{noise}} \]
    • \(E[\cdot]\): the average over all possible training samples and over the noise \(\varepsilon_0\)
    • bias: how far the average estimate is from the truth; variance: how much \(\hat f(x_0)\) moves from one sample to another

The Bias–Variance Decomposition (2/2)

Slide 14 in the PDF

The derivation starts by writing the error at \(x_0\) as a sum of three terms: \(y_0 - \hat f = (f - E\hat f) + (E\hat f - \hat f) + \varepsilon_0\). Squaring and taking expectations, the three squares give the squared bias, the variance and \(\sigma^2\). The cross terms have mean zero: the first term is a constant, \(E[E\hat f - \hat f] = 0\), and \(\varepsilon_0\) is independent of the training sample.

The decomposition (13.1) explains the bias–variance trade-off. A rigid model has large bias and small variance: it gives almost the same forecast on any sample, but a systematically wrong one. A flexible model has small bias and large variance: it fits well on average, but its forecast depends strongly on the particular sample. The optimal complexity balances the two terms.

The third term, \(\sigma^2\), is a floor: no model goes below it. For daily returns, \(\sigma^2\) is almost all of the error, because the predictable part \(f(x)\) is very small. In this situation, any extra variance brought by a flexible model shows up directly in the test error, and the possible gain from reducing bias is small.

In brief

  • Derivation: write \(y_0 - \hat f = (f - E\hat f) + (E\hat f - \hat f) + \varepsilon_0\), all at \(x_0\)
    • square and take the expectation: the three squares give bias\(^2\), variance and \(\sigma^2\)
    • the cross terms have mean 0: \(E[E\hat f - \hat f] = 0\), and \(\varepsilon_0\) is independent of the training sample
  • Reading the decomposition
    • a rigid model: large bias, small variance
    • a flexible model: small bias, large variance
    • \(\sigma^2\) is the floor: no model goes below it; in daily returns \(\sigma^2\) is almost everything

    Test error splits into squared bias, variance and noise, (13.1). Flexibility reduces bias and increases variance, hence the U shape of the test error, and the noise sets a floor that no model goes below. Complexity is therefore chosen on data not used for estimation, which requires a validation scheme suited to time series, the subject of the next section.

    Validation for Time Series

    The section sets the rules by which all the models of the chapter are evaluated. It defines the roles of the training, validation and test samples, the two errors that make a model look better than it is, look-ahead bias and leakage, and the validation schemes suited to time series. An experiment with a simulated random walk shows how large the effect of a wrong scheme can be.

    Training, Validation and Test Samples

    Slide 17 in the PDF

    Data have three roles. The training sample is used to estimate the parameters of each candidate model. The validation sample is used to choose the hyperparameters and the model. The test sample is used for a single measurement of the error of the finally chosen model; only this measurement honestly estimates performance on new data.

    Any decision taken on the basis of the test sample turns it into a validation sample. Trying ten models on the test data and reporting the best is no longer testing but model selection, and the reported error is optimistic. Section 10 quantifies this optimism for Sharpe ratios.

    K-fold cross-validation (CV) splits the data into \(K\) parts; each part is in turn the validation set, and the other \(K - 1\) parts train the model. The scheme is valid for i.i.d. data. For time series, Bergmeir, Hyndman & Koo (2018) show that it remains valid only under strict conditions, when no dependence is left in the model's errors.

    In brief

    • Three roles for the data
      • training: estimate the parameters of each candidate model
      • validation: choose the hyperparameters and the model
      • test: measure the error of the final choice, once
    • Every look at the test sample turns it into a validation sample
      • trying ten models on the test data and reporting the best is model selection, not testing
    • K-fold cross-validation: split the data into \(K\) folds; each fold is the validation set once, the others train the model
      • valid for i.i.d. data; for a time series only under strong conditions (no dependence left in the errors) Bergmeir, Hyndman & Koo (2018)

    Look-Ahead Bias and Leakage

    Slide 18 in the PDF

    Look-ahead bias occurs when the forecast for day \(t\) uses information that was not available at the close of day \(t - 1\). The usual sources are features computed with future prices, for example a centred moving average or a full-sample mean, scaling or standardisation with full-sample statistics, data revised later, and using today's index membership for the past (survivorship).

    Leakage is a related error: information about the test target reaches the training sample. The typical case is overlapping targets. The 21-day return starting on day \(t\) and the one starting on day \(t + 1\) share 20 days. If a random split puts day \(t + 1\) in training and day \(t\) in the test set, the model has seen almost all of the answer before being tested.

    Both errors make a model look better than it can be in real time, and neither shows up in the training error or in code that runs without errors. They must be ruled out by the construction of the validation scheme, not discovered afterwards.

    In brief

    • Look-ahead bias: the forecast for day \(t\) uses information that was not available at the close of day \(t - 1\)
      • features computed with future prices (a centred moving average, a full-sample mean)
      • scaling or standardising with statistics of the whole sample; revised data; today's index members (survivorship)
    • Leakage: information about the test target reaches the training sample
      • overlapping targets: the 21-day return from day \(t\) and from day \(t + 1\) share 20 days
      • a random fold puts day \(t + 1\) in training and day \(t\) in test: the model has seen the answer
    • Both make a model look better than it can be in real time

            Penalised Regression: Ridge and Lasso

            The section starts from the instability of the OLS estimator when features are many and correlated. It introduces ridge regression and the lasso, which add a penalty on the coefficients, computes them in an example with a single feature and applies them to the volatility features of the S&P 500.

            Many Features, Unstable OLS

            Slide 23 in the PDF

            The OLS estimator minimises the sum of squared errors and has the closed form

            \[\hat\beta = \arg\min_\beta \sum_t (y_t - x_t^\top\beta)^2 = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y,\]

            where \(X\) is the \(n \times p\) matrix whose row \(t\) is \(x_t^\top\), and \(y\) is the vector of targets. The estimator is unbiased, but its variance, \(\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}\), with \(\sigma^2\) the error variance, becomes very large when the features are many or strongly correlated, because the matrix \(X^\top X\) becomes nearly singular.

            Volatility features are a typical example: today's realised variance, this week's and this month's move together. OLS can then produce large coefficients of opposite signs that offset each other in sample; out of sample, where the correlations change slightly, the offsetting no longer works and the forecasts deteriorate.

            Penalised regression minimises the squared error plus a penalty on the size of \(\beta\). The penalty introduces a bias towards zero but reduces the variance of the estimator. In terms of the decomposition (13.1), a small bias is accepted for a large reduction in variance.

            In brief

            • OLS (ordinary least squares): \(\hat\beta = \arg\min_\beta \sum_t (y_t - x_t^\top\beta)^2 = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y\)
              • \(X\): the \(n \times p\) matrix whose row \(t\) is \(x_t^\top\); \(y\): the vector of targets; \(\arg\min_\beta\): the \(\beta\) that minimises the sum
              • unbiased, but its variance \(\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}\) (\(\sigma^2\): the error variance) explodes when features are many or strongly correlated
            • Volatility features are highly correlated: today's, this week's and this month's realised variance
              • OLS may give large coefficients of opposite signs that cancel in sample and fail out of sample
            • Penalised regression: minimise the squared error plus a penalty on the size of \(\beta\)
              • accept a little bias for a large reduction in variance (the trade-off of Section 2)

            Ridge Regression (1/2)

            Slide 24 in the PDF

            Hoerl & Kennard (1970) proposed penalising the squared coefficients:

            \[\hat\beta^{\,ridge} = \arg\min_\beta \sum_t (y_t - x_t^\top\beta)^2 + \lambda\sum_j \beta_j^2 = (X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y.\]
            (13.2)

            The term \(\sum_j \beta_j^2\) is large when the coefficients are large, and \(\lambda \ge 0\) sets the strength of the penalty; \(I\) is the \(p \times p\) identity matrix. For \(\lambda = 0\) we obtain OLS, and for \(\lambda \to \infty\) all coefficients tend to zero. The parameter \(\lambda\) is a hyperparameter and is chosen by validation.

            The closed form in (13.2) has an important consequence. The matrix \(X^\top X + \lambda I\) is invertible for any \(\lambda > 0\), even if \(X^\top X\) is not. Ridge therefore works even when there are more features than observations, a situation in which OLS has no unique solution.

            In brief

            • Hoerl & Kennard (1970): minimise the squared error plus a penalty on the squared coefficients
              \[ \hat\beta^{\,ridge} = \arg\min_\beta \sum_t (y_t - x_t^\top\beta)^2 + \lambda\sum_j \beta_j^2 = (X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y \]
            • Notation
              • \(\beta_j\): the coefficient of feature \(j\); \(\sum_j \beta_j^2\): the penalty term, large when the coefficients are large
              • \(\lambda \ge 0\): the strength of the penalty; \(\lambda = 0\) gives OLS, \(\lambda \to \infty\) gives \(\hat\beta \to 0\)
              • \(I\): the \(p \times p\) identity matrix
            • Consequence
              • \(X^\top X + \lambda I\) is always invertible for \(\lambda > 0\): ridge works even with more features than observations

            Ridge Regression (2/2)

            Slide 25 in the PDF

            The case of a single centred feature, with mean zero, shows the mechanism. With \(S_{xx} = \sum_t x_t^2\) and \(S_{xy} = \sum_t x_ty_t\),

            \[\hat\beta^{\,ridge} = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx} + \lambda} = \frac{S_{xx}}{S_{xx} + \lambda}\,\hat\beta^{\,OLS}, \qquad \hat\beta^{\,OLS} = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}.\]

            The factor \(S_{xx}/(S_{xx} + \lambda)\) lies in the interval \((0, 1]\) and is the fraction of the OLS coefficient that ridge keeps. Shrinkage is stronger when \(\lambda\) is large relative to \(S_{xx}\), that is, when the feature carries little information.

            In the example on the slide, \(S_{xx} = 100\) and \(S_{xy} = 30\). The OLS coefficient is \(30/100 = 0.30\), and the ridge coefficient with \(\lambda = 50\) is \(30/150 = 0.20\), with a shrinkage factor of 0.67. Ridge shrinks all coefficients towards zero but sets none of them exactly to zero: for any finite \(\lambda\), the factor stays positive.

            In brief

            • One feature, centred (mean 0): \(S_{xx} = \sum_t x_t^2\), \(S_{xy} = \sum_t x_ty_t\)
              \[ \hat\beta^{\,ridge} = \dfrac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx} + \lambda} = \dfrac{S_{xx}}{S_{xx} + \lambda}\,\hat\beta^{\,OLS}, \qquad \hat\beta^{\,OLS} = \dfrac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}} \]
              • the factor \(S_{xx}/(S_{xx} + \lambda)\) lies in \((0, 1]\): the share of the OLS coefficient that ridge keeps
            • Example: \(S_{xx} = 100\), \(S_{xy} = 30\)
              • OLS: \(30/100 = 0.30\)
              • ridge with \(\lambda = 50\): \(30/150 = 0.20\) (shrinkage factor \(0.67\))
            • Ridge shrinks all coefficients towards zero, but sets none exactly to zero

            The Lasso

            Slide 26 in the PDF

            The lasso, proposed by Tibshirani (1996), replaces the squares by absolute values:

            \[\hat\beta^{\,lasso} = \arg\min_\beta \sum_t (y_t - x_t^\top\beta)^2 + \lambda\sum_j |\beta_j|.\]

            The absolute value has a kink at zero, and the solution can stop at this point: some coefficients become exactly zero. Besides shrinkage, the lasso thus performs variable selection.

            For a single feature, the solution is soft thresholding: \(\hat\beta^{\,lasso} = \mathrm{sign}(S_{xy})\,\max(|S_{xy}| - \lambda/2,\, 0)/S_{xx}\), where \(\mathrm{sign}(S_{xy})\) is the sign, \(+1\) or \(-1\). If \(|S_{xy}| \le \lambda/2\), the coefficient is exactly zero. With the same data as for ridge and \(\lambda = 20\), the coefficient is \((30 - 10)/100 = 0.20\); for \(\lambda \ge 60\) the feature leaves the model.

            In practice, the features are standardised before estimation, because the penalty depends on their units; the mean and standard deviation used for standardisation are computed on the training data only, otherwise look-ahead bias appears. The parameter \(\lambda\) is chosen by walk-forward cross-validation. The elastic net combines the two penalties, \(L_1\) and \(L_2\).

            In brief

            • Tibshirani (1996): \(\hat\beta^{\,lasso} = \arg\min_\beta \sum_t (y_t - x_t^\top\beta)^2 + \lambda\sum_j |\beta_j|\)
              • the absolute value has a kink at 0: some coefficients become exactly zero (variable selection)
            • One feature: soft thresholding
              • \(\hat\beta^{\,lasso} = \mathrm{sign}(S_{xy})\,\max(|S_{xy}| - \lambda/2,\, 0)/S_{xx}\)
              • \(\mathrm{sign}(S_{xy})\): \(+1\) or \(-1\); if \(|S_{xy}| \le \lambda/2\), the coefficient is exactly 0
              • same data, \(\lambda = 20\): \((30 - 10)/100 = 0.20\); zero for \(\lambda \ge 60\)
            • In practice
              • standardise the features first (the penalty depends on their units), with the training mean and standard deviation only
              • choose \(\lambda\) by walk-forward cross-validation; elastic net combines both penalties

                      Trees, Random Forest and Gradient Boosting

                      The section builds regression trees, from the splitting rule to an example computed by hand and a tree estimated on S&P 500 volatility.                       Regression Trees

                      Slide 29 in the PDF

                      A regression tree splits the feature space into rectangles and predicts, in each rectangle, the mean of \(y\) within it. The final rectangles are called leaves. Each split uses a feature \(j\) and a threshold \(s\): observations with \(x_j \le s\) go to the left, those with \(x_j > s\) to the right.

                      The tree is estimated greedily: at each node, the pair \((j, s)\) is chosen that minimises the sum of squared errors (SSE),

                      \[\mathrm{SSE} = \sum_{\text{left}}(y - \bar y_L)^2 + \sum_{\text{right}}(y - \bar y_R)^2,\]

                      where \(\bar y_L\) and \(\bar y_R\) are the means of \(y\) in the two groups, that is, the two forecasts. The procedure is repeated in each half until a stopping rule is met, for example a maximum depth or a minimum number of observations in a leaf.

                      Trees capture nonlinear effects and interactions without these being specified in advance, and they are insensitive to monotone transformations of the features, since they depend only on the order of the values. Their weak point is high variance: a small change in the data can change the first split and, through it, the whole tree, as in the simulation of Section 2.

                      In brief

                      • A regression tree splits the feature space into rectangles and predicts the mean of \(y\) in each leaf
                        • split rule: a feature \(j\) and a threshold \(s\): left \(x_j \le s\), right \(x_j > s\)
                      • Greedy fit: at each node choose \((j, s)\) that minimises \(\mathrm{SSE} = \sum_{\text{left}}(y - \bar y_L)^2 + \sum_{\text{right}}(y - \bar y_R)^2\)
                        • SSE: sum of squared errors; \(\bar y_L\), \(\bar y_R\): the means of \(y\) in the left and right groups (the two forecasts)
                        • repeat in each half until a stopping rule: maximum depth, minimum number of observations per leaf
                      • Strengths and weaknesses
                        • nonlinear effects and interactions without specifying them; insensitive to monotone transformations of \(x\)
                        • high variance: a small change in the data can change the whole tree (Section 2)

                            Random Forest

                            Slide 32 in the PDF

                            A random forest (Breiman, 2001b) averages \(B\) deep trees, each grown on a bootstrap sample of the training data, a method called bagging (bootstrap aggregating); the bootstrap was introduced in Chapter 2. In addition, only a random subset of features is tried at each split. This second source of randomness makes the trees less correlated with each other.

                            The effect of averaging is seen in a simple calculation. If each of the \(B\) trees has variance \(\sigma^2\) and the correlation between any two is \(\rho\), then

                            \[\mathrm{Var}(\text{average}) = \rho\sigma^2 + (1 - \rho)\frac{\sigma^2}{B}.\]
                            (13.3)

                            The second term falls with \(B\), the first does not. With \(\rho = 0.3\) and \(\sigma^2 = 1\), the variance is 1.000 for one tree, 0.370 for 10 trees and 0.302 for 300 trees, close to the floor \(\rho\sigma^2 = 0.3\).

                            Two practical conclusions follow from (13.3). More trees do not cause overfitting; they only bring the variance closer to the floor. The floor itself falls if the trees are less correlated, for example by trying fewer features at each split. The chapter uses 300 trees, at least 20 observations per leaf and half of the features tried at each split.

                            In brief

                            • Breiman (2001b): average \(B\) deep trees, each grown on a bootstrap sample (Chapter 2) of the training data (bagging)
                              • at each split only a random subset of the features is tried: the trees become less correlated
                            • Why averaging works: \(B\) trees, each with variance \(\sigma^2\) and pairwise correlation \(\rho\)
                              • \(\mathrm{Var}(\text{average}) = \rho\sigma^2 + (1 - \rho)\sigma^2/B\): the part \(\rho\sigma^2\), common to all trees, does not fall with \(B\)
                              • with \(\rho = 0.3\), \(\sigma^2 = 1\): \(B = 1\): \(1.000\); \(B = 10\): \(0.370\); \(B = 300\): \(0.302\)
                              • more trees never overfit; less correlated trees (fewer features per split) reduce the floor \(\rho\sigma^2\)
                            • Hyperparameters used here: 300 trees, at least 20 observations per leaf, half of the features tried at each split

                            Gradient Boosting

                            Slide 33 in the PDF

                            Gradient boosting (Friedman, 2001) builds the model step by step, each new small tree learning what the earlier trees missed. It starts from the constant forecast \(F_0 = \bar y\). At step \(m\), a small tree \(h_m\) is fitted to the residuals \(y_t - F_{m-1}(x_t)\), and the model is updated:

                            \[F_m = F_{m-1} + \nu\, h_m, \qquad 0 < \nu \le 1,\]

                            where \(\nu\) is the learning rate. For squared loss, the residuals equal minus the gradient of the loss with respect to the forecast; hence the name of the method, which is gradient descent in the space of functions.

                            The two ensemble methods attack different terms of (13.1). A random forest uses deep trees, with small bias, in parallel, and averaging reduces their variance. Boosting uses small trees, with large bias, in sequence, and each step reduces the bias. This is why, in boosting, too many steps cause overfitting, while in a random forest they do not.

                            The method works with any differentiable loss, replacing the residuals by minus the gradient of that loss. With the quantile loss we obtain quantile gradient boosting, used for VaR in Section 9.

                            In brief

                            • Friedman (2001): build the model step by step, each new small tree fitting what the previous ones missed
                              • start with \(F_0 = \bar y\); at step \(m\), fit a shallow tree \(h_m\) to the residuals \(y_t - F_{m-1}(x_t)\)
                              • update \(F_m = F_{m-1} + \nu\, h_m\), with the learning rate \(0 < \nu \le 1\)
                              • for squared loss the residuals are minus the gradient of the loss: hence the name
                            • Random forest against boosting
                              • forest: deep trees in parallel, averaging reduces variance
                              • boosting: shallow trees in sequence, each step reduces bias; too many steps overfit
                            • Any differentiable loss works: quantile loss gives quantile boosting (Section 9)

                                      From the Perceptron to the Multilayer Perceptron (1/2)

                                      Slide 36 in the PDF

                                      The basic unit of a network is the neuron: \(h = g(b + w^\top x)\). The neuron computes a weighted sum of the inputs \(x\), with weights \(w\) and constant \(b\) (the neuron's intercept), and passes it through an activation function \(g\). The result \(h\) is the neuron's output.

                                      The function \(g\) is nonlinear and fixed in advance. Nonlinearity is necessary: a composition of linear functions is still linear, so a network of neurons without an activation function would be just a linear regression, however many layers it had.

                                      The perceptron of Section 1 uses a step function, with values 0 or 1. Modern networks use smooth or piecewise-linear functions, which allow the gradient to be computed: the logistic function \(1/(1 + e^{-z})\), the hyperbolic tangent tanh and the ReLU (rectified linear unit), \(\max(0, z)\).

                                      In brief

                                      • A neuron: \(h = g(b + w^\top x)\), a weighted sum of the inputs passed through an activation function \(g\)
                                        • \(x\): the inputs; \(w\): their weights; \(b\): a constant (the bias term of the neuron); \(h\): the output of the neuron
                                        • \(g\): a fixed nonlinear function; without it, a network of neurons would be just a linear regression
                                      • Activation functions
                                        • the perceptron uses a step function (0 or 1)
                                        • modern networks use smooth or piecewise linear \(g\): logistic \(1/(1 + e^{-z})\), tanh, ReLU \(\max(0, z)\)

                                      From the Perceptron to the Multilayer Perceptron (2/2)

                                      Slide 37 in the PDF

                                      A multilayer perceptron (MLP) with one hidden layer of \(K\) neurons produces the forecast

                                      \[\hat y = c + \sum_{k=1}^{K} v_k\, g(b_k + w_k^\top x),\]
                                      (13.4)

                                      where \(w_k\) and \(b_k\) are the weights and constant of hidden neuron \(k\), \(v_k\) is the weight of this neuron in the output, and \(c\) is the output constant. Model (13.4) is a linear regression in \(K\) nonlinear features \(g(b_k + w_k^\top x)\), but these features are not chosen by the analyst: they are learned from the data as well.

                                      The number of parameters grows quickly. For 11 inputs and 16 neurons, the hidden layer has \(11 \times 16 = 176\) weights plus 16 constants, that is 192 parameters, and the output has \(16 + 1 = 17\); in total, 209 parameters.

                                      The universal approximation theorem of Hornik, Stinchcombe & White (1989) shows that, given enough neurons, a single hidden layer approximates any continuous function on a compact set. The theorem guarantees flexibility, but says nothing about how much data is needed to estimate such a network without overfitting.

                                      In brief

                                      • MLP (multilayer perceptron) with one hidden layer of \(K\) neurons
                                        \[ \hat y = c + \sum_{k=1}^{K} v_k\, g(b_k + w_k^\top x) \]
                                        • \(w_k\), \(b_k\): the weights and the constant of hidden neuron \(k\); \(v_k\): the weight of neuron \(k\) in the output; \(c\): the output constant
                                        • reading: a linear model in \(K\) nonlinear features \(g(b_k + w_k^\top x)\), which are themselves learned
                                      • Number of parameters with 11 inputs and 16 neurons
                                        • hidden layer: \(11 \times 16\) weights \(+ 16\) constants \(= 192\); output: \(16 + 1 = 17\); total 209

                                            Training a Network

                                            Slide 39 in the PDF

                                            The loss of a network is minimised by gradient descent: \(\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\nabla_\theta L\). The vector \(\theta\) contains all weights and constants, \(\nabla_\theta L\) is the gradient of the loss, and \(\eta > 0\) is the learning rate, the step size. Each step moves \(\theta\) in the direction in which the loss falls fastest.

                                            Back-propagation (Rumelhart, Hinton & Williams, 1986) computes the gradient layer by layer, applying the chain rule from the output back to the inputs. An epoch is one full pass through the training data. Adam is a variant of gradient descent with adaptive steps.

                                            The loss of a network is not convex, so different initial weights lead to different networks. The chapter fixes the random seeds and averages five networks, an ensemble. Against overfitting, the inputs and the target are standardised with training statistics, the weights get an \(L_2\) penalty, as in ridge, and the number of epochs is fixed. With little data, the network must be small: here a single hidden layer with 16 neurons is enough.

                                            • Minimise the loss by gradient descent: \(\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\nabla_\theta L\)
                                              • \(\theta\): all weights and constants; \(\nabla_\theta L\): the gradient, the vector of partial derivatives of the loss; \(\eta > 0\): the learning rate (the step size)
                                              • each step moves \(\theta\) in the direction in which the loss falls fastest
                                              • back-propagation computes \(\nabla_\theta L\) layer by layer with the chain rule Rumelhart, Hinton & Williams (1986)
                                              • one epoch = one pass over the training data; Adam is a gradient method with adaptive step sizes

The network drops this form and lets the data determine how the variance depends on the past.

                                            The NN-ARCH (neural-network ARCH) model thus keeps the mean-plus-volatility structure of Chapter 9 and makes only the shape of the functions flexible. The question is whether this flexibility adds anything to ARCH and GARCH.

Estimation has two steps: a network \(\hat f\) for the mean, with residuals \(e_t = r_t - \hat f(x_{t-1})\), then a network for \(e_t^2\), which gives the conditional variance \(\hat s^2(x_{t-1})\).

                                            The network used, as in the Quantlet, is an RBF (radial basis function) network with 3 units. Unit \(j\) computes \(\exp(-\|x - c_j\|^2/s_j^2)\), where \(c_j\) is its centre, obtained by clustering, \(s_j\) is its width, and \(\|x - c_j\|\) is the distance from \(x\) to the centre. The unit is close to 1 when \(x\) is near \(c_j\) and close to 0 far from it; the network's output is a linear combination of the 3 units.

                                            A technical problem arises in the second step: nothing in the network's construction forces \(\hat s^2 > 0\). A network for the variance needs a floor imposed from outside, while GARCH guarantees positivity through the restrictions on its parameters.

                                                Section summary

                                                An MLP, (13.4), is a linear regression in nonlinear features learned from the data and is trained by gradient descent and back-propagation. The textbook's NN-ARCH model gives an average volatility close to GARCH but, without long memory, reacts weakly to shocks such as the Brexit vote. On the USD/JPY level, a network is 31 times worse than the random walk. The next section applies all the models in a rigorous comparison for volatility.

                                                The first application compares the chapter's models on the target most favourable to them: next week's volatility. The section defines the target and the features, the HAR benchmark and three evaluation tools, out-of-sample \(R^2\), QLIKE and the Diebold–Mariano test, then presents the results for the S&P 500 and Bitcoin, the forecasts in two crisis episodes and the importance of the features.

                                                Target and Features (1/2)

                                                Slide 47 in the PDF

                                                Without intraday data, daily variance is estimated from the open, high, low and close prices, with the proxy of Chapter 8:

                                                \[v_t = o_t^2 + \tfrac12(u_t - d_t)^2 - (2\ln 2 - 1)c_t^2.\]

                                                Here \(o_t\) is the overnight log return, from the previous close to the open, and \(u_t\), \(d_t\), \(c_t\) are the logs of the high, low and close relative to the open. The last two terms form the Garman & Klass (1980) estimator, which uses the day's range to reduce noise relative to the squared return.

                                                The target is \(y_t = \ln\big(\tfrac15\sum_{j=1}^{5} v_{t+j}\big)\), the log of realised variance over the next week, that is over the next five trading days. It is known only at the close of day \(t + 5\), which makes the targets of neighbouring days overlap and calls for the purging of Section 3. The log brings the distribution of the target closer to the Normal distribution and guarantees positive forecasts after applying the exponential function.

                                                In brief

                                                • Daily variance proxy of Chapter 8 (no intraday data): an estimate of the variance of day \(t\) from its open, high, low and close

                                                  • \(u_t, d_t, c_t\): log high, low and close relative to the open (the Garman–Klass term, Garman & Klass (1980))

                                                Target and Features (2/2)

                                                Slide 48 in the PDF

                                                The features are computed at the close of day \(t\). The HAR set contains three features: \(\ln v_t\), the log of the 5-day average of \(v\) (the week) and the log of the 22-day average (the month). The extended set has 11 features: in addition, 4 lags of \(\ln v_t\), from \(\ln v_{t-1}\) to \(\ln v_{t-4}\), the 66-day average, the return \(r_t\), its negative part \(\min(r_t, 0)\) and its absolute value \(|r_t|\).

                                                The feature \(\min(r_t, 0)\) equals the return if it is negative and 0 otherwise. It lets falls and rises have different effects on future volatility, that is, the leverage effect. A linear model with \(r_t\) and \(\min(r_t, 0)\) has one slope for up days and another for down days.

                                                The extended set gives flexible models the chance to find information beyond HAR: individual lags, a longer horizon and asymmetry. If a model beats HAR, these features and the nonlinearities between them are the possible sources of the gain.

                                                In brief

                                                • Features at the close of day \(t\)
                                                  • HAR: \(\ln v_t\), \(\ln\) of the mean over 5 days (week) and over 22 days (month)
                                                  • extended (11): plus 4 lags of \(\ln v_t\) (\(\ln v_{t-1}, \dots, \ln v_{t-4}\)), the 66-day mean, \(r_t\), \(\min(r_t, 0)\), \(|r_t|\)
                                                • Notation
                                                  • \(r_t\): the log return of day \(t\); \(|r_t|\): its absolute value
                                                  • \(\min(r_t, 0)\): the return if negative, 0 otherwise; it lets falls and rises act differently (the leverage effect)

                                                The HAR Benchmark (1/2)

                                                Slide 49 in the PDF

                                                The HAR model of Corsi (2009) explains the log of future variance by three components:

                                                \[y_t = \beta_0 + \beta_d\ln v_t + \beta_w\ln v_t^{(w)} + \beta_m\ln v_t^{(m)} + u_t,\]
                                                (13.5)

                                                where \(v_t^{(w)}\) and \(v_t^{(m)}\) are the averages of \(v\) over the last 5 days and the last 22 days, \(\beta_0\) is the constant, \(\beta_d\), \(\beta_w\), \(\beta_m\) are the weights of the three horizons, and \(u_t\) is the error. The model is estimated by OLS.

                                                The three components correspond to market participants with different horizons: daily, weekly and monthly. With positive weights on all three, a volatility shock stays in the monthly average long after it has left the daily one, so it decays slowly.

                                                This slow decay mimics the long memory of volatility of Chapter 11, without the fractional parameters of a FIGARCH model. A linear model with four coefficients that reproduces this stylised fact is a hard benchmark to beat.

                                                In brief

                                                • HAR (heterogeneous autoregressive) model Corsi (2009)
                                                  \[ y_t = \beta_0 + \beta_d\ln v_t + \beta_w\ln v_t^{(w)} + \beta_m\ln v_t^{(m)} + u_t \]
                                                  • \(v_t^{(w)}\), \(v_t^{(m)}\): the mean of \(v\) over the last 5 days (week) and over the last 22 days (month)
                                                  • \(\beta_0\): the constant; \(\beta_d, \beta_w, \beta_m\): the weights of the three horizons; \(u_t\): the error
                                                • Interpretation
                                                  • three horizons of traders (daily, weekly, monthly); estimated by OLS
                                                  • with positive weights on the three horizons, a shock to volatility fades slowly
                                                  • a simple linear model that mimics long memory (Chapter 11) and is hard to beat

                                                Evaluation (1/3): Out-of-Sample \(R^2\)

                                                Slide 51 in the PDF

                                                The out-of-sample \(R^2\), proposed by Campbell & Thompson (2008), compares the squared error of the model with that of a benchmark forecast \(\tilde y_t\):

                                                \[R^2_{OOS} = 1 - \frac{\sum_t (y_t - \hat y_t)^2}{\sum_t (y_t - \tilde y_t)^2},\]
                                                (13.6)

                                                where \(\hat y_t\) is the model's forecast and the sums run over the test days only. In the chapter, the benchmark \(\tilde y_t\) is either the training mean or the HAR forecast.

                                                The measure is at most 1. A positive value means a smaller squared error than the benchmark's, a negative one a larger error. Unlike the in-sample \(R^2\), \(R^2_{OOS}\) can be negative, and negative values are common when flexible models are applied to noisy targets.

                                                For the S&P 500, the squared error of the MLP is 0.397 and that of HAR is 0.433. The ratio is 0.917, so \(R^2_{OOS} = 1 - 0.917 = +8.3\%\): the MLP reduces the squared error of the HAR forecasts by 8.3%.

                                                In brief

                                                • Out-of-sample \(R^2\) against a benchmark forecast \(\tilde y_t\) Campbell & Thompson (2008)
                                                  \[ R^2_{OOS} = 1 - \frac{\sum_t (y_t - \hat y_t)^2}{\sum_t (y_t - \tilde y_t)^2} \]
                                                  • \(\hat y_t\): the forecast of the model; sums over the test days only
                                                  • benchmark \(\tilde y_t\): the training mean, or HAR
                                                • Reading
                                                  • \(R^2_{OOS} \le 1\); \(R^2_{OOS} > 0\): smaller squared error than the benchmark; \(R^2_{OOS} < 0\): worse than the benchmark
                                                  • unlike the in-sample \(R^2\), it can be negative

                                                Evaluation (2/3): QLIKE

                                                Slide 52 in the PDF

                                                QLIKE (quasi-likelihood), introduced in Chapter 8, is a loss function for variance forecasts:

                                                \[L_t = \frac{\mathrm{RV}_t}{\hat h_t} + \ln\hat h_t,\]
                                                (13.7)

                                                where \(\mathrm{RV}_t\) is the realised variance over the next 5 days, the target in levels, and \(\hat h_t\) is its forecast. Patton (2011) shows that QLIKE is robust to using a noisy proxy instead of the true variance: the ranking of models is not distorted by the noise of the proxy.

                                                For a given \(\mathrm{RV}_t\), the loss is smallest at \(\hat h_t = \mathrm{RV}_t\), and a smaller average QLIKE indicates better forecasts. The loss is asymmetric, however. For \(\mathrm{RV} = 1\), the forecast \(\hat h = 0.5\) costs 1.307 and the forecast \(\hat h = 2\) costs 1.193, although both are off by a factor of 2.

                                                Underestimation is therefore penalised more than overestimation. The asymmetry matches the costs in risk management, where underestimated volatility leads to insufficient capital and VaR violations, that is, to the more costly error.

                                                In brief

                                                • QLIKE (Chapter 8): a loss for variance forecasts, robust to a noisy proxy Patton (2011)
                                                  \[ L_t = \frac{\mathrm{RV}_t}{\hat h_t} + \ln\hat h_t \]
                                                  • \(\mathrm{RV}_t\): the realised variance of the next 5 days (the target, in levels); \(\hat h_t\): its forecast
                                                  • for a given \(\mathrm{RV}_t\), the loss is smallest at \(\hat h_t = \mathrm{RV}_t\); lower average QLIKE is better
                                                • Asymmetry
                                                  • for \(\mathrm{RV} = 1\): \(\hat h = 0.5\) costs \(1.307\), \(\hat h = 2\) costs \(1.193\)
                                                  • under-prediction is punished more: for risk management, underestimating volatility is the costly error

                                                Evaluation (3/3): the Diebold–Mariano Test

                                                Slide 53 in the PDF

                                                The Diebold & Mariano (1995) (DM) test asks whether two models \(A\) and \(B\) have the same expected loss. Starting from the difference of the losses on each day,

                                                \[d_t = L_t^{A} - L_t^{B}, \qquad \mathrm{DM} = \frac{\bar d}{\sqrt{\widehat{\mathrm{LRV}}/n}},\]
                                                (13.8)

                                                where \(\bar d\) is the mean of \(d_t\) and \(n\) is the number of test days. Under the hypothesis of equal accuracy, the DM statistic is approximately standard Normal.

                                                The denominator uses the long-run variance \(\widehat{\mathrm{LRV}}\) of \(d_t\), estimated by Newey–West with 5 lags. The correction is needed because the overlapping 5-day targets make the loss differences autocorrelated; the plain variance would understate the uncertainty of \(\bar d\) and make the test too lenient.

                                                The sign of the statistic gives the direction: a negative DM means that model \(A\) has the smaller loss, a positive DM that \(B\) is better. If \(|\mathrm{DM}| > 1.96\), the difference is significant at the 5% level. In the following table, \(A\) is each machine learning model and \(B\) is HAR.

                                                In brief

                                                • Diebold–Mariano test Diebold & Mariano (1995): do two models \(A\) and \(B\) have the same expected loss?
                                                  \[ d_t = L_t^{A} - L_t^{B}, \qquad \mathrm{DM} = \frac{\bar d}{\sqrt{\widehat{\mathrm{LRV}}/n}} \sim N(0,1) \text{ under equal accuracy} \]
                                                  • \(L_t^{A}\), \(L_t^{B}\): the losses of the two models on day \(t\); \(\bar d\): the mean of \(d_t\); \(n\): the number of test days
                                                  • \(\widehat{\mathrm{LRV}}\): the Newey–West long-run variance of \(d_t\) (5 lags), because overlapping targets make \(d_t\) autocorrelated
                                                • Reading
                                                  • DM \(< 0\): \(A\) has the smaller loss; DM \(> 0\): \(B\) is better
                                                  • \(|\mathrm{DM}| > 1.96\): the difference is significant at 5%

                                                                    Feature Importance: Permutation and Shapley Values

                                                                    Slide 56 in the PDF

                                                                    Permutation importance shuffles the values of one feature in the test data and measures how much the test MSE rises. A large rise shows that the model relies on that feature. Correlated features share their importance, however: if one is shuffled, the model can use another similar one, so each looks less important than the group.

                                                                    Shapley values, from game theory, decompose each forecast: \(\hat f(x) = \phi_0 + \sum_j \phi_j(x)\). Here \(\phi_0\) is the average forecast, and \(\phi_j(x)\) is the contribution of feature \(j\) to this forecast: positive if the feature raises the forecast above the average, negative if it lowers it. The contribution \(\phi_j\) is the average change in \(\hat f\) when \(j\) is added to each subset of the other features. SHAP (Shapley additive explanations), by Lundberg & Lee (2017), offers fast versions for trees; for a linear model, \(\phi_j = \beta_j(x_j - \bar x_j)\).

                                                                    Both tools describe the model, not the economy. An important feature is one the model uses for forecasting, which does not mean that it causes future volatility.

                                                                    In brief

                                                                    • Permutation importance: shuffle one feature in the test data and measure how much the test MSE rises
                                                                      • large increase: the model relies on the feature; correlated features share (and hide) their importance
                                                                    • Shapley values (game theory): \(\hat f(x) = \phi_0 + \sum_j \phi_j(x)\), \(\phi_j\) the fair share of feature \(j\) in the forecast
                                                                      • \(\phi_0\): the average forecast; \(\phi_j(x) > 0\): feature \(j\) raises this forecast above the average, \(\phi_j(x) < 0\): lowers it
                                                                      • \(\phi_j\) averages the change of \(\hat f\) when \(j\) joins every subset of the other features
                                                                      • SHAP (Shapley additive explanations) Lundberg & Lee (2017): fast versions for trees; for a linear model \(\phi_j = \beta_j(x_j - \bar x_j)\)
                                                                    • Both describe the model, not the economy: importance is not causality

                                                                      A Classification Problem

                                                                      Slide 58 in the PDF

                                                                      The target is \(y_t = \mathbf{1}\{r_{t+1} > 0\}\), where \(\mathbf{1}\{\cdot\}\) is the indicator function, equal to 1 if the statement is true and 0 otherwise; \(y_t = 1\) means that tomorrow's return is positive. The features, computed at the close of day \(t\), are the last 5 returns, the sums of returns over 5, 21 and 63 days and the volatility over 21 and 63 days.

The data are the S&P 500 and the BET (Bucharest Exchange Trading), the benchmark index of the Bucharest Stock Exchange (BVB), from 2008, and Bitcoin from 2018. The measures, defined in Chapter 12, are accuracy, the share of days with the sign forecast correctly, and the AUC (area under the ROC curve), the area under the ROC (receiver operating characteristic) curve, for which 0.5 means no ranking ability.

                                                                      Expectations are modest for theoretical reasons. Weak-form efficiency, from Chapter 7, says that any reliable pattern in past returns would be exploited by trading and would disappear. A model that nevertheless finds such a pattern must be checked all the more carefully.

                                                                            Application: VaR 1% by Quantile Methods

                                                                            The third application forecasts VaR 1% directly as a conditional quantile. The section introduces quantile regression and the quantile loss, its version with boosted trees, and compares them by backtesting with historical simulation and with GARCH-t on the S&P 500 and Bitcoin.

                                                                            Quantile Regression (1/2)

                                                                            Slide 62 in the PDF

                                                                            The quantile regression of Koenker & Bassett (1978) estimates the conditional quantile of order \(\alpha\), \(q_\alpha(x) = x^\top\beta_\alpha\). The quantile \(q_\alpha(x)\) is the level below which \(y\) lies with probability \(\alpha\), conditional on \(x\), and the coefficients \(\beta_\alpha\) are specific to the level \(\alpha\): the same feature can have different effects on the tail and on the centre of the distribution.

                                                                            Estimation minimises the quantile loss. For an error \(u = y - q\),

                                                                            \[\rho_\alpha(u) = u\,(\alpha - \mathbf{1}\{u < 0\}).\]
                                                                            (13.9)

                                                                            An observation below the quantile (\(u < 0\)) costs \((1 - \alpha)|u|\), and one above it costs \(\alpha|u|\). For \(\alpha = 0.5\) the two costs are equal and we obtain median regression.

                                                                            With \(\alpha = 0.01\), observations below the quantile are 99 times more expensive than those above it. The minimum of the average loss is reached when about 1% of the observations lie below \(q\), which is exactly the property that defines the 1% quantile.

                                                                            In brief

                                                                            • Koenker & Bassett (1978): estimate the conditional \(\alpha\)-quantile \(q_\alpha(x) = x^\top\beta_\alpha\) by minimising the quantile (pinball) loss
                                                                              • \(q_\alpha(x)\): the level below which \(y\) falls with probability \(\alpha\), given \(x\); \(\beta_\alpha\): coefficients specific to the level \(\alpha\)
                                                                            • The loss of an error \(u = y - q\):
                                                                              \[ \rho_\alpha(u) = u\,(\alpha - \mathbf{1}\{u < 0\}) \]
                                                                              • below the quantile (\(u < 0\)) the error costs \((1 - \alpha)|u|\), above it \(\alpha|u|\)
                                                                              • with \(\alpha = 0.01\), the minimum is reached when about 1% of the observations lie below \(q\)

                                                                            Quantile Regression (2/2)

                                                                            Slide 63 in the PDF

                                                                            With \(\alpha = 0.01\) and \(q = -\mathrm{VaR}\), the loss (13.9) becomes the average quantile loss of Chapter 10: \((\alpha - I_t)(r_t + \mathrm{VaR}_t)\), where \(I_t = 1\) if \(r_t < -\mathrm{VaR}_t\), that is, if a violation occurs, and 0 otherwise. The same function thus serves both for estimating and for evaluating VaR forecasts.

                                                                            VaR 1% for day \(t + 1\) is \(\widehat{\mathrm{VaR}}_t = -\hat q_{0.01}(x_t)\), with features \(x_t\): the daily, weekly and monthly volatility, computed as square roots of the proxy, and the return \(r_t\). No distribution of returns is assumed: the data choose how volatility translates into the 1% tail. CAViaR (Engle & Manganelli, 2004) is a dynamic version of this idea, in which yesterday's quantile enters the equation for today's quantile.

                                                                            Quantile gradient boosting (QGB) uses the same loss, with trees added by boosting as in Section 5. It is more flexible than linear regression, but at the 1% level each leaf sees few observations from the tail, so the variance of the estimate is high.

                                                                            In brief

                                                                            • With \(\alpha = 0.01\) and \(q = -\mathrm{VaR}\): the average quantile loss of Chapter 10
                                                                              • \((\alpha - I_t)(r_t + \mathrm{VaR}_t)\), with \(I_t = 1\) if \(r_t < -\mathrm{VaR}_t\) (an exception) and 0 otherwise
                                                                            • VaR 1% for day \(t + 1\): \(\widehat{\mathrm{VaR}}_t = -\hat q_{0.01}(x_t)\), with \(x_t\) = daily, weekly and monthly volatility (square roots of the proxy) and \(r_t\)
                                                                              • no distribution is assumed: the data choose how volatility maps into the tail; CAViaR Engle & Manganelli (2004) is a dynamic version
                                                                            • Quantile gradient boosting: the same loss with boosted trees (Section 5)
                                                                              • flexible, but at 1% each leaf sees few tail observations: high variance

                                                                                    Data Snooping and the Deflated Sharpe Ratio

                                                                                    The section quantifies the optimism that arises when the best of many strategies tried on the same data is reported. It computes the expected maximum of the Sharpe ratios of strategies without skill, illustrates it with 30 moving-average rules on the S&P 500 and introduces the deflated Sharpe ratio, which corrects for the number of trials.

                                                                                    The Best of \(N\) Strategies (1/2)

                                                                                    Slide 67 in the PDF

                                                                                    Data snooping means trying many models or rules on the same data and reporting the best (White, 2000). With enough trials, a rule without any skill looks excellent in sample, because the maximum of many noisy estimates lies systematically above their true value. Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016) show that hundreds of published return predictors would not survive a correction for multiple testing.

                                                                                    The Sharpe ratio is the mean excess return divided by its standard deviation (Chapter 1). For a strategy without skill, the annual Sharpe ratio estimated on \(Y\) years of data is approximately \(N(0, 1/Y)\). The zero mean expresses the lack of skill, and the variance \(1/Y\) is the estimation error, which falls with the length of the sample.

                                                                                    Over 10 years, the standard deviation of the estimated Sharpe ratio is \(1/\sqrt{10} = 0.316\). A strategy without skill thus has a reasonable chance of showing a Sharpe ratio of 0.3 or more by luck alone, and when many strategies are tried, the best of them will look much better.

                                                                                    In brief

                                                                                    • Data snooping: trying many models or rules on the same data and reporting the best White (2000)
                                                                                      • with enough trials, a rule without skill looks excellent in sample
                                                                                      • hundreds of published return predictors would not survive a multiple-testing correction Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016)
                                                                                    • The annual Sharpe ratio of a strategy without skill over \(Y\) years is about \(N(0, 1/Y)\)
                                                                                      • Sharpe ratio: mean excess return divided by its standard deviation (Chapter 1); \(Y\): the number of years of data
                                                                                      • mean 0: no skill; variance \(1/Y\): the estimation error, which falls with the length of the sample

                                                                                    The Best of \(N\) Strategies (2/2)

                                                                                    Slide 68 in the PDF

                                                                                    Bailey & López de Prado (2014) give an approximation for the expected maximum of \(N\) independent trials without skill:

                                                                                    \[\mathrm{E}[\max] \approx \sigma\Big[(1 - \gamma)\,\Phi^{-1}\big(1 - \tfrac1N\big) + \gamma\,\Phi^{-1}\big(1 - \tfrac{1}{Ne}\big)\Big],\]
                                                                                    (13.10)

                                                                                    where \(\sigma\) is the standard deviation of the Sharpe ratios across trials, \(1/\sqrt{Y}\) in the case above, \(N\) is the number of trials, \(\Phi^{-1}\) is the quantile function of the standard Normal distribution, \(e \approx 2.718\), and \(\gamma = 0.5772\) is the Euler–Mascheroni constant.

                                                                                    The term \(\Phi^{-1}(1 - 1/N)\) grows roughly like \(\sqrt{2\ln N}\). The advantage given by luck thus grows slowly with the number of trials, but without limit: ten times more trials always add something to the best apparent result.

                                                                                    For 10 years, with \(\sigma = 0.316\) and \(N = 100\), the formula gives \(0.316\,[(1 - \gamma)\,2.326 + \gamma\,2.680] = 0.80\). An annual Sharpe ratio of 0.80 therefore appears, on average, by luck alone if the best of 100 strategies is chosen. In a simulation, the best of 10, 100 and 1,000 strategies without skill has an average Sharpe ratio of 0.48, 0.79 and 1.02.

                                                                                    In brief

                                                                                    • Expected maximum of \(N\) independent zero-skill trials Bailey & López de Prado (2014)
                                                                                      \[ \mathrm{E}[\max] \approx \sigma\Big[(1 - \gamma)\,\Phi^{-1}\big(1 - \tfrac1N\big) + \gamma\,\Phi^{-1}\big(1 - \tfrac{1}{Ne}\big)\Big] \]
                                                                                      • \(\sigma\): the standard deviation of the Sharpe ratios across trials (\(1/\sqrt{Y}\) above); \(N\): the number of trials
                                                                                      • \(\Phi^{-1}\): the quantile function of the standard Normal distribution; \(e \approx 2.718\); \(\gamma = 0.5772\): the Euler–Mascheroni constant

                                                                                              The Deflated Sharpe Ratio (1/2)

                                                                                              Slide 70 in the PDF

                                                                                              The deflated Sharpe ratio (DSR) of Bailey & López de Prado (2014) is the probability that the true Sharpe ratio exceeds what the best of \(N\) trials would show by luck:

                                                                                              \[\mathrm{DSR} = \Phi\Big(\frac{(\widehat{SR} - SR_0)\sqrt{T - 1}}{\sqrt{1 - \hat\gamma_3\widehat{SR} + \frac{\hat\gamma_4 - 1}{4}\widehat{SR}^2}}\Big).\]
                                                                                              (13.11)

                                                                                              Here \(\widehat{SR}\) is the daily Sharpe ratio of the chosen strategy, \(T\) is the number of days, and \(SR_0\) is the expected maximum from (13.10), computed with \(\sigma\) equal to the standard deviation of the \(N\) Sharpe ratios tried. \(\hat\gamma_3\) is the skewness and \(\hat\gamma_4\) the kurtosis of daily returns, with value 3 for the Normal distribution; \(\Phi\) is the standard Normal distribution function.

                                                                                              The DSR is a probability in the interval \((0, 1)\). A value above 0.95 indicates a Sharpe ratio that is significant after correcting for the \(N\) trials. The denominator measures the precision of the estimate \(\widehat{SR}\): negative skewness and heavy tails, common in financial strategies, enlarge it and reduce the DSR, because a Sharpe ratio estimated from such returns is less precise.

                                                                                              In brief

                                                                                              • Bailey & López de Prado (2014): the probability that the true Sharpe ratio exceeds what the best of \(N\) trials would show by luck
                                                                                                \[ \mathrm{DSR} = \Phi\Big(\dfrac{(\widehat{SR} - SR_0)\sqrt{T - 1}}{\sqrt{1 - \hat\gamma_3\widehat{SR} + \frac{\hat\gamma_4 - 1}{4}\widehat{SR}^2}}\Big) \]
                                                                                              • Notation
                                                                                                • \(\widehat{SR}\): the per-day Sharpe ratio of the chosen strategy; \(T\): the number of days
                                                                                                • \(SR_0\): the expected maximum of \(N\) zero-skill trials (above), with \(\sigma\) = the standard deviation of the \(N\) Sharpe ratios tried
                                                                                                • \(\hat\gamma_3\): the skewness, \(\hat\gamma_4\): the kurtosis of the daily returns (3 for the Normal distribution); \(\Phi\): the standard Normal distribution function

                                                                                              The Deflated Sharpe Ratio (2/2)

                                                                                              Slide 71 in the PDF

                                                                                              The step-by-step calculation uses the best MA rule, with \(N = 30\) rules tried and \(T = 3{,}769\) days. The daily Sharpe ratio is \(\widehat{SR} = 0.0416\), that is \(0.0416 \times \sqrt{252} = 0.66\) per year; the skewness of daily returns is \(\hat\gamma_3 = -0.44\) and the kurtosis \(\hat\gamma_4 = 12.0\).

                                                                                              The luck threshold is \(SR_0 = 0.0105\,[(1 - \gamma)\,1.834 + \gamma\,2.249] = 0.0219\) per day, that is 0.35 per year. The argument of \(\Phi\) in (13.11) is \(z = 1.20\), so DSR \(= \Phi(1.20) = 0.88\). Without the correction for \(N\), with \(SR_0 = 0\), we obtain the probabilistic Sharpe ratio (PSR), equal to 0.994.

                                                                                              Taken alone, the rule looks significant: the PSR is well above 0.95. After correcting for the 30 trials, the DSR falls to 0.88, below the 0.95 threshold, and the Sharpe ratio is no longer significant. The result in 2015–2026, 22nd of 30, confirms this conclusion.

                                                                                              Evaluation in the Paper

                                                                                              Slide 73 in the PDF

                                                                                              The authors use

                                                                                              \[R^2_{OOS} = 1 - \frac{\sum_{(i,t)}(r_{i,t+1} - \hat r_{i,t+1})^2}{\sum_{(i,t)} r_{i,t+1}^2},\]

                                                                                              where \(r_{i,t+1}\) is the return of stock \(i\) in month \(t + 1\), \(\hat r_{i,t+1}\) is its forecast, and the sums run over all stock–month pairs in the test period. The benchmark is a zero forecast, not the historical mean as in (13.6). The authors show that the historical mean of an individual stock is so noisy that it is easy to beat, so the zero benchmark is more demanding.

                                                                                              The models are compared with Diebold–Mariano tests between all pairs, adapted to a panel of stocks: the same tool as in Section 7. A Bonferroni correction is applied for the 12 comparisons, in which the significance level is divided by the number of comparisons, a simple form of protection against the data snooping of Section 10.

                                                                                              Economic value is measured through a portfolio. Each month, stocks are sorted into deciles by their forecast; the top decile is bought and the bottom decile sold. The portfolios are value-weighted, and for each one the Sharpe ratio, the drawdown and the turnover, the share of the portfolio traded monthly, are reported.

                                                                                              In brief

                                                                                              • \(R^2_{OOS} = 1 - \sum_{(i,t)}(r_{i,t+1} - \hat r_{i,t+1})^2 / \sum_{(i,t)} r_{i,t+1}^2\): the benchmark is a forecast of zero, not the historical mean
                                                                                                • \(r_{i,t+1}\): the return of stock \(i\) in month \(t + 1\); \(\hat r_{i,t+1}\): its forecast; the sums run over all stock-months of the test period
                                                                                                • the authors note that the historical mean of a stock is so noisy that it is easy to beat: a zero benchmark is harder
                                                                                              • Diebold–Mariano tests between all pairs of models, with a Bonferroni correction for the 12 comparisons
                                                                                                • the same tool as Section 7, applied to a panel of stocks
                                                                                              • Economic value: each month, sort stocks into deciles by forecast; buy the top decile, sell the bottom one
                                                                                                • value-weighted portfolios; Sharpe ratio, drawdown and turnover (the share of the portfolio traded each month)

                                                                                                Interpretation and Limits

                                                                                                Slide 75 in the PDF

                                                                                                The paper finds that nonlinear models, trees and networks, are superior to linear ones, because they capture interactions between firm characteristics. All methods agree on the main signals: price trends, that is momentum and short-term reversal, liquidity and volatility. Shallow networks are enough: 3–4 layers do better than 5, and deep learning does not help for monthly returns.

                                                                                                A monthly \(R^2_{OOS}\) below 1% is, in this context, an important result. The comparison with Section 8 is instructive: there, no model beat the rule "always up" for the sign of a single index on the next day.

                                                                                                The results also have limits. Portfolios built on the networks turn over more than 100% of their value each month, and transaction costs reduce the gains. The evidence comes from a cross-section of thousands of stocks, where the edge is obtained by ranking stocks relative to each other; it says little about timing the market for a single index.

                                                                                                In brief

                                                                                                • What the paper shows
                                                                                                  • nonlinear models (trees, networks) beat linear ones because they capture interactions between characteristics
                                                                                                  • all methods agree on the main signals: price trends (momentum, short-term reversal), liquidity, volatility
                                                                                                  • shallow networks are enough: 3–4 layers beat 5; “deep” learning does not help with monthly returns
                                                                                                • What to keep in mind
                                                                                                  • an \(R^2_{OOS}\) below 1% per month is a large result here: compare with the sign forecasts of Section 8
                                                                                                  • neural portfolios turn over more than 100% of their value each month: trading costs reduce the gains
                                                                                                  • the evidence is a cross-section of thousands of stocks; it says little about timing one index

                                                                                                Key Takeaways

                                                                                                Slide 80 in the PDF

                                                                                                Machine learning is judged by its error on new data, and the bias–variance trade-off sets how flexible a model should be. Time series need walk-forward validation with purging: random folds on overlapping targets simulate false predictability, with an \(R^2\) above 30% even for a random walk.

                                                                                                Ridge and the lasso shrink coefficients, a random forest averages trees, boosting adds small trees in sequence, and neural networks learn nonlinear features. For volatility, HAR is hard to beat, and the gains are a few percent; for the sign of returns, no model gains over the rule "always up".

                                                                                                For VaR 1%, linear quantile regression works, while flexible quantile models lack data in the tail. The number of trials is reported, the Sharpe ratio is deflated, and every model is compared with a simple benchmark.
