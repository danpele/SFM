Chapter 10 measured the risk of a single position through VaR (value at risk) 1% and ES (expected shortfall) 2.5%. For a bank, this measure answers how much the bank itself can lose. Supervisors have a different concern: when does the loss of one bank become a loss of the whole financial system, with loans no longer granted and payments no longer made? This is systemic risk, and the chapter shows how it is measured from market prices.

The data of the chapter are the daily prices of 13 listed banks, six American, five European and two Romanian, from 2005 to September 2026. Four crises are visible in them: the failure of Lehman Brothers in 2008, the euro-area crisis of 2011–2012, March 2020 and March 2023. The portfolio of US banks lost 82% of its value by March 2009, and the average correlation among them rose from 0.59 in calm years to 0.94 in March 2020: in crises, several banks behave almost like a single one.

The chapter then builds the measures used by researchers and central banks: tail dependence, CoVaR and ΔCoVaR (conditional value at risk and the contribution to it), MES (marginal expected shortfall) and SRISK (systemic risk, the capital shortfall in a crisis), Granger-causality networks and Diebold–Yilmaz connectedness. The main result is that ranking banks by their own VaR 1% differs from ranking them by their contribution to the risk of the system. The chapter ends with the macroprudential tools of Basel III and the institutions that apply them in the European Union (EU) and in Romania.

What Is Systemic Risk?

The section defines systemic risk and explains why VaR computed separately for each bank does not capture it. It then presents the three channels through which a local problem becomes a problem of the system: direct contagion, common exposures with fire sales, and deposit withdrawals. It ends with the problem of banks too big to be allowed to fail and with a table of the measures used in the rest of the chapter.

A Definition

Slide 6 in the PDF

In 2009, at the request of the G-20, the IMF (International Monetary Fund), the BIS (Bank for International Settlements) and the FSB (Financial Stability Board) defined systemic risk as the risk that the financial system as a whole stops performing its functions, namely lending, payments and trading, with large costs for the real economy (IMF, BIS & FSB, 2009). The emphasis is on functions, not institutions: the failure of a small bank whose services are taken over immediately by other banks is not a systemic event.

The same report proposes three criteria for judging whether an institution is systemically important. Size shows what share of the system's services the institution provides. Substitutability shows whether other institutions can quickly take over its activity, for example the settlement of payments or lending to a particular type of client. Interconnectedness shows how many other institutions its failure would drag down, through direct exposures or through lost confidence.

The definition yields the idea that organises the chapter: systemic risk is a property of the system, not of a single balance sheet. A bank can look safe when viewed on its own and still be dangerous for the system, because its losses arrive exactly when the other banks also lose. The measures of the following sections all try to capture this link between the bank and the system.

In brief

  • Systemic risk: the risk that the financial system as a whole stops working (lending, payments, trading), with large costs for the real economy IMF, BIS & FSB (2009)
    • the definition of the IMF (International Monetary Fund), the BIS and the FSB (Financial Stability Board) for the G-20 in 2009
  • Three criteria for a systemically important institution IMF, BIS & FSB (2009)
    • size: how much of the system's services it provides
    • substitutability: whether others can take over its activity quickly
    • interconnectedness: how many others it would drag down
  • Key point: systemic risk is a property of the system, not of a single balance sheet

Why VaR of Each Bank Is Not Enough

Slide 7 in the PDF

The VaR 1% of a bank, defined in Chapter 10, is the loss of this bank exceeded with probability 1%. The measure looks only at the distribution of the bank's returns and says nothing about what happens to the other banks on the days when this bank is in trouble. Two banks with the same VaR 1% can have entirely different effects on the system: one loses on days when the rest of the market is calm, the other loses together with everyone else.

Regulation built only on individual VaR can even amplify risk, through a fallacy of composition. If every bank sells risky assets to reduce its own VaR, the simultaneous sales push down the prices of those assets, and the mark-to-market losses raise the risk of all. What is prudent for one bank taken separately becomes destabilising when all banks do the same. Likewise, a small bank with a large VaR can matter less for the system than a large, interconnected bank with a moderate VaR.

What is needed, therefore, are conditional measures that link the tail of the bank to the tail of the system. There are two directions. CoVaR measures the tail of the system given that the bank is in distress (Adrian & Brunnermeier, 2016). MES and SRISK measure the loss of the bank given that the market crashes; the first was proposed by Acharya, Pedersen, Philippon & Richardson (2017) and the second by Brownlees & Engle (2017). The chapter estimates both on the same 13 banks, and the difference in direction will turn out to matter.

In brief

  • VaR 1% of a bank (Chapter 10): the loss of this bank exceeded with probability 1%
    • it ignores what happens to the others when this bank is in trouble
  • Fallacy of composition: if every bank sells risky assets to cut its own VaR, prices fall for all and the risk of all rises
    • a small bank with a high VaR can matter less than a large, connected bank with a moderate VaR
  • We need measures of the tail of the system given the institution, or of the institution given the tail of the system
    • CoVaR: the system given that the bank is in distress Adrian & Brunnermeier (2016)
    • MES and SRISK: the bank given that the market crashes Acharya, Pedersen, Philippon & Richardson (2017); Brownlees & Engle (2017)

Channel 1: Direct Contagion

Slide 8 in the PDF

Contagion means that the failure of one institution causes losses or failures at other institutions. The most direct channel runs through the exposures between banks: interbank loans, derivative contracts in which the failed bank is the counterparty, deposits held at other banks. The banking system is thus a network of balance sheets, and a loss that wipes out the capital of one bank is passed on to its creditors, then to their creditors (Allen & Gale, 2000).

The structure of the network matters. Allen & Gale (2000) and Haldane & May (2011) show that many links have a double effect. A small shock is split among many counterparties, each loses a little, and the system absorbs it. A large shock, however, spreads along the same links to almost all institutions. The property is summarised by the phrase "robust yet fragile": the same system withstands small shocks well and gives way suddenly under large ones.

This is the origin of the idea of an institution too interconnected to fail. The problem is not its size but the number of counterparties that its failure would hit at the same time. For such an institution, the authorities have a reason to intervene even if its balance sheet is not very large, and this expectation changes its behaviour before the crisis.

In brief

  • Contagion: the failure of one institution causes losses or failures at others
    • direct exposures: interbank loans, derivatives, deposits held at other banks
    • a network of balance sheets: a loss travels along the links Allen & Gale (2000)
  • Network structure matters Allen & Gale (2000); Haldane & May (2011)
    • many links spread small shocks thin, so the system absorbs them
    • the same links transmit large shocks everywhere: “robust yet fragile”
  • Too interconnected to fail: an institution whose failure would hit many counterparties at once

Channel 2: Common Exposures and Fire Sales

Slide 9 in the PDF

The second channel needs no direct link between banks. Common exposures arise when banks hold the same assets, for example mortgages or the bonds of the same government. A single shock to those assets hits all of them on the same day. The correlation between banks then comes from their assets, not from contracts between them.

Fire sales turn the initial loss into a spiral. A bank that must sell quickly, to cover losses or withdrawals of funding, sells below fundamental value, because the natural buyers of the asset are in trouble themselves (Shleifer & Vishny, 2011). The lower price is a loss for all holders of the asset who mark their positions to market. Brunnermeier & Pedersen (2009) describe the liquidity spiral: losses lead to higher margins demanded by creditors, higher margins lead to new sales, and the sales push prices down again.

Leverage amplifies all these mechanisms. A bank whose assets are 20 times its equity has capital equal to 5% of assets. A 5% fall in the value of the assets therefore wipes out its capital. The higher the leverage, the smaller the price fall that triggers fire sales, and this link returns in the SRISK formula of section 5.

In brief

  • Common exposures: banks hold the same assets (mortgages, government bonds), so one shock hits all of them at once
    • no link between the banks is needed: the correlation comes from the assets
  • Fire sales: a bank that must sell quickly sells below the fundamental value Shleifer & Vishny (2011)
    • the lower price is a loss for every holder of the asset (marked to market)
    • liquidity spiral: losses \(\to\) higher margins \(\to\) more sales \(\to\) lower prices Brunnermeier & Pedersen (2009)
  • Leverage amplifies everything: with assets 20 times equity, a 5% fall in asset value wipes out the equity

Channel 3: Bank Runs and Information Contagion

Slide 10 in the PDF

A bank run occurs when depositors withdraw their money at the same time, for fear that others will withdraw first (Diamond & Dybvig, 1983). The mechanism comes from the structure of a bank's balance sheet: the bank holds long-term loans that are hard to sell and owes short-term deposits that can be claimed at any moment. It cannot pay all depositors at once, even if its assets are worth more than its liabilities.

The Diamond–Dybvig model has two equilibria. If every depositor believes the others will keep their money, nobody has a reason to withdraw. If every depositor believes the others will withdraw, withdrawing becomes rational for all, and the bank, solvent at the start, ends up selling assets at a loss. Deposit insurance removes the motive to withdraw: an insured depositor gets the money back in any case, so there is no reason to run to the bank.

Information contagion extends this mechanism to other banks. The problems of one bank make investors and depositors doubt the banks that resemble it, even without any direct exposure. The photograph on the slide shows depositors queuing outside a Northern Rock branch in September 2007: it was the first run on a British bank in a century.

In brief

  • Bank run: depositors withdraw at once because they fear that others will withdraw first Diamond & Dybvig (1983)
    • a bank holds long, illiquid loans and short, liquid deposits: it cannot pay everyone at once
    • a run can hit a solvent bank; deposit insurance removes the reason to run
  • Information contagion: trouble at one bank makes investors doubt similar banks
    • Northern Rock (United Kingdom), September 2007: the first run on a British bank in over a century

Too Big to Fail

Slide 11 in the PDF

A bank is too big to fail when its failure would hurt the economy so much that the government prefers to rescue it. If investors anticipate the rescue, the bank's creditors bear less risk than they otherwise would and ask for a lower interest rate. The bank thus receives an implicit subsidy, paid by taxpayers through the guarantee that the government provides.

The subsidy has a predictable consequence, moral hazard. Gains from risky positions stay with shareholders, while part of the losses is shared with taxpayers, so the bank has a reason to take more risk. The problem reinforces itself: the larger the bank grows, the more credible the implicit guarantee and the cheaper its funding.

After 2008, the authorities responded in two ways. Global systemically important banks, G-SIBs, are identified in a list published every year and must hold additional capital, the more so the higher their importance score (BCBS, 2018). Resolution plans must show how a bank in distress can be wound down without taxpayers' money. Measures from market data, such as CoVaR and SRISK, complement these rules: they allow banks to be ranked by systemic importance without waiting for a failure.

In brief

  • Too big to fail: a bank whose failure would damage the economy so much that the state rescues it
    • the expected rescue lowers its funding cost: an implicit subsidy
    • moral hazard: the bank takes more risk because losses are shared with taxpayers
  • The regulatory answer after 2008 BCBS (2018)
    • G-SIBs (global systemically important banks): a list published every year, with extra capital for each bank
    • resolution plans: a failing bank must be wound down without taxpayers' money
  • Market measures (CoVaR, SRISK) let us rank banks without waiting for a failure

Measures of This Chapter at a Glance

Slide 12 in the PDF

The table on the slide links each measure to the question it answers. Correlation and tail dependence ask whether banks and markets crash together and are estimated from ranks, that is, from pseudo-observations, and from a t copula. CoVaR and ΔCoVaR ask how much worse the system is when a bank is in distress and are estimated by quantile regression. MES asks how much the bank loses on the market's worst days and is a conditional average.

SRISK asks how much capital the bank would miss in a crisis and combines MES with leverage. Networks ask who transmits shocks to whom, through Granger-causality tests and through the decomposition of forecast error variances. Each measure looks at the same problem from a different angle, and sections 3–6 estimate them on the same data.

All these measures use only market prices, so they are available daily for any listed bank, without waiting for accounting reports. Their weakness is shared: prices can be calm while risk builds up, as happened in 2006–2007, when low volatility made all return-based measures show low risk just before the largest crisis in the sample.

Section summary

Systemic risk is the risk that the financial system stops performing its functions. It arises through direct contagion, through common exposures and fire sales amplified by leverage, and through deposit runs, and individual VaR does not see it. The measures of the chapter link the tail of the bank to the tail of the system, in both directions of conditioning. The next section shows what these mechanisms look like in the data of the four crises.

    Interpretation of the Bank Portfolios

    Slide 15 in the PDF

    In 2008–2009, US and European banks lost about four fifths of their value. The trough did not come on the day of the Lehman failure but in March 2009, almost six months later. A systemic crisis is a process that unfolds over months: losses are discovered gradually, funding is withdrawn, fire sales continue. A risk measure that looks at a single bad day captures only the start of this process.

    The European banks stayed below their 2005 level for most of the period. After 2009 came the sovereign debt crisis in the euro area and a long period of low interest rates. The Romanian banks had their largest fall in the euro-area crisis of 2011–2012, not in 2008: the parent banks of many banks in Romania are in the euro area, and doubts about them spread to the local markets.

    The episodes of March 2020 and March 2023 look different. The falls were steep but short, because central banks and governments reacted within days. The difference from 2008 lies both in the larger capital of the banks and in the speed of public intervention.

    In brief

    • 2008–2009: US and European banks lost about four fifths of their value; the trough came in March 2009, six months after Lehman
      • a systemic crisis is a process that lasts months, not a single bad day
    • European banks stayed below their 2005 level for most of the period: the euro-area crisis and low interest rates
    • Romanian banks: their worst fall came in the euro-area crisis of 2011–2012 (the parent banks of many Romanian banks are in the euro area)
    • March 2020 and March 2023: sharp falls, but short; central banks and governments reacted within days

    2008: Lehman Brothers

    Slide 16 in the PDF

    On 15 September 2008, Lehman Brothers filed for bankruptcy, the largest bankruptcy in US history, with assets of over USD 600 billion. The next day, the Federal Reserve rescued AIG (American International Group), an insurer that had sold many banks protection against default on mortgage debt. On the same day, the value of a unit of a money market fund holding Lehman paper fell below one dollar, and investors began to pull their money out of such funds, an important source of short-term funding for banks.

    The episode illustrates all three channels of the previous section at once. Direct exposures arose through derivative contracts with Lehman, whose outcome was uncertain. Common exposures arose through US mortgages held by banks on both sides of the Atlantic. The withdrawal of short-term funding worked like a bank run outside classic deposits.

    From the end of August to the trough in November 2008, the US bank portfolio fell by 59%, the European one by 54%, and the S&P 500 by 41%. Banks lost more than the market as a whole, a sign that they were the source of the crisis, not just its victims.

    In brief

    • 15 September 2008: Lehman Brothers files for bankruptcy, the largest bankruptcy in US history (assets above 600 billion USD)
      • 16 September: AIG, an insurer that had sold protection on mortgage debt to many banks, is rescued by the Federal Reserve
      • the same day, a money market fund holding Lehman debt “breaks the buck” and investors run from such funds
    • All three channels at once
      • direct exposures (derivatives with Lehman), common exposures (mortgages), a run on short-term funding
    • From the end of August to the trough in November: US banks \(-59\%\), European banks \(-54\%\), S&P 500 \(-41\%\)

    2010–2012: The Euro-Area Crisis

    Slide 17 in the PDF

    The euro-area crisis started in 2010 in Greece and spread to Ireland, Portugal, Spain and Italy, through doubts about the sustainability of public debt. The central mechanism was the bank–sovereign loop. Banks hold many bonds of their own government, and the government implicitly guarantees the banks. A fall in bond prices weakens the banks' capital; rescuing the banks raises government debt and lowers bond prices again.

    The spiral was stopped on 26 July 2012 by a statement. Mario Draghi, President of the ECB (European Central Bank), said in London that the European Central Bank was ready to do whatever it takes to preserve the euro (Draghi, 2012). The statement was not accompanied at that moment by bond purchases; what mattered was the change in investors' expectations about what the central bank would do in the event of a new fall.

    In the window July 2011 – September 2012, at the weakest point, the European bank portfolio had lost 41%, the US one 37%, and the Romanian one 34%. The fact that US banks also fell sharply shows how closely the global banking system was tied to the fate of the euro area.

    In brief

    • Greece (2010), then Ireland, Portugal, Spain and Italy: doubts about government debt
      • the bank–sovereign loop: banks hold their own government's bonds, and the government stands behind its banks
      • a fall in bond prices weakens the banks; rescuing the banks weakens the state
    • 26 July 2012: Mario Draghi, President of the ECB (European Central Bank): “the ECB is ready to do whatever it takes to preserve the euro” Draghi (2012)
      • a promise, not a purchase, stopped the spiral: the role of expectations

    March 2023: SVB

    Slide 19 in the PDF

    SVB had an unusual business model. Its deposits came mostly from technology firms, and most of them exceeded the insured limit. The bank invested them largely in long-term bonds. The rise in interest rates in 2022 lowered the price of these bonds and produced large unrealised losses, which would become real if the bank had to sell.

              Interpretation: When Diversification Fails

              Slide 23 in the PDF

              When the average correlation approaches 1, a portfolio of several banks behaves almost like a single bank. The benefit of diversification, which rests on the losses of one bank being offset by the gains of another, disappears exactly in the period when it would be most useful.               Contagion or Interdependence? Forbes and Rigobon (1/2)

              Slide 24 in the PDF

              Forbes & Rigobon (2002) start from a simple model, \(y = \beta x + e\), in which \(x\) is the return of the source market, \(y\) the return of the other market, \(\beta\) the strength of the link, and \(e\) a shock to \(y\) unrelated to \(x\). The correlation between \(x\) and \(y\) is \(\rho = \beta\sigma_x/\sqrt{\beta^2\sigma_x^2 + \sigma_e^2}\). If the variance of \(x\) rises while \(\beta\) and \(\sigma_e\) stay the same, the correlation rises automatically, because the common part of \(y\) becomes larger relative to its own noise.

              A higher correlation in a crisis can therefore be the same link, seen in a more volatile period. Forbes and Rigobon call this case interdependence. Contagion, in their sense, means that the link itself, \(\beta\), becomes stronger in the crisis. To separate the two cases, the crisis correlation is recomputed at the volatility of the calm period:

              \[\rho^* = \frac{\rho}{\sqrt{1 + \delta(1 - \rho^2)}}, \qquad \delta = \frac{\sigma^2_{\text{crisis}}}{\sigma^2_{\text{calm}}} - 1\]
              (15.1)

              In (15.1), \(\rho\) is the correlation measured in the crisis, \(\sigma^2\) is the variance of the source market in each period, and \(\delta\) is the relative increase in this variance. The corrected correlation \(\rho^*\) is compared with the correlation of the calm period, \(\rho_{\text{calm}}\): if \(\rho^* > \rho_{\text{calm}}\), the link has strengthened and the data point to contagion; otherwise, the rise in correlation is explained by volatility.

              In brief

              • If \(y = \beta x + e\) with fixed \(\beta\), the correlation of \(x\) and \(y\) rises automatically when the variance of \(x\) rises Forbes & Rigobon (2002)
                • \(x\): the return of the source market; \(y\): the return of the other market; \(\beta\): the strength of the link; \(e\): a shock of \(y\) unrelated to \(x\)
                • a higher correlation in a crisis may be the same link seen in a more volatile period: interdependence
                • contagion: the link itself becomes stronger
              • Corrected correlation:
                \[ \rho^* = \frac{\rho}{\sqrt{1 + \delta(1 - \rho^2)}}, \qquad \delta = \frac{\sigma^2_{\text{crisis}}}{\sigma^2_{\text{calm}}} - 1 \]
              • Notation
                • \(\rho\): the correlation measured in the crisis; \(\sigma^2\): the variance of the source market in each period
                • \(\delta\): the relative rise of that variance; \(\rho^*\): the crisis correlation that the calm-period volatility would give
                • compare \(\rho^*\) with \(\rho_{\text{calm}}\): \(\rho^* > \rho_{\text{calm}}\) points to contagion

              Tail Dependence (Chapter 10) (1/2)

              Slide 26 in the PDF

              Correlation describes the whole joint distribution, including ordinary days. For systemic risk, however, what matters are the bad days, and these are better studied through pseudo-observations. For each series, the return of a day is replaced by its rank among the \(n\) returns of the series, divided by \(n + 1\): \(U = \text{rank}/(n + 1)\). The values of \(U\) lie in \((0, 1)\), and \(U \le 0.05\) means that the day is one of the worst 5% of the days of the series. The marginal distributions no longer matter: only the dependence structure remains, that is, the copula of Chapter 10.

              Lower tail dependence at level \(q\) is the conditional probability

              \[\lambda(q) = P(U_1 \le q \mid U_2 \le q),\]
              (15.2)

              where \(U_1\) is the pseudo-observation of the bank and \(U_2\) that of the market. \(\lambda(q)\) is the probability that the bank is in its worst \(q\) of days, given that the market is in its worst \(q\) of days. If the bank and the market were independent, \(\lambda(q) = q\); if they moved perfectly together, \(\lambda(q) = 1\).

              The limit \(\lambda_L = \lim_{q \to 0}\lambda(q)\) is the tail dependence coefficient. It tells whether extreme crashes of the two series tend to occur together even at the farthest levels of the tail. For systemic risk, this measure is more relevant than correlation: it tells how often a bank and the market are together in their worst days.

              In brief

              • Pseudo-observations: \(U = \text{rank}/(n + 1)\), the rank of a return among the \(n\) returns of its series, scaled to \((0, 1)\)
                • \(U \le 0.05\): one of the worst 5% of days of that series; the marginal distributions no longer matter
              • Lower tail dependence at level \(q\): \(\lambda(q) = P(U_1 \le q \mid U_2 \le q)\)
                • \(U_1\): the bank; \(U_2\): the market; \(\lambda(q)\): the probability that the bank is in its worst \(q\) days, given that the market is in its worst \(q\) days
                • under independence \(\lambda(q) = q\); the limit \(\lambda_L = \lim_{q \to 0}\lambda(q)\) is the tail-dependence coefficient
              • For systemic risk: tail dependence measures how often a bank and the market are in their worst days together

              Tail Dependence (Chapter 10) (2/2)

              Slide 27 in the PDF

              The limit \(\lambda_L\) cannot be estimated directly from the data, because at very small \(q\) too few days remain. It is obtained from a parametric copula. The Gaussian copula has \(\lambda_L = 0\) for any correlation below 1: extreme crashes become, in the limit, independent. The t copula, by contrast, has a positive tail dependence coefficient (Demarta & McNeil, 2005):

              \[\lambda_L = 2\,t_{\nu+1}\Big(-\sqrt{(\nu + 1)(1 - \rho)/(1 + \rho)}\Big) > 0.\]
              (15.3)

              In (15.3), \(t_{\nu+1}\) is the distribution function of the Student-t distribution with \(\nu + 1\) degrees of freedom, \(\rho\) is the correlation parameter of the copula, and \(\nu\) its degrees of freedom. The coefficient rises when \(\rho\) rises and when \(\nu\) falls: a small \(\nu\) means strong joint crashes, and as \(\nu \to \infty\) the t copula becomes Gaussian and \(\lambda_L \to 0\).

              Estimation has two steps. The parameter \(\rho\) is obtained from Kendall's \(\tau\), a rank correlation, through \(\rho = \sin(\pi\tau/2)\); \(\tau = (C - D)/\binom{n}{2}\), where \(C\) and \(D\) are the numbers of concordant and discordant pairs of days, that is, pairs in which the two series move in the same direction or in opposite directions. The degrees of freedom \(\nu\) are then estimated by maximum likelihood, with \(\rho\) held fixed.

              In brief

              • The t copula (Chapter 10) has a positive tail-dependence coefficient Demarta & McNeil (2005); the Gaussian copula has \(\lambda_L = 0\)
                \[ \lambda_L = 2\,t_{\nu+1}\Big(-\sqrt{(\nu + 1)(1 - \rho)/(1 + \rho)}\Big) > 0 \]
              • Notation
                • \(t_{\nu+1}\): the distribution function of a Student-t with \(\nu + 1\) degrees of freedom
                • \(\rho\): the correlation parameter of the copula; \(\nu\): its degrees of freedom; a small \(\nu\) gives strong joint crashes
              • Estimation
                • \(\rho = \sin(\pi\tau/2)\) from Kendall's \(\tau\), a rank correlation; \(\nu\) by maximum likelihood
                • \(\tau = (C - D)/\binom{n}{2}\); \(C\), \(D\): the numbers of concordant and discordant pairs of days (both series move in the same or in opposite directions)

                  From VaR to CoVaR

                  Slide 30 in the PDF

                  Let \(X^i\) be the return of institution \(i\) and \(X^{sys}\) the return of the system. For the level \(\alpha\), the tail probability, \(q_\alpha(X^i)\) is the \(\alpha\)-quantile of \(X^i\), and \(\mathrm{VaR}^i_\alpha = -q_\alpha(X^i)\) is the loss of \(i\) exceeded with probability \(\alpha\), as in Chapter 10. Adrian & Brunnermeier (2016) define CoVaR as the VaR of the system on a day when the institution is exactly at its own VaR:

                  \[\mathrm{CoVaR}^{sys|i}_\alpha = -q_\alpha\big(X^{sys} \mid X^i = q_\alpha(X^i)\big).\]
                  (15.4)

                  In (15.4), \(q_\alpha(X^{sys} \mid \cdot)\) is the \(\alpha\)-quantile of the system's return, conditional on the event after the bar. For \(\alpha = 1\%\), the measure is the VaR 1% of the system on a day when institution \(i\) is at its own VaR 1%, and it is called "CoVaR 1%".

                  CoVaR alone mixes two things: the risk of the system in general and the effect of the institution. The institution's contribution is isolated by the difference between the distress state and an ordinary day for the institution:

                  \[\Delta\mathrm{CoVaR}^{sys|i}_\alpha = \mathrm{CoVaR}^{sys|X^i = q_\alpha}_\alpha - \mathrm{CoVaR}^{sys|X^i = q_{50\%}}_\alpha,\]
                  (15.5)

                  where \(q_{50\%}\) is the median of \(X^i\). ΔCoVaR tells how much the VaR of the system rises when the institution moves from an ordinary day to a distress day. The reference is the median, not the mean, because the median describes a typical day and is not affected by the tails.

                  In brief

                  • \(X^i\): return of institution \(i\); \(X^{sys}\): return of the system; level \(\alpha\) = probability of the tail (Chapter 10)
                    • \(q_\alpha(X^i)\): the \(\alpha\)-quantile of \(X^i\); \(\mathrm{VaR}^i_\alpha = -q_\alpha(X^i)\): the loss of \(i\) exceeded with probability \(\alpha\)
                  • CoVaR (conditional VaR of the system) Adrian & Brunnermeier (2016)
                    \begin{equation*} \mathrm{CoVaR}^{sys|i}_\alpha = -q_\alpha\big(X^{sys} \mid X^i = q_\alpha(X^i)\big) \end{equation*}
                    • \(q_\alpha(X^{sys} \mid \cdot)\): the \(\alpha\)-quantile of the system return, conditional on what follows the bar
                    • the VaR 1% of the system on a day when institution \(i\) is at its own VaR 1%: “CoVaR 1%”
                  • \(\boldsymbol{\Delta}\)CoVaR: the contribution of \(i\) to systemic risk
                    \begin{equation*} \Delta\mathrm{CoVaR}^{sys|i}_\alpha = \mathrm{CoVaR}^{sys|X^i = q_\alpha}_\alpha - \mathrm{CoVaR}^{sys|X^i = q_{50\%}}_\alpha \end{equation*}
                    • \(q_{50\%}\): the median of \(X^i\), a normal day of the institution
                    • reading: how much the VaR of the system rises when \(i\) moves from a normal day (its median) to distress

                  Quantile Regression (Chapter 13)

                  Slide 31 in the PDF

                  The estimation tool is the linear quantile regression of Koenker & Bassett (1978), met in Chapter 13 for VaR 1%. It models the conditional quantile directly, \(q_\alpha(Y \mid X = x) = a_\alpha + b_\alpha x\), with intercept \(a_\alpha\) and slope \(b_\alpha\). The coefficients are estimated by minimising the quantile loss:

                  \[\min_{a,\,b}\ \sum_t \rho_\alpha(y_t - a - bx_t), \qquad \rho_\alpha(u) = u\big(\alpha - \mathbf{1}\{u < 0\}\big).\]
                  (15.6)

                  Here \(u\) is the residual, and \(\mathbf{1}\{u < 0\}\) is 1 if the point lies below the line and 0 otherwise. A positive residual costs \(\alpha u\), a negative one costs \((1 - \alpha)|u|\). For \(\alpha = 1\%\), a point below the line costs 99 times as much as one at the same distance above it, so the minimum is reached when the line passes below about 99% of the points.

                  The idea is the same as in Chapter 13, where the explanatory variable was a measure of past volatility. Here the explanatory variable is the return of another institution on the same day. The slope \(b_\alpha\) can differ from the OLS (ordinary least squares) slope: the tail of the system's distribution can react to the institution more strongly or more weakly than its centre, and quantile regression captures exactly this difference.

                  In brief

                  • Linear quantile regression Koenker & Bassett (1978): \(q_\alpha(Y \mid X = x) = a_\alpha + b_\alpha x\)
                    • \(a_\alpha\): the intercept (constant term); \(b_\alpha\): the slope
                    • estimated by minimising \(\sum_t \rho_\alpha(y_t - a - bx_t)\), with the check loss \(\rho_\alpha(u) = u(\alpha - \mathbf{1}\{u < 0\})\)
                    • \(u\): the residual; \(\mathbf{1}\{u < 0\}\): 1 if the point is below the line, 0 otherwise
                    • for \(\alpha = 1\%\), a residual below the line costs 99 times more than one above it: the line passes below 99% of the points
                  • Same idea as VaR 1% by quantile regression in Chapter 13, now with another institution as the regressor
                    • the slope \(b_\alpha\) can differ from the OLS slope: the tail can react more strongly than the centre

                  Estimating CoVaR in Three Steps

                  Slide 32 in the PDF

                  The first step is the quantile regression of the system's return on the institution's return at level \(\alpha\): \(X^{sys}_t = a + bX^i_t + \varepsilon_t\) (Adrian & Brunnermeier, 2016). It yields the estimated intercept \(\hat a\) and the estimated slope \(\hat b\). By construction, the \(\alpha\)-quantile of the error \(\varepsilon_t\) is zero, so \(\hat a + \hat b x\) is the \(\alpha\)-quantile of the system on a day when the institution has return \(x\).

                  The second step estimates two empirical quantiles of the institution: \(\hat q_\alpha(X^i)\), the distress state, and \(\hat q_{50\%}(X^i)\), the median. The third step plugs them into the regression line:

                  \[\mathrm{CoVaR}_\alpha = -\big(\hat a + \hat b\,\hat q_\alpha(X^i)\big), \qquad \Delta\mathrm{CoVaR}_\alpha = \hat b\,\big(\hat q_{50\%}(X^i) - \hat q_\alpha(X^i)\big).\]
                  (15.7)

                  In the ΔCoVaR formula, the intercept drops out: only the slope and the distance between the institution's two quantiles matter. An institution has a large ΔCoVaR either because the system reacts strongly to it (large slope) or because its returns have a long tail (large distance between the median and the 1% quantile). In this chapter, the system is approximated by the region's stock index, the S&P 500, the Euro Stoxx 50 or the BET; Adrian and Brunnermeier use the portfolio of all financial institutions.

                  In brief

                  • Step 1: quantile regression of the system on the institution at level \(\alpha\): \(X^{sys}_t = a + bX^i_t + \varepsilon_t\) Adrian & Brunnermeier (2016)
                    • \(\hat a\), \(\hat b\): the estimated intercept and slope; the \(\alpha\)-quantile of the error \(\varepsilon_t\) is zero by construction
                  • Step 2: the empirical quantiles of the institution: \(\hat q_\alpha(X^i)\) (distress) and \(\hat q_{50\%}(X^i)\) (median)
                  • Step 3: plug in
                    • \(\mathrm{CoVaR}_\alpha = -(\hat a + \hat b\,\hat q_\alpha(X^i))\)
                    • \(\Delta\mathrm{CoVaR}_\alpha = \hat b\,\big(\hat q_{50\%}(X^i) - \hat q_\alpha(X^i)\big)\): only the slope and the distance between the two quantiles matter
                  • Here the system is proxied by the regional equity index (S&P 500, Euro Stoxx 50, BET); Adrian and Brunnermeier use the portfolio of all financial institutions

                                  Time-Varying \(\Delta\)CoVaR

                                  Slide 39 in the PDF

                                  The estimate so far gives a single ΔCoVaR for the whole period 2005–2026. Adrian & Brunnermeier (2016) let the quantiles depend on state variables lagged by one period, \(M_{t-1}\), a vector of variables known at the close of day \(t - 1\), such as market stress or interest rates. For the institution, two quantile regressions are estimated, \(X^i_t = c + \gamma^\top M_{t-1}\), at levels \(\alpha\) and 50%, which give the conditional quantiles \(\hat q_{\alpha,t}(X^i)\) and \(\hat q_{50\%,t}(X^i)\).

                                  For the system, the quantile regression \(X^{sys}_t = a + bX^i_t + \delta^\top M_{t-1}\) is estimated at level \(\alpha\), and the daily contribution is

                                  \[\Delta\mathrm{CoVaR}_t = \hat b\,\big(\hat q_{50\%,t}(X^i) - \hat q_{\alpha,t}(X^i)\big).\]
                                  (15.8)

                                  The coefficients \(c\), \(\gamma\), \(a\), \(b\) and \(\delta\) are the coefficients of the quantile regressions; the \(\delta\) here has nothing to do with the \(\delta\) of the Forbes–Rigobon correction. The paper uses seven state variables: the VIX, liquidity and credit spreads, interest rates, and the returns of the market and of the real-estate sector. The chapter's estimate uses a reduced set, the level of the VIX and the index return of the previous day.

                                  In (15.8), the slope \(\hat b\) is fixed; the variation over time comes only from the distance between the bank's two conditional quantiles. When the VIX rises, the bank's 1% quantile falls much more than its median, the distance between them grows, and ΔCoVaR grows with it.

                                  • Adrian and Brunnermeier let the quantiles depend on lagged state variables \(M_{t-1}\) Adrian & Brunnermeier (2016)

                                    • \(X^i_t = c + \gamma^\top M_{t-1}\) at levels \(\alpha\) and 50%: \(\hat q_{\alpha,t}(X^i)\) and \(\hat q_{50\%,t}(X^i)\)
                                    • \(X^{sys}_t = a + bX^i_t + \delta^\top M_{t-1}\) at level \(\alpha\); then \(\Delta\mathrm{CoVaR}_t = \hat b\,(\hat q_{50\%,t} - \hat q_{\alpha,t})\)

                                    Marginal Expected Shortfall

                                    Slide 42 in the PDF

                                    Acharya, Pedersen, Philippon & Richardson (2017) define the marginal expected shortfall, MES, as the average loss of institution \(i\) on the days when the market is in its tail:

                                    \[\mathrm{MES}^i_\alpha = -\mathrm{E}\big[X^i \mid X^m \le q_\alpha(X^m)\big],\]
                                    (15.9)

                                    where \(X^m\) is the market return and \(q_\alpha(X^m)\) its \(\alpha\)-quantile. The chapter uses the worst 5% of the market's days, "MES 5%". The direction of conditioning is the opposite of CoVaR: here the bank is conditioned on the market, not the market on the bank.

                                    The word "marginal" has a precise meaning. If the market is a portfolio of institutions with weights \(w_i\), its return is \(X^m = \sum_i w_i X^i\), and the ES of the market at level \(\alpha\) is \(-\mathrm{E}[X^m \mid X^m \le q_\alpha(X^m)] = \sum_i w_i\,\mathrm{MES}^i_\alpha\). MES is therefore the contribution of institution \(i\) to the ES of the system, defined in Chapter 10, and the contributions add up exactly to the total risk.

                                    Estimation is direct: the days are sorted by the market return, the worst \(\lceil n\alpha \rceil\) days are kept, the bank's returns on those days are averaged and the sign is changed. No model is needed; the price of simplicity is that the estimate rests on only 5% of the days.

                                    In brief

                                    • MES Acharya, Pedersen, Philippon & Richardson (2017): the expected loss of institution \(i\) on the days when the market is in its tail
                                      • \(\mathrm{MES}^i_\alpha = -E\big[X^i \mid X^m \le q_\alpha(X^m)\big]\); we use the worst 5% of market days: “MES 5%”
                                      • \(X^m\): the market return; \(q_\alpha(X^m)\): its \(\alpha\)-quantile; the bar: “on the days when”
                                      • the direction is the opposite of CoVaR: the bank given the market, not the market given the bank
                                    • Why “marginal”: ES of the market \(= \sum_i w_i\,\mathrm{MES}^i\) when the market is a portfolio with weights \(w_i\)
                                      • MES is the contribution of \(i\) to the ES of the system (Chapter 10)
                                    • Estimation: sort the days by the market return, take the worst \(\lceil n\alpha \rceil\) days, average the bank's returns on those days, change the sign

                                              From MES to LRMES

                                              Slide 47 in the PDF

                                              Daily MES describes one bad day, but a crisis lasts months. What matters for a bank's capital is the cumulative loss in a long crisis, for example a 40% fall of the market in six months. This loss is LRMES, the long-run MES. Acharya, Engle & Richardson (2012) propose a simple approximation:

                                              \[\mathrm{LRMES} \approx 1 - \exp(-18 \times \mathrm{MES}),\]
                                              (15.10)

                                              where MES is the bank's daily loss, as a fraction, on the days when the market falls by more than 2%. The factor 18 turns the daily loss on bad days into the loss in a six-month crisis, and the form \(1 - e^{-x}\) keeps LRMES between 0 and 1: a bank cannot lose more than its entire market value.

                                              For a bank with MES \(= 3\% = 0.03\), LRMES is \(1 - e^{-0.54} = 1 - 0.583 = 41.7\%\): the bank would lose about 41.7% of its market value in such a crisis. For the 13 banks of the chapter, LRMES lies between 40% and 65%. Brownlees & Engle (2017) estimate LRMES differently, from a dynamic model of volatility and correlation, with GARCH (generalised autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity) and dynamic conditional correlation.

                                              In brief

                                              • A crisis lasts months: we need the loss of the bank if the market falls 40% over six months: LRMES (long-run MES)
                                                • approximation of Acharya, Engle & Richardson (2012): \(\mathrm{LRMES} \approx 1 - \exp(-18 \times \mathrm{MES})\), with MES the daily loss (as a fraction) on the days when the market falls by more than 2%
                                                • the factor 18 turns the daily loss on bad days into the loss over a six-month crisis; \(1 - e^{-x}\) keeps LRMES between 0 and 1
                                              • Example: MES \(= 3\% = 0.03\): \(\mathrm{LRMES} = 1 - e^{-0.54} = 1 - 0.583 = 41.7\%\)

                                              • Our 13 banks: LRMES between 40% and 65%; Brownlees and Engle estimate it instead from a dynamic model (GARCH and dynamic correlation) Brownlees & Engle (2017)

                                              SRISK: the Capital Shortfall in a Crisis

                                              Slide 48 in the PDF

                                              A bank needs equity of at least a fraction \(k\) of its assets; Brownlees & Engle (2017) use \(k = 8\%\). Let \(D\) be the book value of debt and \(W\) the market value of equity, so that assets are approximately \(D + W\). In a crisis, equity falls to \(W_{\text{crisis}} = (1 - \mathrm{LRMES})W\), while debt stays the same. The capital shortfall is the required capital minus the remaining capital, \(k(D + W_{\text{crisis}}) - W_{\text{crisis}}\), that is,

                                              \[\mathrm{SRISK} = kD - (1 - k)(1 - \mathrm{LRMES})\,W.\]
                                              (15.11)

                                              In (15.11), \(kD\) is the capital required for the debt, and \((1 - k)(1 - \mathrm{LRMES})W\) is the equity left after the crisis, net of the capital required for itself. A positive SRISK means missing capital in a crisis; a negative SRISK means a surplus. Aggregate SRISK, the sum of the positive values over all banks, is the capital that the government might have to cover in a systemic crisis.

                                              The example on the slide uses \(D = 900\) and \(W = 100\) (billion) and LRMES \(= 41.7\%\): SRISK \(= 0.08 \times 900 - 0.92 \times (1 - 0.417) \times 100 = 72 - 53.6 = 18.4\) billion. Under normal conditions the bank has equity of 100 billion, but after a 40% fall of the market it would be 18.4 billion short of the 8% requirement.

                                              In brief

                                              • A bank needs equity of at least a fraction \(k\) of its assets; \(k = 8\%\) in Brownlees & Engle (2017)
                                                • \(D\): book value of debt; \(W\): market value of equity; assets \(\approx D + W\)
                                              • SRISK \(= k(D + W_{\text{crisis}}) - W_{\text{crisis}}\) with \(W_{\text{crisis}} = (1 - \mathrm{LRMES})W\):
                                                • \(\mathrm{SRISK} = kD - (1 - k)(1 - \mathrm{LRMES})\,W\) Brownlees & Engle (2017)
                                                • \(kD\): the capital required on the debt; \((1 - k)(1 - \mathrm{LRMES})W\): the equity left after the crisis, net of the capital required on it
                                                • SRISK \(> 0\): capital missing in a crisis; the aggregate SRISK (sum of the positive values) is what the state might have to cover
                                              • Example: \(D = 900\), \(W = 100\) (billion), LRMES \(= 41.7\%\)
                                                • SRISK \(= 0.08 \times 900 - 0.92 \times (1 - 41.7\%) \times 100 = 72 - 53.6 = 18.4\) billion

                                                        The Network View

                                                        Slide 51 in the PDF

                                                        A network consists of nodes, here the banks, and directed links of the form "a shock at \(i\) affects \(j\)". The network view puts at the centre the direct-contagion channel of section 1: it asks not only how risky a bank is, but through whom its risk is transmitted.

                                                        The real links between banks, such as interbank loans or derivative contracts, are largely confidential; only supervisors know them. Researchers therefore estimate the links from stock returns, which are public. The chapter uses two statistical tools: Granger causality, which asks whether past returns of \(i\) help forecast \(j\) (Billio, Getmansky, Lo & Pelizzon, 2012), and variance decomposition, which asks what share of the forecast error of \(j\) comes from shocks to \(i\) (Diebold & Yilmaz, 2014).

                                                        A statistical link is a comovement, not a contract. It can arise from direct exposures, from common exposures, or from investors reacting to the same news with different delays. An estimated network shows where to look, not why the link exists.

                                                        In brief

                                                        • A network: nodes (banks) and directed links (“a shock to \(i\) affects \(j\)”)
                                                          • real links (interbank loans) are mostly confidential; we estimate links from returns
                                                        • Two statistical tools
                                                          • Granger causality: do past returns of \(i\) help to forecast \(j\)? Billio, Getmansky, Lo & Pelizzon (2012)
                                                          • variance decompositions: what share of the forecast error of \(j\) comes from shocks to \(i\)? Diebold & Yilmaz (2014)
                                                        • A statistical link is a co-movement, not a contract: it shows where to look, not why

                                                        Granger Causality

                                                        Slide 52 in the PDF

                                                        Series \(x\) Granger-causes series \(y\) if past values of \(x\) improve the forecast of \(y\) beyond what the past of \(y\) provides (Granger, 1969). With one lag, the test uses the regression

                                                        \[y_t = a + b\,y_{t-1} + c\,x_{t-1} + e_t,\]
                                                        (15.13)

                                                        in which \(a\) is the constant, \(b\) the effect of \(y\)'s own past, \(c\) the effect of yesterday's value of \(x\), and \(e_t\) the error. The null hypothesis \(H_0: c = 0\) is tested with a \(t\) test at 5%; rejecting it means a link from \(x\) to \(y\). The word "causality" has here only the meaning of predictive precedence.

                                                        Billio, Getmansky, Lo & Pelizzon (2012) summarise a network by the degree of Granger causality, DGC: the share of the \(N(N - 1)\) ordered pairs of banks in which the link is significant. \(N\) is the number of banks, and the pairs are ordered because \(i \to j\) and \(j \to i\) are tested separately. The paper uses monthly returns, rolling 36-month windows, one lag and the 5% level. It additionally filters each series with a GARCH(1,1) model; with only 36 monthly observations per window, the chapter reports the unfiltered test.

                                                        Interpreting DGC requires a benchmark. If there were no links at all, about 5% of the pairs would be significant by chance, because each test has a 5% probability of rejecting a true null hypothesis. A DGC of 5% therefore indicates no network, and only values clearly above 5% indicate real links.

                                                        In brief

                                                        • \(x\) Granger-causes \(y\) if past values of \(x\) improve the forecast of \(y\) beyond the past of \(y\) Granger (1969)
                                                          • regression \(y_t = a + b\,y_{t-1} + c\,x_{t-1} + e_t\); test \(H_0: c = 0\) with a \(t\) test at 5%
                                                          • \(a\): the constant; \(b\): the own past of \(y\); \(c\): the effect of yesterday's \(x\); \(e_t\): the error; \(c \ne 0\): a link from \(x\) to \(y\)
                                                        • Degree of Granger causality (DGC) Billio, Getmansky, Lo & Pelizzon (2012): the share of the \(N(N-1)\) ordered pairs with a significant link
                                                          • \(N\): the number of banks; \(N(N - 1)\): the ordered pairs (\(i \to j\) and \(j \to i\) count separately)
                                                          • the paper: monthly returns, 36-month rolling windows, lag 1, 5% level
                                                          • the paper also filters each series with a GARCH(1,1) model; with 36 monthly observations per window we report the unfiltered test
                                                        • Under no links, about 5% of pairs are significant by chance: DGC must be compared with 5%

                                                            Diebold–Yilmaz Connectedness

                                                            Slide 55 in the PDF

                                                            The second tool starts from a VAR (vector autoregression) model estimated on the returns of the \(N\) banks:

                                                            \[x_t = c + \Phi_1x_{t-1} + \dots + \Phi_px_{t-p} + u_t,\]
                                                            (15.14)

                                                            where \(x_t\) is the vector of the \(N\) returns, \(\Phi_j\) are \(N \times N\) coefficient matrices, and \(u_t\) are the shocks. From the model, returns are forecast \(H\) days ahead and the forecast error variance is decomposed. \(d_{ij}\) is the share of the \(H\)-day forecast error variance of bank \(i\) due to shocks to bank \(j\), computed by the generalised decomposition of Pesaran & Shin (1998), which does not depend on the ordering of the variables. The shares are normalised so that each row sums to 100%.

                                                            Diebold & Yilmaz (2012) and Diebold & Yilmaz (2014) build measures of connectedness from the table \(d_{ij}\). FROM, the risk received by bank \(i\) from the others, is \(\sum_{j \ne i} d_{ij}\), the row sum without the diagonal. TO, the risk transmitted by bank \(i\) to the others, is \(\sum_{j \ne i} d_{ji}\), the column sum without the diagonal. NET \(=\) TO \(-\) FROM separates net transmitters from net receivers. Total connectedness is

                                                            \[C = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i \ne j} d_{ij},\]
                                                            (15.15)

                                                            the average share of a bank's risk that comes from the other banks. The chapter's estimate uses the daily returns of the 11 US and European banks since 2005, the VAR order chosen by the BIC (Bayesian information criterion), and a horizon of \(H = 10\) days.

                                                            In brief

                                                            • Fit a VAR (vector autoregression) to the returns of \(N\) banks; forecast \(H\) days ahead
                                                              • VAR(\(p\)): \(x_t = c + \Phi_1x_{t-1} + \dots + \Phi_px_{t-p} + u_t\); \(x_t\): the vector of the \(N\) returns; \(\Phi_j\): \(N \times N\) coefficient matrices; \(u_t\): the shocks
                                                              • \(d_{ij}\): the share of the \(H\)-day forecast error variance of bank \(i\) due to shocks to bank \(j\) (generalized decomposition, Pesaran & Shin (1998))
                                                              • each row sums to 100%
                                                            • Measures Diebold & Yilmaz (2012); Diebold & Yilmaz (2014)
                                                              • FROM others: \(\sum_{j \ne i} d_{ij}\); TO others: \(\sum_{j \ne i} d_{ji}\); NET \(=\) TO \(-\) FROM
                                                              • total connectedness: \(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i \ne j} d_{ij}\), the average share of risk that comes from the others
                                                            • Here: daily returns of 11 banks since 2005, VAR order by BIC (Bayesian information criterion), \(H = 10\) days

                                                                          Further Reading: the Financial Risk Meter

                                                                          Slide 59 in the PDF

                                                                          The FRM (Financial Risk Meter) of Mihoci, Althof, Chen & Härdle (2020) is a daily index of systemic risk built from quantile regressions with many regressors. For each bank, a lasso quantile regression, the method of Chapter 13, of its returns on the returns of all other banks and on macroeconomic variables is estimated.

                                                                          The lasso penalty, \(\lambda\), is chosen for each bank so that the model fits the tail well. When banks move together, many regressors are relevant at the same time, and the optimal penalty is large. The FRM is the average of these \(\lambda\) values over all banks: a large value signals a period in which the tails of the banks are tightly linked.

                                                                          The method has several extensions. TENET (tail-event driven network) of Härdle, Wang & Yu (2016) estimates CoVaR with many banks at once and builds from the coefficients a network of tail links. Ben Amor, Althof & Härdle (2022) apply the FRM to emerging markets, and the Quantinar video course, listed in the chapter's reading list, presents the method step by step.

                                                                          In brief

                                                                          • FRM (Financial Risk Meter) Mihoci, Althof, Chen & Härdle (2020): a daily index of systemic risk from quantile regressions with many regressors
                                                                            • for each bank: a lasso quantile regression (Chapter 13) of its returns on the returns of all other banks and on macro variables
                                                                            • the penalty \(\lambda\) needed to fit the tail is large when the banks move together: FRM = the average \(\lambda\)
                                                                          • Extensions
                                                                            • TENET (tail-event driven network) Härdle, Wang & Yu (2016): CoVaR with many banks, a network of tail links
                                                                            • FRM for emerging markets Ben Amor, Althof & Härdle (2022); the video course on Quantinar (Reading and Tools)

                                                                          Micro- and Macroprudential

                                                                          Slide 60 in the PDF

                                                                          Microprudential supervision asks whether each bank, taken separately, is safe: whether it has enough capital, a correct VaR model, good backtesting results, the topics of Chapter 10. Macroprudential supervision asks whether the system is safe, and the fallacy of composition of section 1 shows why the answers to the two questions can differ.

                                                                          The macroprudential dimension has two components. The first is over time: risk builds up in booms, when lending and leverage grow, and becomes visible only in crises. The response is to build capital buffers in good times, which can be used in bad times. The second component is across institutions: larger and more interconnected banks contribute more to systemic risk, so they need more capital.

                                                                          The measures of this chapter, CoVaR, SRISK and connectedness, are among the tools with which central banks monitor the second dimension. The forward result of Adrian and Brunnermeier and the rise of aggregate SRISK before Lehman also link them to the first.

                                                                          In brief

                                                                          • Microprudential: is each bank safe on its own? (capital, VaR, backtesting, Chapter 10)
                                                                          • Macroprudential: is the system safe? Two dimensions
                                                                            • in time: risk builds up in booms (credit growth, leverage) and shows in busts: build buffers in good times
                                                                            • across institutions: larger and more connected banks need more capital
                                                                          • The measures of this chapter (CoVaR, SRISK, connectedness) are among the tools that central banks use to monitor the second dimension

                                                                          Basel III Capital Buffers

                                                                          Slide 61 in the PDF

                                                                          Basel III, the regulatory framework adopted after the crisis, requires minimum CET1 (common equity tier 1) capital of 4.5% of risk-weighted assets (BCBS, 2011). Buffers are added on top of this minimum. The capital conservation buffer is 2.5%; a bank that falls below it can continue to operate, but its dividends and bonuses are restricted until it rebuilds the buffer.

                                                                          The countercyclical capital buffer lies between 0 and 2.5% and responds to the time dimension of systemic risk. The authority raises it when credit grows too fast and releases it in a crisis, so that the accumulated capital can absorb losses without banks cutting lending abruptly.

                                                                          For the cross-institution dimension, there are surcharges tied to systemic importance. The G-SIB surcharge lies between 1% and 3.5% and depends on a score that combines the bank's size, interconnectedness, substitutability, complexity and cross-jurisdictional activity (BCBS, 2018). In the European Union there are in addition O-SII (other systemically important institutions) buffers, for banks important at the national level, and a systemic risk buffer.

                                                                          In brief

                                                                          • Minimum CET1 (common equity tier 1) capital: 4.5% of risk-weighted assets BCBS (2011); on top of it, buffers Basel Framework, RBC30:
                                                                            • capital conservation buffer: 2.5%; below it, dividends and bonuses are restricted
                                                                            • countercyclical buffer: 0–2.5%, raised when credit grows too fast, released in a crisis
                                                                          • For systemic importance
                                                                            • G-SIB surcharge: 1% to 3.5%, by a score of size, interconnectedness, substitutability, complexity and cross-border activity BCBS (2018)
                                                                            • in the EU: O-SII (other systemically important institutions) buffers and a systemic risk buffer

                                                                          Who Does It: the ESRB and the BNR

                                                                          Slide 62 in the PDF

                                                                          In the European Union, the ESRB (European Systemic Risk Board) was set up in 2010, after the crisis, and is chaired by the President of the ECB. It monitors systemic risk in the EU and issues warnings and recommendations to national authorities. The ESRB does not set capital requirements directly; its role is to identify risks and to ask national authorities to act.

                                                                          In Romania, the macroprudential authority is the CNSM (Comitetul Național pentru Supravegherea Macroprudențială, the National Committee for Macroprudential Oversight), whose members are the BNR (Banca Națională a României, the National Bank of Romania), the ASF (Autoritatea de Supraveghere Financiară, the Financial Supervisory Authority) and the Government. On CNSM recommendations, the BNR sets the capital buffers of the banks: the countercyclical buffer, the O-SII buffers and the systemic risk buffer.

                                                                          The BNR also uses limits applied directly to borrowers: the debt service-to-income ratio, the ratio of monthly repayments to income, and the loan-to-value ratio, the ratio of the loan to the value of the collateral. These limits act on the time dimension of risk, slowing lending in booms. The BNR also publishes a financial stability report, in which it analyses the risks of the Romanian banking system.

                                                                          In brief

                                                                          • ESRB (European Systemic Risk Board), set up in 2010 after the crisis, chaired by the President of the ECB (ESRB website)
                                                                            • monitors systemic risk in the EU, issues warnings and recommendations to national authorities
                                                                          • Romania: CNSM (Comitetul Național pentru Supravegherea Macroprudențială), with the BNR, the ASF (financial supervisory authority) and the Government (CNSM website)

                                                                          The first question compares two banks: bank A has a higher VaR 1% than bank B. This does not make A more systemically important. Systemic importance depends on the bank's link with the system, measured by ΔCoVaR or MES, not on its own tail; Citigroup and Bank of America have among the largest VaR 1% and among the smallest ΔCoVaR in the sample.

                                                                          The second question gives a quantile regression at 1% with \(\hat b = 0.5\), \(\hat q_{1\%}(X^i) = -6\%\) and \(\hat q_{50\%}(X^i) = 0\). ΔCoVaR is \(0.5 \times (0 - (-6)) = 3\%\).

                                                                          The third question asks why SRISK can be negative. A bank with low leverage, below the threshold \(L^*\), keeps enough capital even after a 40% fall of the market: it has a capital surplus. The next chapter, Chapter 16, reviews the course.

                                                                          In brief

                                                                          • Question: bank A has a larger VaR 1% than bank B. Is A more systemic?
                                                                            • Answer: not necessarily: systemic importance depends on the link with the system (\(\Delta\)CoVaR, MES), not on the bank's own tail
                                                                          • Question: a quantile regression at 1% gives \(\hat b = 0.5\), \(\hat q_{1\%}(X^i) = -6\%\), \(\hat q_{50\%}(X^i) = 0\). What is \(\Delta\)CoVaR?
                                                                            • Answer: \(0.5 \times (0 - (-6)) = 3\%\)
                                                                          • Question: why can SRISK be negative?
                                                                            • Answer: a bank with low leverage keeps enough capital even after a market fall of 40%: it has a capital surplus

                                                                          Section summary

                                                                          Systemic risk is measured from market prices through the link between the tail of the bank and the tail of the system, in both directions of conditioning, and through networks estimated from returns. The measures answer different questions and carry large uncertainty, and macroprudential policy uses them alongside the Basel III capital rules.
