The previous chapters established two facts that seem to pull in opposite directions. Daily returns are almost uncorrelated at small lags, as the weak form of the efficient market hypothesis requires (Chapter 7); their volatility, however, is persistent: turbulent days follow turbulent days (Chapters 8 and 9). This chapter asks the question at the scale of long lags: do today's returns remember what happened months ago? The central tool is the Hurst exponent \(H\), which measures at the same time the memory of a series and the way its risk grows with the horizon. For a random walk, \(H = 0.5\).

The fractal market hypothesis (FMH) of Edgar Peters adds a second theme: who trades, and over which horizon. The market is stable when it contains investors with very different horizons, from minutes to decades, and crises arise when these horizons collapse into one. The hypothesis is stated verbally, but it has statistical consequences that can be tested: the self-similarity of returns across scales, memory over long horizons, and a change of this memory in crises.

On the daily S&P 500 data from 2000 to September 2026, the direction of returns has no long memory: the DFA (detrended fluctuation analysis) exponent of returns is 0.49, inside the 95% band of memoryless series, \([0.46, 0.54]\). The size of returns, however, has strong long memory: the DFA exponent of absolute returns is 0.95. The same contrast appears in all six markets of the chapter, with one partial exception, the BET, whose returns show a weak persistence. The chapter builds the estimators behind these figures, shows how structural breaks and persistent volatility can mimic long memory, and discusses what the results mean for scaling risk with the horizon.

From Efficient to Fractal Markets

The section starts from the efficient market hypothesis and from the facts that its standard model, the random walk with Normal increments, does not explain. It then introduces the fractal market hypothesis, which describes the market through its investors and their horizons, illustrates it with the 1987 crash and shows which of its statistical consequences can be tested. The section ends with a comparison of the efficient market hypothesis, the fractal market hypothesis and the adaptive markets hypothesis.

What the EMH Leaves Open

Slide 5 in the PDF

The efficient market hypothesis (EMH) states that prices reflect the available information (Fama, 1970). In its weak form, studied in Chapter 7, past returns do not help to anticipate future returns. The associated statistical model is the random walk of log prices, with independent increments. In the language of this chapter, the random walk corresponds to the Hurst exponent \(H = 0.5\), and any systematic departure from 0.5 points to some form of long-range dependence.

The random walk with Normal increments, however, leaves several stylised facts unexplained. Returns have heavy tails (Chapters 2, 3 and 5): extreme days are far more frequent than the Normal distribution allows. Volatility comes in clusters, the volatility clustering of Chapters 8 and 9. And crashes such as that of 19 October 1987, discussed below, do not follow from new information of a comparable size.

There is one more question the EMH does not ask: who trades, and over which horizon. The theory treats all investors as a single representative investor who evaluates information correctly. The fractal market hypothesis starts exactly here: it describes the market as a population of investors with different horizons and ties the stability of the market to the diversity of these horizons.

In brief

  • EMH (efficient market hypothesis, Fama (1970); Chapter 7): prices reflect the available information, so returns cannot be forecast from past returns
    • the random walk of prices implies independent increments: in this chapter, \(H = 0.5\)
  • Facts that the random walk with Normal increments does not explain
    • heavy tails (Chapters 2, 3, 5); volatility clustering (Chapters 8, 9)
    • crashes such as that of 19 October 1987 (a few slides ahead)
  • The EMH says nothing about who trades and at which horizon: all investors are treated as one representative investor

Edgar Peters and the Fractal Market Hypothesis

Slide 6 in the PDF

The fractal market hypothesis was formulated by Peters (1994). Its starting point is that the market consists of investors with many investment horizons, from traders who hold a position for a few minutes to pension funds that invest for decades. The same price event means different things to these investors, and their interaction drives price dynamics.

Peters states the hypothesis in five claims. First: the market is stable when it contains investors with very different horizons, because they provide liquidity to each other. Second: short horizons react mostly to sentiment and technical information, long horizons to fundamental information. Third: if fundamental information becomes doubtful, long-term investors withdraw or start trading short term; the horizons become uniform and the market becomes unstable. Fourth: the price combines short-term technical trading with long-term fundamental valuation. Fifth: an asset unrelated to the economic cycle has no long-term trend, so its dynamics are dominated by short-term trading and liquidity.

The word "fractal" refers to the structure across scales. If the same kind of mechanism acts on every horizon, returns over different horizons should look statistically alike after a suitable rescaling. This property, self-similarity, is the subject of the next section.

In brief

  • FMH (fractal market hypothesis) Peters (1994): the market is made of investors with many investment horizons, from minutes to decades
  • The five statements of Peters
    • the market is stable when it contains investors with many different horizons: this ensures liquidity
    • short horizons react to sentiment and technical information; long horizons react to fundamental information
    • if the fundamental information becomes doubtful, long-term investors leave or start to trade short term: horizons become uniform and the market becomes unstable
    • prices combine short-term technical trading and long-term fundamental valuation
    • an asset without a link to the economic cycle has no long-term trend: short-term trading and liquidity dominate

Investment Horizons and Liquidity: an Example

Slide 7 in the PDF

The example on the slide makes the mechanism concrete. A stock falls by 3% one morning. For a day trader, whose horizon is a few hours, a 3% fall is a large move that can wipe out the day's gains, and the reaction is to sell. For a pension fund with a ten-year horizon, the same fall is small compared with the variation it expects over that horizon, and the lower price is an opportunity to buy.

The two types of investor are therefore natural counterparties. The long-horizon investor supplies the liquidity the short-horizon investor needs, and the price adjusts in an orderly way, without jumps. In this interpretation, liquidity is not a property of the asset but the result of participants valuing the same price over different horizons.

If the pension fund also starts to judge the market over the next few hours, for instance because it no longer trusts fundamental information, nobody buys. Liquidity disappears and the fall accelerates. The same pattern of behaviour can repeat on every horizon, which gives returns that are statistically alike at different scales: the meaning of "fractal" in the name of the hypothesis.

In brief

  • A share falls by 3% in one morning
    • a day trader (horizon: hours) sees a large move and sells
    • a pension fund (horizon: ten years) sees a small move relative to its horizon and buys at a lower price
  • The long-horizon investor supplies the liquidity that the short-horizon investor demands: the price stays orderly
  • If the pension fund also starts to fear the next hours, nobody buys: there is no liquidity and the fall accelerates
  • Fractal: the same pattern of behaviour repeats at every horizon, with statistically similar returns at different scales

1987: a Crash as a Collapse of Horizons

Slide 8 in the PDF

On 19 October 1987, the S&P 500 fell by about 20% in a single day (Carlson, 2007). The chart published by the Federal Reserve shows the index in the days around the crash: the fall happened in a single session, without new economic information of a comparable size.

The FMH interpretation focuses on horizons. Portfolio insurance and automated trading programmed to sell when prices fall made long-horizon portfolios sell exactly like short-term traders. All horizons effectively collapsed into one, and the buyers who should have provided liquidity disappeared.

A testable consequence follows from this interpretation. If short horizons dominate in crises, the statistical structure of returns across scales should change during crisis periods. The next slide shows how this idea translates into quantities that can be estimated.

In brief

  • 19 October 1987: the S&P 500 fell by about 20% in one day Carlson (2007)
  • The FMH reading
    • portfolio insurance and program trading made long-term portfolios sell like short-term traders

Testing the FMH: Scaling, Horizons and Liquidity

Slide 9 in the PDF

The FMH is a verbal theory, without a probabilistic model of its own. Its statistical consequences can still be tested, and the chapter follows three of them. The first is self-similarity: the distribution of \(h\)-day returns should look the same after rescaling by \(h^H\) (Section 2). The second is memory: the Hurst exponent \(H\) measures dependence over long horizons (Sections 3–5). The third is change: in crises, \(H\) and the weight of short horizons should shift (Section 8).

The reference studies for the third consequence are those of Kristoufek (2012) and Kristoufek (2013) on the 2008 financial crisis. The first uses local Hurst exponents, estimated on rolling windows, and the second uses wavelet power, which shows how the variance of a series is distributed across time scales and how this distribution changes over time. Both studies analyse major stock indices.

Their result agrees with the FMH: the most turbulent periods of the crisis coincide with the dominance of short investment horizons. The result does not prove Peters's mechanism, but it shows that his prediction about horizons has a measurable counterpart in the data.

In brief

  • The FMH is a verbal theory; its statistical consequences can be tested
    • self-similarity: the distribution of \(h\)-day returns looks the same after rescaling by \(h^H\) (Section 2)
    • memory: the Hurst exponent \(H\) measures dependence over long horizons (Sections 3–5)
    • change: in crises, \(H\) and the share of short horizons should change (Section 8)
  • Landmark evidence: Kristoufek (2012) and Kristoufek (2013) on the 2008 crisis
    • local Hurst exponents and wavelet power of US and European indices
    • the most turbulent periods coincide with a dominance of short investment horizons, as the FMH predicts

EMH, FMH and AMH

Slide 10 in the PDF

The table compares three hypotheses about markets. The EMH (Fama, 1970) works with a representative investor, and prices are always right given the available information. The FMH (Peters, 1994) works with investors on many horizons, and stability comes from their diversity. The adaptive markets hypothesis (AMH) of Lo (2004) describes the market as an ecosystem of "species" of investors and strategies that compete and adapt, so that the degree of efficiency changes with the environment.

The row on memory translates the three hypotheses into the language of the chapter. For the EMH, \(H = 0.5\). For the FMH, \(H\) may differ from 0.5, because the structure of horizons can produce long-range dependence. For the AMH, \(H\) varies over time with market conditions. The last row shows how each hypothesis sees crises: for the EMH they lie outside the theory, for the FMH they are collapses of horizons, and for the AMH they are episodes in which many strategies die out at the same time.

The three hypotheses are not rival tests among which the data would pick one. The EMH is a good approximation in normal conditions; the FMH and the AMH try to explain where and why this approximation breaks down. For statistical analysis, the practical consequence is that \(H\) must be estimated both on the full sample and on subperiods.

The section introduces the geometric language of the chapter. Benoît Mandelbrot: Roughness as a Measure

Slide 11 in the PDF

Benoît Mandelbrot (1924–2010) brought to finance the idea that roughness can be measured. In Mandelbrot (1963) he showed that changes in cotton prices have heavy tails and that their distribution looks alike at the daily, monthly and yearly scale. The model he proposed then was the family of \(\alpha\)-stable distributions, the subject of Chapter 3, which are invariant, up to a rescaling, under the summation of independent variables.

In Mandelbrot (1967), Mandelbrot asked a question of geometry: how long is the coast of Britain? The measured length of a coastline grows as the ruler gets shorter, because a shorter ruler follows more of its bays and headlands. The rate at which the length grows as the ruler shrinks defines a fractal dimension, which measures how rough the curve is.

Mandelbrot & Van Ness (1968) defined fractional Brownian motion, the central probabilistic model of this chapter. The common idea of these papers is the notion of a fractal: an object whose parts resemble the whole, either exactly, as in geometric figures, or in distribution, as in price series. The Mandelbrot set in the picture is the best-known visual example.

  • Mandelbrot (1963): cotton prices have heavy tails and look alike at daily, monthly and yearly scales

Fractal Dimension

Slide 12 in the PDF

The simplest fractal dimension is the similarity dimension. If an object consists of \(N\) copies of itself, each scaled down by the factor \(1/s\), its dimension is \(D = \ln N / \ln s\). The definition reproduces the usual dimensions: a segment consists of 2 halves, so \(D = \ln 2/\ln 2 = 1\); a square consists of 4 squares with half the side, so \(D = \ln 4/\ln 2 = 2\).

For fractal objects the dimension is not an integer. The Koch curve consists of 4 copies of itself, each scaled down by 3, so \(D = \ln 4/\ln 3 = 1.262\): more than a line, less than a surface. The Sierpinski triangle consists of 3 copies scaled down by 2, with \(D = \ln 3/\ln 2 = 1.585\). For the west coast of Britain, Mandelbrot (1967) gives \(D \approx 1.25\).

The link with finance runs through the graph of a price path. For fractional Brownian motion, the fractal dimension of the graph is \(D = 2 - H\). An ordinary Brownian motion, with \(H = 0.5\), has \(D = 1.5\). A path with persistent trends (\(H > 0.5\)) is smoother and has \(D < 1.5\); a path with frequent reversals (\(H < 0.5\)) is rougher and has \(D > 1.5\).

In brief

  • Similarity dimension: an object made of \(N\) copies of itself, each scaled by \(1/s\), has \(D = \ln N/\ln s\)
    • a segment: 2 halves, \(D = \ln 2/\ln 2 = 1\); a square: 4 quarters of side \(1/2\), \(D = \ln 4/\ln 2 = 2\)
    • the Koch curve: 4 copies scaled by \(1/3\), \(D = \ln 4/\ln 3 = 1.262\)
    • the Sierpinski triangle: 3 copies scaled by \(1/2\), \(D = \ln 3/\ln 2 = 1.585\)
  • The west coast of Britain: \(D \approx 1.25\) Mandelbrot (1967)
  • The graph of a price path: \(D = 2 - H\)
    • Brownian motion: \(D = 1.5\); a smoother, trending path (\(H > 0.5\)) has \(D < 1.5\)

Self-Similar Processes

Slide 13 in the PDF

A stochastic process \(X_t\) is self-similar with exponent \(H\) if, for every \(c > 0\),

\[X_{ct} \overset{d}{=} c^H X_t,\]
(11.1)

where \(\overset{d}{=}\) denotes equality in distribution (Franke, Härdle & Hafner (2019), Def. 14.1). Stretching the time axis by the factor \(c\) has the same effect as stretching the values by the factor \(c^H\). The exponent \(H\) is called the Hurst exponent.

The Brownian motion of Chapter 4 is self-similar with \(H = 1/2\): \(W_{ct} \overset{d}{=} \sqrt{c}\,W_t\). For log prices with self-similar increments, (11.1) has a direct consequence for multi-day returns. If \(r_t^{(h)}\) is the \(h\)-day return, its standard deviation is \(\mathrm{sd}(r_t^{(h)}) = h^H\,\mathrm{sd}(r_t)\). The square-root-of-time rule, \(\sqrt{h}\), routinely used to scale volatility and VaR, is the special case \(H = 1/2\).

The relation gives a first estimator of \(H\). Taking logarithms, \(\ln \mathrm{sd}(r^{(h)}) = \ln \mathrm{sd}(r) + H \ln h\), so on a chart with both axes logarithmic the points must lie on a line with slope \(H\). The next slide applies this estimator to three markets.

In brief

  • Self-similar process Franke, Härdle & Hafner (2019), Def. 14.1: \(X_{ct} \overset{d}{=} c^H X_t\) for every \(c > 0\); \(H\) is the Hurst exponent
    • \(\overset{d}{=}\): equal in distribution; stretching time by \(c\) equals stretching values by \(c^H\)
  • Brownian motion (Chapter 4) is self-similar with \(H = 1/2\): \(W_{ct} \overset{d}{=} \sqrt{c}\,W_t\)
  • Consequence for log prices with self-similar increments
    • \(\mathrm{sd}(r_t^{(h)}) = h^H\,\mathrm{sd}(r_t)\), where \(r_t^{(h)}\) is the \(h\)-day return and sd the standard deviation
    • the square-root-of-time rule \(\sqrt{h}\) is the special case \(H = 1/2\)
  • A first estimator of \(H\): the slope of \(\ln \mathrm{sd}(r^{(h)})\) on \(\ln h\)

        Hurst, the Nile and the R/S Statistic

        The Hurst exponent is named after a hydrologist who designed reservoirs on the Nile. The section shows how the problem of reservoir capacity led to the R/S statistic, computes the statistic step by step on a small example, builds an estimator of \(H\) from it, and shows that in small samples this estimator is biased upwards even for data without memory.

        Harold Edwin Hurst and the Nile

        Slide 16 in the PDF

        Harold Edwin Hurst (1880–1978) was a British hydrologist who worked in Egypt from 1906 and designed reservoirs on the Nile. His practical question was how large a reservoir must be to release the mean flow of the river every year. A reservoir that receives a year's flow and releases the mean fills up in high-flow years and empties in low-flow years, so the capacity needed equals the range of the cumulative deviations of the flows from their mean.

        Hurst (1951) studied how this range grows with the number of years \(n\). For independent flows, theory predicts growth like \(n^{0.5}\). In series of river flows, rainfall and tree-ring thickness, Hurst found growth like \(n^K\), with \(K\) averaging about 0.73. The difference between the exponents is amplified over long periods: the required capacity grows much faster with the number of years than the assumption of independent flows predicts.

        The explanation is that high-flow years tend to follow high-flow years, and dry years follow dry years. Deviations from the mean do not offset each other quickly but accumulate over long periods. This is long memory, and Hurst's exponent became its standard measure. The Nilometer on Roda Island in Cairo, in the picture, built in 861, is one of the historical sources of data on the level of the Nile.

          • years of high flow follow years of high flow: long memory

        The R/S Statistic

        Slide 17 in the PDF

        The R/S statistic formalises Hurst's calculation for a block of observations \(x_1, \dots, x_n\) with mean \(\bar x\). The cumulative deviations from the mean are \(Y_k = \sum_{j=1}^{k} (x_j - \bar x)\), for \(k = 1, \dots, n\); by construction, \(Y_n = 0\). Their range is \(R_n = \max_k Y_k - \min_k Y_k\), and the standard deviation of the block is \(S_n = \sqrt{\frac1n \sum_j (x_j - \bar x)^2}\). The rescaled range statistic, defined in Franke, Härdle & Hafner (2019), 14.4.1, is

        \[(R/S)_n = \frac{R_n}{S_n}\]
        (11.2)

        Dividing by \(S_n\) removes the unit of measurement, so \((R/S)_n\) is comparable across assets with different volatilities.

        What matters is how \((R/S)_n\) behaves as \(n\) grows. For i.i.d. data with finite variance, \((R/S)_n\) grows like \(n^{1/2}\), as a consequence of the functional central limit theorem: the cumulative deviations behave like a Brownian bridge, whose range is of order \(\sqrt{n}\). Under long memory, the cumulative deviations wander further from zero, and \((R/S)_n\) grows like \(n^H\), with \(H > 1/2\).

        A useful property of the statistic is its robustness to heavy tails (Mandelbrot & Wallis, 1969). An extreme value inflates both the range \(R_n\) and the standard deviation \(S_n\), and their ratio is affected much less than either of them. For financial returns, with their heavy tails, this property is an advantage over estimators built directly on variances.

        In brief

        • Block \(x_1, \dots, x_n\) with mean \(\bar x\); cumulative deviations \(Y_k = \sum_{j=1}^{k}(x_j - \bar x)\), \(k = 1, \dots, n\)
          • \(Y_n = 0\) by construction
        • Range: \(R_n = \max_k Y_k - \min_k Y_k\); standard deviation: \(S_n = \sqrt{\tfrac1n\sum_j (x_j - \bar x)^2}\)
        • R/S statistic (rescaled range) Franke, Härdle & Hafner (2019), 14.4.1: \((R/S)_n = R_n/S_n\)
          • dividing by \(S_n\) removes the unit: \((R/S)_n\) is comparable across assets
          • for i.i.d. data with finite variance, \((R/S)_n\) grows like \(n^{1/2}\); under long memory like \(n^H\), \(H > 1/2\)
        • Robust to heavy tails: \(R\) and \(S\) are inflated by the same extreme values Mandelbrot & Wallis (1969)

                From R/S to the Hurst Exponent

                Slide 19 in the PDF

                The standard procedure for estimating \(H\) with R/S follows Mandelbrot & Wallis (1969). For a series of \(N\) returns, block sizes \(n\) are chosen between 10 and \(N/4\), equally spaced on a logarithmic scale. For each \(n\), the series is split into \(\lfloor N/n \rfloor\) non-overlapping blocks, R/S is computed in each block, and the average of these values is \((R/S)_n\).

                The exponent is then estimated by ordinary least squares (OLS) in the regression

                \[\ln (R/S)_n = \ln c + H \ln n + e_n,\]
                (11.3)

                where \(c\) is a constant and \(e_n\) is the regression error. The estimated slope \(\hat H\) is the R/S estimator of the Hurst exponent. The lower limit of 10 observations avoids blocks so small that R/S is dominated by sampling effects, and the upper limit \(N/4\) guarantees at least four blocks for each average.

                The log-log plot of \((R/S)_n\) against \(n\) is the main diagnostic tool. If the points lie on a line, a single scaling exponent describes the series; a curvature or a change of slope points to different behaviour on short and long scales. The method is applied to returns, which are stationary, not to prices: for a price series, the cumulative deviations grow linearly with the block size, and \((R/S)_n\) grows roughly like \(n\), whatever the memory.

                In brief

                • Steps Mandelbrot & Wallis (1969)
                  • choose block sizes \(n\) between 10 and \(N/4\), evenly spaced on a log scale
                  • split the \(N\) returns into \(\lfloor N/n\rfloor\) non-overlapping blocks; compute \(R/S\) in each block and average: \((R/S)_n\)
                  • regress \(\ln (R/S)_n = \ln c + H \ln n + e_n\) by OLS (ordinary least squares): the slope \(\hat H\) estimates \(H\)
                  • \(N\): the number of returns; \(n\): the block size; \(c\): a constant; \(e_n\): the regression error
                • The log-log plot is the main diagnostic: a straight line supports a single scaling exponent
                • Applied to returns (stationary), not to prices: for prices, \((R/S)_n\) grows like \(n\)

                Interpreting \(H\)

                Slide 20 in the PDF

                The table summarises the three regimes of \(H\). For \(0 < H < 0.5\), increments are negatively correlated and memory is anti-persistent: a rise tends to be followed by a fall. The path is rough, with fractal dimension \(D = 2 - H > 1.5\). For \(H = 0.5\), increments are uncorrelated, as in a random walk, and \(D = 1.5\). For \(0.5 < H < 1\), increments are positively correlated, memory is persistent and produces trends, and the path is smoother, with \(D < 1.5\).

                A common confusion is to read \(H\) as a probability. \(H = 0.7\) does not mean that the sign of the return repeats on 70% of days. \(H\) is a scaling exponent: it describes how the variability of sums grows with the length of the period and, equivalently, how slowly autocorrelations decay at long lags.

                Mandelbrot & Wallis (1968) gave biblical names to the two departures from the independent Gaussian model. The Noah effect refers to extreme values: heavy tails, the flood. The Joseph effect refers to long runs of high or low values, as in "seven years of plenty, seven years of famine". The two effects are distinct and have different measures: \(H\) measures the Joseph effect, and the tail index of Chapter 5 measures the Noah effect.

                Small Samples: the Bias of R/S

                Slide 21 in the PDF

                The result that \((R/S)_n\) grows like \(n^{1/2}\) for i.i.d. data is asymptotic. Anis & Lloyd (1976) computed the exact expected value of \((R/S)_n\) for independent Normal summands and showed that it approaches \(c\,n^{1/2}\) only for large \(n\); for small \(n\), \((R/S)_n\) grows faster than \(n^{1/2}\). The slope estimated on blocks from \(n = 10\) to \(N/4\) is therefore above 0.5 even for data without any memory.

                A Monte Carlo simulation with 500 i.i.d. Normal series measures this bias. The mean of the R/S estimator is 0.588 for series of 250 observations, 0.562 for 1000 observations and 0.547 for 5000 observations. The bias shrinks with the length of the series but does not vanish at the usual lengths of financial data. For \(N = 1000\), 95% of the estimates lie in the interval \([0.50, 0.63]\).

                The practical rule is to compare \(\hat H\) not with 0.5 but with the distribution of \(\hat H\) under the null hypothesis, simulated for the same \(N\) and with the same block sizes. An R/S estimate of 0.56 on 1000 observations is no evidence of long memory: it is exactly the mean of the estimator for memoryless data. Section 5 builds such bands for all the estimators of the chapter.

                In brief

                • For i.i.d. data, \((R/S)_n\) approaches \(c\,n^{1/2}\) only for large \(n\); for small \(n\) it grows faster Anis & Lloyd (1976)
                  • the fitted slope over \(n = 10, \dots, N/4\) is above 0.5 even without any memory
                • Monte Carlo, 500 i.i.d. Normal series
                  • mean \(\hat H_{R/S}\): \(0.588\) for \(N = 250\), \(0.562\) for \(N = 1000\), \(0.547\) for \(N = 5000\)
                  • 95% band for \(N = 1000\): \([0.50, 0.63]\)
                • Rule: compare \(\hat H\) with the distribution of \(\hat H\) under the null hypothesis for the same \(N\), not with 0.5 (Section 5)

                Section summary

                Hurst's R/S statistic measures the range of the cumulative deviations relative to the standard deviation, and its slope on a log-log plot estimates \(H\). For independent data the slope is 0.5 asymptotically, but in usual samples it is biased upwards: on average 0.562 for 1000 i.i.d. observations. \(H\) measures the Joseph effect, persistence, not the Noah effect, extreme values. The next section gives the exact definition of long memory and the models that generate it.

                Long Memory: Definitions and Models

                The section defines long memory through autocorrelations and through the spectral density and compares it with the short memory of ARMA models.                 Short and Long Memory

                Slide 22 in the PDF

                Let \(X_t\) be a stationary process with autocorrelation function (ACF) \(\rho(k)\). The process has long memory if its autocorrelations are not summable, \(\sum_k |\rho(k)| = \infty\) (Franke, Härdle & Hafner (2019), 14.1). The usual parametric form of this property is hyperbolic decay,

                \[\rho(k) \sim C\,k^{2d - 1}, \qquad k \to \infty,\]
                (11.4)

                with a constant \(C > 0\) and the memory parameter \(0 < d < 0.5\); the symbol \(\sim\) means that the ratio of the two sides tends to 1. For \(0 < d < 0.5\) the exponent \(2d - 1\) lies between −1 and 0, so the sum of the autocorrelations diverges. Hyperbolic decay implies non-summability; the converse does not hold in general, but all the models of this chapter have the form (11.4).

                In the frequency domain, the same property appears as a pole of the spectral density at zero: \(f(\lambda) \sim C\,\lambda^{-2d}\) as \(\lambda \to 0\). The spectral density \(f(\lambda)\) decomposes the variance of the series over frequencies, and frequencies close to zero correspond to very long cycles. A long-memory series has a large share of its variance in these long cycles.

                Short memory is that of ARMA (autoregressive moving average) models. For an AR(1) with coefficient \(\phi\), \(\rho(k) = \phi^k\) decays exponentially, and the sum \(\sum_k |\phi|^k\) is finite, equal to \(1/(1 - |\phi|)\). The difference between \(\phi^k\) and \(k^{2d-1}\) is the difference between a shock forgotten after a few periods and a shock that still matters after hundreds of periods. The link with the Hurst exponent is \(d = H - 1/2\).

                In brief

                • Stationary \(X_t\) with autocorrelations \(\rho(k)\) (ACF, autocorrelation function); long memory Franke, Härdle & Hafner (2019), 14.1:
                  • \(\sum_{k} |\rho(k)| = \infty\): the autocorrelations are not summable
                  • the usual form: hyperbolic decay \(\rho(k) \sim C\,k^{2d - 1}\) as \(k \to \infty\), with the memory parameter \(0 < d < 0.5\); it implies non-summability, the converse does not hold in general
                  • in frequency: the spectral density has a pole at zero, \(f(\lambda) \sim C\,\lambda^{-2d}\) as \(\lambda \to 0\)
                  • \(C > 0\): a constant; \(\sim\): the ratio of the two sides tends to 1; \(f(\lambda)\): the share of variance at frequency \(\lambda\) (long cycles: \(\lambda\) near 0)
                • Short memory: ARMA (autoregressive moving average) models, e.g. AR(1) with \(\rho(k) = \phi^k\)
                  • exponential decay: \(\sum_k |\phi|^k = 1/(1 - |\phi|) < \infty\)
                • Link with the Hurst exponent: \(d = H - 1/2\)

                Worked Example: How Fast Does Memory Fade?

                Slide 23 in the PDF

                The example compares two processes with the same first-order autocorrelation. The first is a fractional Gaussian noise with \(H = 0.8\), the long-memory model defined a few slides further on. Its first-order autocorrelation is \(\rho(1) = 2^{2H-1} - 1 = 2^{0.6} - 1 = 0.516\), and at long lags the autocorrelations are \(\rho(10) = 0.191\), \(\rho(50) = 0.100\) and \(\rho(100) = 0.076\).

                The second process is an AR(1) with \(\phi = 0.516\), chosen to have the same first-order autocorrelation. Its autocorrelations are the powers of \(\phi\): \(\rho(10) = 0.516^{10} = 0.0013\), and \(\rho(100) = 0.516^{100}\) is practically zero.

                In the short run, the two processes cannot be told apart by their first autocorrelation. In the long run, they are very different: after 10 lags, the autocorrelation of the long-memory process is two orders of magnitude larger than that of the AR(1), and after 100 lags a shock still matters only for the long-memory process. An ARMA model fitted to the autocorrelations at small lags cannot reproduce such persistence.

                In brief

                • Long memory: fractional Gaussian noise (next slides) with \(H = 0.8\)
                  • \(\rho(1) = 2^{2H - 1} - 1 = 2^{0.6} - 1 = 0.516\)
                  • \(\rho(10) = 0.191\), \(\rho(50) = 0.100\), \(\rho(100) = 0.076\)
                • Short memory: AR(1) with the same first autocorrelation, \(\phi = 0.516\)
                  • \(\rho(10) = \phi^{10} = 0.0013\); \(\rho(100) = \phi^{100}\), practically zero
                • Same short-run behaviour, very different long run: after 100 days, a shock still matters only under long memory

                Fractional Brownian Motion

                Slide 24 in the PDF

                Fractional Brownian motion (fBm) \(B_H(t)\), with \(0 < H < 1\), is the Gaussian process with continuous paths that is self-similar with exponent \(H\) and has stationary increments (Mandelbrot & Van Ness, 1968), with the properties given in Franke, Härdle & Hafner (2019), Def. 14.2. These three properties determine it up to a scale constant: it is the natural generalisation of Brownian motion to Hurst exponents other than 1/2.

                The moments of the process are given by Theorem 14.1 of Franke, Härdle & Hafner (2019). The process starts at zero, \(B_H(0) = 0\), has mean zero and variance \(E[B_H(t)^2] = \sigma^2 |t|^{2H}\), where \(\sigma^2\) is the variance at \(t = 1\). The covariance between two points in time is

                \[\mathrm{Cov}\big(B_H(t), B_H(s)\big) = \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\left(|t|^{2H} + |s|^{2H} - |t - s|^{2H}\right).\]
                (11.5)

                A variance growing like \(|t|^{2H}\) is the self-similarity (11.1) expressed through moments.

                For \(H = 1/2\), formula (11.5) becomes \(\sigma^2 \min(t, s)\), the covariance of Brownian motion, whose increments are independent. For \(H \neq 1/2\), increments over disjoint intervals are correlated at all lags: positively for \(H > 1/2\), negatively for \(H < 1/2\). Because the process is Gaussian and its covariance is known exactly, it can be simulated exactly from the covariance matrix of the increments; the circulant embedding method makes this computation fast even for long series.

                In brief

                • Fractional Brownian motion (fBm) \(B_H(t)\), \(0 < H < 1\) Mandelbrot & Van Ness (1968); Franke, Härdle & Hafner (2019), Def. 14.2: a Gaussian process with continuous paths, self-similar with exponent \(H\) and with stationary increments
                • Properties (Franke, Härdle & Hafner (2019), Th. 14.1)
                  • \(B_H(0) = 0\), \(E[B_H(t)] = 0\), \(E[B_H(t)^2] = \sigma^2 |t|^{2H}\) (\(\sigma^2\): the variance at \(t = 1\))
                  • \(\mathrm{Cov}(B_H(t), B_H(s)) = \tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\left(|t|^{2H} + |s|^{2H} - |t - s|^{2H}\right)\)
                • \(H = 1/2\): Brownian motion, independent increments; \(H \neq 1/2\): the increments are correlated at all lags
                • Simulation: exact, from the covariance matrix of the increments (circulant embedding, as in the notebook)

                      Fractional Gaussian Noise

                      Slide 26 in the PDF

                      Fractional Gaussian noise (fGn) is the series of fBm increments, \(Y_k = B_H(k+1) - B_H(k)\) (Franke, Härdle & Hafner (2019), 14.3). Because fBm has stationary increments, fGn is a stationary series, the counterpart of white noise with memory. Its autocorrelation function follows from (11.5):

                      \[\rho(k) = \tfrac12\left(|k+1|^{2H} - 2|k|^{2H} + |k-1|^{2H}\right),\]
                      (11.6)

                      and for large \(k\), \(\rho(k) \approx H(2H - 1)\,k^{2H-2}\). Comparing with (11.4), the exponent \(2H - 2\) equals \(2d - 1\) for \(d = H - 1/2\).

                      The sign of the factor \(H(2H - 1)\) gives the three regimes. For \(1/2 < H < 1\), all autocorrelations are positive and not summable: long memory, with \(d = H - 1/2\) between 0 and 1/2. For \(H = 1/2\), all autocorrelations from lag 1 on are zero: white noise. For \(0 < H < 1/2\), the autocorrelations are negative and summable: anti-persistence, a form of memory different from long memory in the sense of definition (11.4).

                      fGn is the model behind the R/S and DFA estimators. For an fGn series, both estimators estimate the Hurst exponent \(H\) of the fBm the series comes from. This is why returns are treated as a possible fGn and log prices as a possible fBm.

                      In brief

                      • Fractional Gaussian noise (fGn): the increments \(Y_k = B_H(k + 1) - B_H(k)\), a stationary series Franke, Härdle & Hafner (2019), 14.3
                        • \(\rho(k) = \tfrac12\left(|k + 1|^{2H} - 2|k|^{2H} + |k - 1|^{2H}\right)\)
                        • for large \(k\): \(\rho(k) \approx H(2H - 1)\,k^{2H - 2}\)
                      • Three regimes
                        • \(1/2 < H < 1\): \(\rho(k) > 0\) and not summable: long memory with \(d = H - 1/2 \in (0, 1/2)\)
                        • \(H = 1/2\): white noise
                        • \(0 < H < 1/2\): \(\rho(k) < 0\), summable: anti-persistence
                      • fGn is the model behind R/S and DFA: for fGn data, both estimate \(H\)

                        ARFIMA(0,\(d\),0): Fractional Differencing

                        Slide 28 in the PDF

                        The second long-memory model comes from time-series econometrics (Granger & Joyeux, 1980; Hosking, 1981). The ARFIMA(0,\(d\),0) model (autoregressive fractionally integrated moving average) is defined by

                        \[(1 - L)^d X_t = \varepsilon_t,\]
                        (11.7)

                        where \(\varepsilon_t\) is i.i.d. with mean 0 and variance \(\sigma^2\), and \(L\) is the lag operator, \(L X_t = X_{t-1}\). For \(d = 0\) the model is white noise, and for \(d = 1\) it is the random walk of Chapter 7. What is new is that \(d\) may take non-integer values.

                        The operator \((1 - L)^d\) with non-integer \(d\) is defined by the binomial expansion (Franke, Härdle & Hafner (2019), 14.2): \((1 - L)^d = \sum_{j \ge 0} \pi_j L^j\), with \(\pi_0 = 1\) and \(\pi_j = \pi_{j-1}(j - 1 - d)/j\). The model can then be written as \(X_t = \varepsilon_t - \sum_{j \ge 1} \pi_j X_{t-j}\): the current value depends on the whole past, with weights that decay slowly, like a power of \(j\). In infinite moving-average form, \(X_t = \sum_{j \ge 0} \psi_j \varepsilon_{t-j}\), with \(\psi_0 = 1\) and \(\psi_j = \psi_{j-1}(j - 1 + d)/j\), each past shock contributes with a weight that also decays like a power.

                        The autocorrelations have a closed form, \(\rho(k) = \Gamma(1-d)\Gamma(k+d)/[\Gamma(d)\Gamma(k+1-d)]\), where \(\Gamma\) is the gamma function, with \(\Gamma(n) = (n-1)!\) for integers. More useful for computation is the recursion \(\rho(k) = \rho(k-1)(k - 1 + d)/(k - d)\), starting from \(\rho(1) = d/(1 - d)\). For large \(k\), \(\rho(k) \sim C k^{2d - 1}\), so the model has long memory for \(0 < d < 1/2\), with the same parameter \(d\) as in definition (11.4).

                        In brief

                        • ARFIMA(0,\(d\),0) Granger & Joyeux (1980); Hosking (1981): \((1 - L)^d X_t = \varepsilon_t\), \(\varepsilon_t\) i.i.d. \((0, \sigma^2)\), \(L\) the lag operator (\(L X_t = X_{t-1}\))
                          • ARFIMA: autoregressive fractionally integrated moving average; \(d = 0\): white noise, \(d = 1\): random walk (Chapter 7)
                        • Binomial expansion Franke, Härdle & Hafner (2019), 14.2: \((1 - L)^d = \sum_{j \ge 0} \pi_j L^j\), \(\pi_0 = 1\), \(\pi_j = \pi_{j-1}\,(j - 1 - d)/j\)
                          • \(X_t = \varepsilon_t - \sum_{j \ge 1}\pi_j X_{t-j}\): the whole past matters, with slowly decaying weights
                          • MA(\(\infty\)) form: \(X_t = \sum_{j \ge 0}\psi_j\varepsilon_{t-j}\), \(\psi_0 = 1\), \(\psi_j = \psi_{j-1}\,(j - 1 + d)/j\)
                        • Autocorrelations: \(\rho(k) = \dfrac{\Gamma(1 - d)\,\Gamma(k + d)}{\Gamma(d)\,\Gamma(k + 1 - d)}\), i.e. \(\rho(k) = \rho(k - 1)\,\dfrac{k - 1 + d}{k - d}\)
                          • \(\Gamma(\cdot)\): the gamma function, \(\Gamma(n) = (n - 1)!\) for integers; \(\rho(1) = d/(1 - d)\)
                          • for large \(k\), \(\rho(k) \sim C k^{2d - 1}\): long memory for \(0 < d < 1/2\)

                        The Memory Parameter \(d\)

                        Slide 29 in the PDF

                        The table, taken from Franke, Härdle & Hafner (2019), Table 14.1, describes the behaviour of the ARFIMA model over the whole range of \(d\). For \(d\) between −0.5 and 0, memory is anti-persistent, and the ACF decays hyperbolically, with negative values. For \(d = 0\), memory is short and the ACF decays exponentially, as for ARMA models. For \(d\) between 0 and 0.5, memory is long and the series is stationary, with finite variance. On this range, \(H = d + 1/2\).

                        For \(d\) between 0.5 and 1, the series has long memory but is no longer stationary: its variance is infinite. The series is still mean-reverting, in the sense that the effect of a shock eventually dies out. For \(d = 1\), the series has a unit root: shocks are permanent and the ACF does not decay. Fractional integration thus covers the whole space between the stationary case \(d = 0\) and the unit root \(d = 1\) of Chapter 7, which classical tests treat as a choice between two values.

                        The general ARFIMA(\(p\),\(d\),\(q\)) model combines the two kinds of memory (Franke, Härdle & Hafner (2019), 14.6.1): an ARMA(\(p\),\(q\)) part describes the short-run dynamics, and the parameter \(d\) describes the long-range dependence. The separation allows long memory to be estimated without the autocorrelations at small lags contaminating it.

                              Section summary

                              Long memory means autocorrelations that are not summable and that decay hyperbolically, \(\rho(k) \sim C k^{2d - 1}\), or, equivalently for the usual models, a spectral density with a pole at zero. Fractional Brownian motion and its increments, fractional Gaussian noise, realise this property for \(H > 1/2\); the ARFIMA model realises it through fractional differencing, with \(d = H - 1/2\). In both models the autocorrelations remain sizeable after hundreds of lags, where an AR(1) with the same first autocorrelation has long forgotten the shock. The next section shows how \(H\) and \(d\) are estimated from data.

                              The section compares four tools for measuring long memory: the R/S estimator, detrended fluctuation analysis (DFA), the GPH log-periodogram regression and Lo's modified R/S test. For each, it shows what it estimates and where it is weak, and then builds Monte Carlo bands that take finite samples into account.                               Detrended Fluctuation Analysis (DFA)

                              Slide 33 in the PDF

                              Detrended fluctuation analysis (DFA) was introduced by Peng et al. (1994) for DNA sequences and was designed for signals with slowly changing trends. The procedure has four steps. First, the profile of the series is built, \(Y_k = \sum_{j \le k} (x_j - \bar x)\), the same running sum as in the R/S statistic. Next, the profile is split into blocks of size \(n\), and a straight line is fitted by OLS in each block.

                              The third step computes the fluctuation function \(F(n)\), the root mean square of the deviations of the profile from the local lines, over all blocks. The fourth step repeats the computation for several block sizes and estimates the exponent from the relation

                              \[F(n) \propto n^{\alpha},\]
                              (11.8)

                              as the slope of \(\ln F(n)\) against \(\ln n\).

                              For an fGn, \(\alpha = H\): \(\alpha = 0.5\) for white noise, and \(0.5 < \alpha < 1\) for long memory. For an fBm, that is, for a price series, \(\alpha = H + 1\), because the profile of an already cumulated series is a double sum. The local line removes the linear trend in each block, which makes DFA less sensitive than R/S to slow trends. Kantelhardt et al. (2001) show that \(F(n)\) is unreliable for blocks smaller than about 10 observations, where the local line absorbs too large a part of the fluctuation.

                              In brief

                              • DFA Peng et al. (1994): designed for signals with slowly changing trends
                                • 1. profile \(Y_k = \sum_{j \le k}(x_j - \bar x)\)
                                • 2. split \(Y\) into blocks of size \(n\); in each block, fit a straight line by OLS (linear detrending)
                                • 3. \(F(n)\) = root mean square of the deviations from the lines, over all blocks
                                • 4. \(F(n) \propto n^{\alpha}\): the slope of \(\ln F(n)\) on \(\ln n\) is \(\hat\alpha\)
                              • Interpretation
                                • for fGn, \(\alpha = H\): \(\alpha = 0.5\) white noise, \(0.5 < \alpha < 1\) long memory; for fBm, \(\alpha = H + 1\)
                                • the local line removes linear trends inside each block; \(F(n)\) is unreliable for \(n\) below about 10 Kantelhardt et al. (2001)

                                    The GPH Log-Periodogram Regression

                                    Slide 35 in the PDF

                                    The GPH estimator works with the periodogram, the classical estimator of the spectral density. At the Fourier frequencies \(\lambda_j = 2\pi j / T\), the periodogram is

                                    \[I(\lambda_j) = \frac{1}{2\pi T}\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T} x_t e^{-i\lambda_j t}\right|^2,\]

                                    where \(T\) is the number of observations and \(i\) is the imaginary unit. The periodogram estimates the spectral density, which for a long-memory series behaves like \(C\lambda^{-2d}\) near frequency zero.

                                    Geweke & Porter-Hudak (1983) turn this property into a regression (Franke, Härdle & Hafner (2019), 14.5.2):

                                    \[\ln I(\lambda_j) = c - d\,\ln\!\left(4\sin^2(\lambda_j/2)\right) + e_j, \qquad j = 1, \dots, m,\]
                                    (11.9)

                                    where \(m\) is the number of low frequencies used, \(c\) a constant and \(e_j\) the error. Near zero, \(4\sin^2(\lambda/2) \approx \lambda^2\), so the regressor \(-\ln(4\sin^2(\lambda_j/2))\) is approximately \(-2\ln\lambda_j\), and the OLS slope estimates \(d\) directly. The corresponding Hurst exponent is \(\hat H = \hat d + 0.5\). The asymptotic standard error of \(\hat d\) is \(\pi/\sqrt{24m}\), and the usual choice of bandwidth is \(m = \lfloor T^{0.5} \rfloor\).

                                    The choice of \(m\) is a trade-off between bias and variance. A small \(m\) keeps only the lowest frequencies, where the relation \(f(\lambda) \sim C\lambda^{-2d}\) is most accurate, so the bias is small; but few points mean a large variance. A large \(m\) reduces the variance but lets in higher frequencies, where short-run dynamics distort the spectral density and bias \(\hat d\).

                                    In brief

                                    • Periodogram at the Fourier frequencies \(\lambda_j = 2\pi j/T\): \(I(\lambda_j) = \dfrac{1}{2\pi T}\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T} x_t e^{-i\lambda_j t}\right|^2\)
                                      • \(T\): the number of observations; \(i\): the imaginary unit; \(|\cdot|^2\): the squared modulus
                                      • it estimates the spectral density, which behaves like \(C\lambda^{-2d}\) near zero
                                    • GPH Geweke & Porter-Hudak (1983); Franke, Härdle & Hafner (2019), 14.5.2: \(\ln I(\lambda_j) = c - d\,\ln\!\left(4\sin^2(\lambda_j/2)\right) + e_j\), \(j = 1, \dots, m\)
                                      • \(m\): the number of low frequencies used; \(c\): a constant; \(e_j\): the error
                                      • \(\hat d\) = OLS slope on \(-\ln(4\sin^2(\lambda_j/2))\); \(\hat H = \hat d + 0.5\)
                                      • asymptotic standard error \(\pi/\sqrt{24m}\); the bandwidth \(m = \lfloor T^{0.5}\rfloor\)
                                    • Trade-off: a small \(m\) keeps only the long-run frequencies (small bias, large variance); a large \(m\) lets short-run dynamics in (bias)

                                          Lo's Modified R/S Test

                                          Slide 37 in the PDF

                                          The classical R/S statistic has a specificity problem: short memory inflates it too. An AR(1) with positive autocorrelation makes the cumulative deviations wander further from zero, just as long memory does, although its autocorrelations decay exponentially. A test based on classical R/S therefore mistakes short memory for long memory.

                                          Lo (1991) corrects this problem by replacing the standard deviation in the denominator with an estimate of the long-run variance:

                                          \[V_N(q) = \frac{R_N}{\sqrt{N}\,\hat\sigma_N(q)}, \qquad \hat\sigma^2_N(q) = \hat\gamma_0 + 2\sum_{j=1}^{q}\left(1 - \frac{j}{q+1}\right)\hat\gamma_j,\]
                                          (11.10)

                                          where \(N\) is the number of returns, \(R_N\) is the range of the cumulative deviations over the whole sample, and \(\hat\gamma_j\) are the sample autocovariances. \(\hat\sigma^2_N(q)\) is the Newey–West estimator of the long-run variance (Newey & West, 1987), which includes the autocovariances up to lag \(q\); for \(q = 0\) it gives classical R/S. Lo chooses \(q\) by Andrews's data-dependent rule: \(q = \lfloor (3N/2)^{1/3}\,(2\hat\rho_1/(1 - \hat\rho_1^2))^{2/3} \rfloor\), where \(\hat\rho_1\) is the first-order autocorrelation.

                                          The null hypothesis of the test is short memory. At the 5% level, it is rejected if \(V_N(q)\) falls outside the interval \([0.809, 1.862]\), whose end points are the 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles of the range of a Brownian bridge. Applying the test, Lo concluded that the returns of US stock indices have no long memory once short memory is accounted for.

                                          In brief

                                          • Problem: short memory (e.g. an AR(1)) also raises R/S, so classical R/S mistakes short memory for long memory
                                          • Lo (1991): \(V_N(q) = \dfrac{R_N}{\sqrt{N}\,\hat\sigma_N(q)}\), \(\hat\sigma^2_N(q) = \hat\gamma_0 + 2\sum_{j=1}^{q}\left(1 - \tfrac{j}{q + 1}\right)\hat\gamma_j\)

                                                Small Samples and Monte Carlo Bands

                                                Slide 39 in the PDF

                                                The asymptotic distributions of the R/S and DFA estimators are unreliable at the sample sizes of finance, and for R/S the finite-sample bias is, in addition, systematic. Weron (2002) proposes replacing them with simulation. The procedure has three steps: simulate many i.i.d. Normal series of the same length \(N\) as the data; apply exactly the same estimator to each, with the same block sizes or the same bandwidth; the 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles of the estimates \(\hat H\) form the 95% band under the hypothesis \(H = 0.5\).

                                                The decision rule is simple: the hypothesis \(H = 0.5\) is rejected only if \(\hat H\) lies outside the band. The band automatically contains the bias of the estimator, because it is centred on its mean under the null, not on 0.5.

                                                The i.i.d. Normal series is the simplest possible null hypothesis, but not the most suitable one for every question. Financial returns have small autocorrelations at short lags and time-varying volatility, and both can bias the estimators. A more demanding null is obtained by simulating a fitted short-memory model, such as an AR or a GARCH; Section 6 shows why this choice matters for volatility.

                                                In brief

                                                • Asymptotic results are unreliable for R/S and DFA at the sample sizes of finance; use simulation Weron (2002)
                                                  • 1. simulate many i.i.d. Normal series with the same length \(N\) as the data
                                                  • 2. apply exactly the same estimator (same block sizes, same bandwidth)
                                                  • 3. the 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles of \(\hat H\) form the 95% band under \(H = 0.5\)
                                                • Reject \(H = 0.5\) only if \(\hat H\) lies outside the band
                                                • i.i.d. Normal is the simplest null; a fitted short-memory model (AR, GARCH) gives a stricter null (Section 6)

                                                    Spurious Long Memory

                                                    A large estimate of \(H\) does not by itself prove long memory. The section shows two mechanisms that produce spurious long memory in processes that do not have it: regime changes in the mean and very persistent GARCH volatility. The historical case of the Nile, whose mean shifts after 1898, and two simulation experiments measure the effect, and the last slide collects the checks that separate genuine from spurious long memory.

                                                    Breaks Look Like Memory

                                                    Slide 43 in the PDF

                                                    A series with a few changes in its mean, called structural breaks, has a sample ACF that decays slowly, like that of a long-memory series. The mechanism is simple. If the series has two regimes with means \(\mu_1 \neq \mu_2\), and the ACF is computed around the overall mean \(\bar x\), every pair of observations from the same regime shares the deviation \(\mu_i - \bar x\). The product of these deviations is positive whatever the distance between the observations, so the sample autocorrelations stay positive up to lags of the order of the regime lengths.

                                                    The literature has documented the effect both in theory and in data. Diebold & Inoue (2001) show that rare regime switches produce estimates of \(d\) that cannot be told apart from genuine long memory. Granger & Hyung (2004) show that part of the long memory in absolute S&P 500 returns comes from occasional breaks. Lobato & Savin (1998), on the other hand, find that the long memory of squared S&P 500 returns survives their checks, while it does not appear in returns.

                                                    The conclusion is not that long memory is always an illusion, but that an estimate of \(d\) or \(H\) has two possible interpretations, and the data must separate them. Franke, Härdle & Hafner (2019), 14.4.3, presents tests designed to distinguish genuine from spurious long memory.

                                                    In brief

                                                    • A series with a few shifts in the mean has a sample ACF that decays slowly, like long memory
                                                      • two regimes with means \(\mu_1 \neq \mu_2\): every pair of observations from the same regime shares the deviation \(\mu_i - \bar x\)
                                                    • Landmark results
                                                      • Diebold & Inoue (2001): rare regime switches produce estimates of \(d\) indistinguishable from true long memory
                                                      • Granger & Hyung (2004): part of the long memory of S&P 500 absolute returns comes from occasional breaks
                                                      • Lobato & Savin (1998): long memory in squared S&P 500 returns survives the checks; in returns, it is not found
                                                    • Tests of true against spurious long memory: Franke, Härdle & Hafner (2019), 14.4.3

                                                        Checks against Spurious Long Memory

                                                        Slide 48 in the PDF

                                                        The slide collects five checks. The shuffle test permutes the observations of the series at random: the distribution stays the same, heavy tails included, but the order disappears. If \(\hat H\) falls to 0.5 after shuffling, the memory lay in the order of the observations, not in their distribution. Subsample analysis estimates the exponent before and after known breaks, for instance the crises of 2008 and 2020: genuine long memory must appear in every subsample, while memory produced by a break disappears when the break is excluded.

                                                        The third check compares several estimators and bandwidths: R/S, DFA and GPH with \(m = T^{0.5}\) and with \(m = T^{0.6}\) should lead to the same conclusion. The previous simulation showed that GPH reacts much more strongly than DFA to a shift in the mean, so a large gap between them is a warning sign. The fourth check is Lo's modified R/S test, which removes the effect of short memory.

                                                        The fifth check replaces the i.i.d. null hypothesis with a model-based one: Monte Carlo bands simulated from an AR or a GARCH fitted to the data. No check is decisive on its own, but a conclusion that passes all of them is far more credible than a single estimate above 0.5.

                                                              Models for Long Memory in Volatility

                                                              Slide 53 in the PDF

                                                              The FIGARCH (fractionally integrated GARCH) model of Baillie, Bollerslev & Mikkelsen (1996) applies fractional differencing \((1 - L)^d\) in the equation for \(\varepsilon_t^2\) (Franke, Härdle & Hafner (2019), 14.6.2). Volatility shocks then decay hyperbolically, not exponentially. The model contains two familiar cases: \(d = 0\) gives GARCH, and \(d = 1\) gives IGARCH (integrated GARCH), in which volatility shocks are permanent (Chapter 9). FIGARCH covers the space between them.

                                                              A second family of models starts from the FMH idea of heterogeneous horizons. Müller et al. (1997) describe the market through components with different time resolutions, from intraday traders to long-term investors, each reacting to volatility measured over its own horizon. Andersen & Bollerslev (1997) show that many volatility components with different persistence, each with short memory, add up to a process with long memory.

                                                              Corsi (2009) turns this idea into a simple model, HAR (heterogeneous autoregressive). Tomorrow's volatility is explained by daily, weekly and monthly volatility, that is, by three horizons. Each component is a simple average, and the model is estimated by OLS; yet the sum of the three components produces an approximate long memory, enough to reproduce the slowly decaying autocorrelations of volatility over the horizons that matter in practice.

                                                              In brief

                                                              • FIGARCH (fractionally integrated GARCH) Baillie, Bollerslev & Mikkelsen (1996); Franke, Härdle & Hafner (2019), 14.6.2: \((1 - L)^d\) applied to \(\varepsilon_t^2\)
                                                                • shocks to volatility decay hyperbolically; \(d = 0\) gives GARCH, \(d = 1\) gives IGARCH (integrated GARCH, Chapter 9)
                                                              • Heterogeneous horizons, the FMH idea in volatility models
                                                                • Müller et al. (1997): market components with different time resolutions (intraday traders to long-term investors)
                                                                • Andersen & Bollerslev (1997): many volatility components with different persistence add up to long memory
                                                                • Corsi (2009): HAR (heterogeneous autoregressive) model, tomorrow's volatility on the daily, weekly and monthly volatility: three horizons, approximate long memory

                                                                    Pitfalls of Rolling Estimates

                                                                    Slide 58 in the PDF

                                                                    Rolling estimates have five pitfalls. The first is multiple testing: with 273 windows, each tested at the 5% level, about 14 windows would leave the band by chance even if \(H = 0.5\) in all of them. An isolated window outside the band is no evidence; only long periods outside the band are meaningful. The second pitfall is overlap: consecutive windows share 979 of their 1000 observations, so the path of the estimates is smooth by construction, and neighbouring estimates are not independent.

                                                                    The third pitfall is the window length. Shorter windows react faster to changes but have wider bands: for DFA with \(N = 250\), the 95% band has width 0.32. The fourth pitfall is volatility regimes: a crisis inside a four-year window shifts the estimate for four years, and its exit from the window produces sharp jumps, as seen for the S&P 500.

                                                                    The fifth pitfall is dating. Each estimate must be dated at the end of its window, never at its middle. An estimate dated at the middle uses data from the future (look-ahead), and a "prediction" of a crisis based on such estimates may be merely the reflection of the crisis itself, already inside the window.

                                                                    In brief

                                                                    • Multiple testing: 273 windows at 5% each give about 14 windows outside the band by chance
                                                                      • look for long runs outside the band, not for isolated windows
                                                                    • Overlap: consecutive windows share 979 of their 1000 observations; the path is smooth by construction
                                                                    • Window length: shorter windows react faster but have wider bands (DFA, \(N = 250\): width \(0.32\))
                                                                    • Volatility regimes: a crisis inside a window shifts the estimate for four years
                                                                    • Dating: date each estimate at the end of its window, never at the middle (no look-ahead)

                                                                    Scaling Risk with \(h^H\)

                                                                    Slide 59 in the PDF

                                                                    If returns are self-similar and Normal with mean zero, the \(h\)-day VaR 1% is

                                                                    \[\mathrm{VaR}_{1\%}(h) = -z_{0.01}\,\sigma\,h^H,\]
                                                                    (11.11)

                                                                    where \(z_{0.01} = -2.326\) is the 1% quantile of the \(N(0,1)\) distribution and \(\sigma\) is the daily volatility. The formula follows the convention \(\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha = -q_\alpha\), the negative of the \(\alpha\)-quantile of returns, and the factor \(h^H\) replaces the factor \(\sqrt{h}\) of the square-root-of-time rule. With a daily volatility \(\sigma = 1.2\%\), the one-day VaR 1% is \(2.326 \times 1.2\% = 2.79\%\).

                                                                    Over ten days, the scaling factor depends on \(H\). For \(H = 0.45\), the factor is \(10^{0.45} = 2.82\), and the 10-day VaR 1% is 7.87%. For \(H = 0.5\), the factor is \(\sqrt{10} = 3.16\), and the VaR is 8.83%. For \(H = 0.55\), the factor is 3.55, and the VaR is 9.91%. An exponent of 0.55 instead of 0.5 means 12% more risk at 10 days and 32% more at 250 days, because the ratio of the factors, \(h^{0.05}\), grows with the horizon.

                                                                    The data of the chapter show both directions. The 10-day ratio of standard deviations is 2.80 for the S&P 500 and 3.50 for the BET, against \(\sqrt{10} = 3.16\): the \(\sqrt{h}\) rule overstates the risk of the S&P 500 and understates that of the BET, with consequences for the 10-day VaR of Chapter 10. For derivative pricing, long memory in prices raises a theoretical problem: Rogers (1997) shows that in a market without transaction costs, fBm-type prices with \(H \neq 1/2\) admit arbitrage. This is why long memory enters option-pricing models through volatility, where it has been found empirically, not through prices.

                                                                    In brief

                                                                    • If returns are self-similar: \(h\)-day VaR 1% \(= -z_{0.01}\,\sigma\,h^H\), with \(z_{0.01} = -2.326\) (Normal case, mean 0)
                                                                      • \(z_{0.01}\): the 1% quantile of \(N(0,1)\); \(\sigma\): the daily volatility; \(h^H\) replaces the factor \(\sqrt{h}\)
                                                                      • daily volatility \(\sigma = 1.2\%\): one-day VaR 1% \(= 2.79\%\)
                                                                    • Ten days
                                                                      • \(H = 0.45\): factor \(2.82\), VaR \(7.87\%\); \(H = 0.5\): \(3.16\), \(8.83\%\); \(H = 0.55\): \(3.55\), \(9.91\%\)
                                                                      • \(H = 0.55\) instead of 0.5: 12% more risk at 10 days, 32% more at 250 days
                                                                    • Data: the 10-day ratio is \(2.80\) for the S&P 500 and \(3.50\) for the BET, against \(\sqrt{10} = 3.16\): the \(\sqrt{h}\) rule overstates S&P 500 risk and understates BET risk (Chapter 10)
                                                                    • Pricing: with fBm prices and \(H \neq 1/2\), a frictionless market admits arbitrage Rogers (1997); long memory enters option models through volatility instead

                                                                    An Open Question

                                                                    Slide 60 in the PDF

                                                                    The question is whether the change in the Hurst exponent appears before a crisis or only after it. The FMH predicts a collapse of horizons in crises, and in the data of the chapter the exponent of absolute returns rises during and after the crises of 2008 and 2020. If the exponent also changed systematically beforehand, it could serve as an early-warning indicator.

                                                                    The difficulty is that a rolling window contains the crisis only after it has begun. The rise of the exponent in the chapter's charts may therefore be merely the effect of crisis days entering the window. The question is whether there is a signal in the windows that end before the crisis begins. Earlier evidence comes from the studies of Kristoufek (2013), on investment horizons in the 2008 crisis, and Cajueiro & Tabak (2004), on emerging markets.

                                                                    The question remains open for several reasons: each sample contains few crises, the windows overlap, volatility regimes shift the estimates, and the large number of possible estimators and window lengths makes a chance finding easy. AI tools can speed up such a study, but its results must be verified (Wang et al., 2023).

                                                                    In brief

                                                                    • Does a change in the Hurst exponent come before a crisis, or only after it?

                                                                      • a rolling window contains the crisis only after it starts: is there any signal before?

                                                                    Several errors are common in long-memory analyses and must be looked for explicitly in AI-generated code. The first is dating: an estimate dated at the middle of its window uses data from the future and can "anticipate" a crisis that the window already contains. The second is the conversion between parameters: \(d = H - 0.5\), not \(H + 0.5\), and DFA applied to prices rather than returns gives \(H + 1\), not \(H\).

                                                                    The third error concerns the bands. Each \(\hat H\) needs a band computed for its own \(N\), and a comparison with 0.5 is wrong for R/S, which is biased upwards. The fourth is confusing returns with volatility: long memory in \(|r_t|\) does not make the direction of returns predictable. The fifth concerns the GPH bandwidth: \(m = T/2\), that is, all frequencies, does not give a "more precise" estimate but one biased by short-run dynamics; results are reported for \(m = T^{0.5}\) and \(m = T^{0.6}\).

                                                                    The last check concerns references. Every paper cited by an AI tool must exist, and its DOI must be verified, because invented references with plausible titles are a known failure of language models.

                                                                    In brief

                                                                    • Dating: an estimate dated at the middle of its window uses future data
                                                                    • Conversions: \(d = H - 0.5\), not \(H + 0.5\); DFA on prices gives \(H + 1\), not \(H\)
                                                                    • Bands: every \(\hat H\) needs a band for its own \(N\); R/S is biased upwards
                                                                    • Returns or volatility: memory in \(|r_t|\) does not make the direction of returns predictable
                                                                    • GPH bandwidth: \(m = T/2\) (all frequencies) is not “more accurate”; report \(T^{0.5}\) and \(T^{0.6}\)
                                                                    • References: every cited paper must exist; check the DOI

                                                                    Key Takeaways

                                                                    Slide 64 in the PDF

                                                                    The fractal market hypothesis describes the market through its investors and their horizons: stability needs investors with many horizons, and crises are collapses of these horizons. The Hurst exponent \(H\) measures memory and scaling at the same time: \(H = 0.5\) means no memory, \(H > 0.5\) persistence and \(H < 0.5\) anti-persistence; the memory parameter of ARFIMA models is \(d = H - 0.5\).

                                                                    The exponent is estimated with R/S, DFA and GPH, and short memory is tested with Lo's test, always with a Monte Carlo band computed for the same \(N\). Structural breaks and persistent GARCH volatility create spurious long memory, and the remedies are the shuffle test, splitting the sample and comparing several estimators.

                                                                    On the data, the returns of large markets have no long memory, and the BET shows a weak persistence that declines over time; volatility has long memory in all the markets studied. Multi-day risk scales like \(h^H\), so the \(\sqrt{h}\) rule is right only for \(H = 0.5\).

                                                                    In brief

                                                                    • FMH: stability needs investors with many horizons; crises are collapses of horizons
                                                                    • \(H\) measures memory and scaling: \(H = 0.5\) none, \(H > 0.5\) persistence, \(H < 0.5\) anti-persistence; \(d = H - 0.5\)
                                                                    • R/S, DFA, GPH and Lo's test, always with a Monte Carlo band for the same \(N\)
                                                                    • Breaks and persistent GARCH volatility create spurious long memory: shuffle, split, compare
                                                                    • Returns: no long memory in large markets (BET: persistence); volatility: long memory everywhere
                                                                    • Multi-day risk scales like \(h^H\)

                                                                    Slide 65 in the PDF

The R/S statistic, (11.2), grows like \(n^H\), and the regression (11.3) estimates \(H\) from its slope. The autocorrelations of fractional Gaussian noise are given by (11.6), and the ARFIMA(0,\(d\),0) model, (11.7), has autocorrelations given by the recursion \(\rho(k) = \rho(k-1)(k-1+d)/(k-d)\), with \(d = H - 0.5\).

                                                                    Check Yourself

                                                                    Slide 66 in the PDF

                                                                    The first question asks whether an R/S estimate of \(\hat H = 0.56\) on 1000 i.i.d. returns is evidence of long memory. It is not: the i.i.d. band for \(N = 1000\) is \([0.50, 0.63]\), centred around 0.562, so the estimate is exactly what the R/S estimator produces on memoryless data.

                                                                    The second question asks for the Hurst exponent corresponding to a GPH estimate \(\hat d = 0.35\) for absolute returns. The answer is \(\hat H = \hat d + 0.5 = 0.85\): long memory in volatility, not in returns.

                                                                    The third question asks how to interpret a fall in the DFA exponent of absolute returns from 0.9 to 0.5 after a random shuffle. Shuffling keeps the distribution and destroys the order, so the memory comes from the order of the days, not from the distribution or its heavy tails. The next chapter, Chapter 12, deals with scoring models.
