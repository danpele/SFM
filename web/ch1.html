A price level on its own says little about an investment. A share quoted at a few lei and an index quoted in thousands of points cannot be compared, and neither number shows how much an investor gained, how much the value fluctuated along the way or how deep the worst fall was. This chapter turns raw market data into numbers that do allow such comparisons: returns, measures of risk and drawdowns, together with their estimation error.

The chapter works throughout with real daily data for the BET index of the Bucharest Stock Exchange (BVB), the S&P 500, the DAX, Bitcoin and five BVB stocks, over the comparison period 2015–2026. The data already show the main themes of the course. Over this period 100 units invested in Bitcoin grew to 29,475, against 379 in the S&P 500, but Bitcoin's maximum drawdown was −83.4%. Daily returns have heavy tails: beyond three standard deviations the S&P 500 has 5.2 times as many days as the Normal distribution predicts. The annualised Sharpe ratio of the S&P 500, 0.72, has a standard error of 0.29, so most rankings of assets by such indicators are not statistically significant.

Before any statistic, the chapter deals with the quality of the data: the choice of the primary source, the adjustment of prices for dividends and splits, and the errors that real series contain.

Financial Data: Sources and Quality

Every result in this course starts from a data file, and every error in that file passes into the results. This section describes what financial data are, how often they are recorded, where they come from and which data the course uses. It then shows, on real series, the two operations that most often decide whether a price series can be used: the adjustment for corporate actions and the check against a second source. It ends with three systematic biases that leave no visible trace in the data but make results look better than they are.

What Financial Data Are

Slide 5 in the PDF

Financial data fall into four groups. Market data are the information produced by a trading venue: trade prices, the best bid and ask quotes, traded volume, and derived series such as index levels, exchange rates, interest rates and crypto prices. The best bid is the highest price at which someone is willing to buy at a given moment, the best ask the lowest price at which someone is willing to sell; the gap between them, the spread, is the cost of an immediate round trip.

Corporate actions form a separate group because they change the quoted price without changing the value of an investor's holding. When a company pays a cash dividend, the share price falls on the ex-dividend day by roughly the amount paid, but the shareholder receives the cash. In a split, each old share becomes several new ones at a proportionally lower price; in a consolidation (reverse split) several old shares become one. Bonus shares work like a split. A series of quoted prices that ignores these events contains jumps that no investor experienced.

Fundamental and macroeconomic data, such as balance sheets, earnings, inflation, gross domestic product (GDP) and policy rates, describe the firms and the economy behind the prices. Alternative data include news, social media, satellite images and blockchain transactions. The course works mainly with daily market data.

In brief

  • Market data: what happens on a trading venue
    • trade prices, best bid and ask quotes, traded volume
    • index levels, exchange rates, interest rates, crypto prices
  • Corporate actions: events that change the price without changing the value of a holding
    • dividends, splits, share consolidations, bonus shares
  • Fundamental and macro data
    • balance sheets and earnings; inflation, GDP, policy rates
  • Alternative data
    • news, social media, satellite images, blockchain transactions
  • This course works mainly with daily market data

From the Ticker Tape to Data Feeds

Slide 6 in the PDF

The first real-time price feed was the stock ticker, in use from 1867. The machine received trade prices by telegraph and printed them on a paper tape: the price of the last trade, a few minutes after it had taken place. Investors far from the exchange floor could follow the market almost as it happened.

Today exchanges publish electronic feeds that data vendors collect and redistribute. Every quote and every trade is recorded with a time stamp to the microsecond. Most statistical studies, including this course, do not use these records directly but their daily summaries, the OHLCV bars (open, high, low, close, volume) introduced below.

The tape survives in two forms. Moving bands of prices and news still run along building facades, such as the Reuters building in New York shown on the slide. The short ticker symbols that identify securities also come from the tape: at the BVB, TLV is Banca Transilvania, SNP is OMV Petrom and H2O is Hidroelectrica.

In brief

  • From 1867, stock tickers printed trade prices on a paper tape, sent by telegraph
    • the first real-time price feed: the price of the last trade, minutes after it happened
  • Today: electronic feeds from the exchanges, resold by data vendors
    • every quote and trade, time-stamped to the microsecond
    • daily summaries (OHLCV) are what most studies use
  • The ticker survived: scrolling prices and news on building fronts; ticker symbols such as TLV, SNP, H2O on the BVB

Data Frequency

Slide 7 in the PDF

The finest level of data is tick data: every trade and every quote. A liquid market produces millions of records per day. Tick prices carry microstructure noise; the best-known source is the bid–ask bounce, in which successive trades execute alternately at the bid and at the ask, so that the trade price jumps back and forth even when the value of the asset does not change. Intraday bars, of 1, 5 or 60 minutes, summarise the ticks and are the input of realised volatility, the subject of Chapter 8.

Daily data, with one row per trading day, are the standard of this course and of most academic studies. Exchanges trade about 250 days per year, crypto markets 365 days per year, weekends and holidays included. Weekly, monthly and annual data give fewer observations but are closer to the horizon of a long-term investor.

The frequency matters for every annualised figure in this chapter. The rule is to annualise with the actual number of observations per year of each series, about 252 for an exchange and 365 for Bitcoin, never with a single factor for all assets. The consequences of using the wrong factor are shown in the section on annualisation.

In brief

  • Tick data: every trade and quote
    • millions of records per day; noise from the bid–ask bounce
  • Intraday bars: 1, 5 or 60 minutes
    • used for realised volatility (Chapter 8)
  • Daily data: one row per trading day
    • the standard of this course and of most academic studies
    • exchanges trade about 250 days a year; crypto markets trade 365 days a year
  • Weekly, monthly, yearly data
    • fewer observations, closer to the horizon of long-term investors
  • Rule: annualise with the actual number of observations per year of each series

Where the Data Come From

Slide 8 in the PDF

The table on the slide lists six types of source. Exchanges (the BVB, Deutsche Börse, the New York Stock Exchange, NYSE) publish official prices, volumes and index levels, on their sites and through paid feeds. Data vendors such as Bloomberg, LSEG (the London Stock Exchange Group, formerly Refinitiv) and EODHD (EOD Historical Data, where EOD means end of day) gather prices from many markets, compute adjusted prices and add fundamental data, by subscription. Central banks, such as the National Bank of Romania (BNR), the European Central Bank (ECB) and the Federal Reserve, publish reference exchange rates and interest rates free of charge.

Statistical databases such as FRED (Federal Reserve Economic Data, from the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis) and Eurostat provide macroeconomic series and government bond yields. Academic databases such as CRSP (the Center for Research in Security Prices at the University of Chicago) and the Kenneth French data library provide cleaned returns, including those of delisted firms, and factor returns, under a university licence or free. Crypto exchanges and aggregators supply prices around the clock and on-chain data.

A vendor collects and cleans the data, but it is not the origin of the number. When two sources disagree, the reference is the primary source of the figure: the exchange for a closing price, the central bank for a reference exchange rate. The EUR/RON example shows why this rule matters.

Daily Bars: OHLCV

Slide 10 in the PDF

A daily bar records five numbers for day \(t\): the opening price \(O_t\), the highest price \(H_t\), the lowest price \(L_t\), the closing price \(C_t\) and the volume \(V_t\), the number of shares traded. By construction, the open and the close lie between the low and the high, \(L_t \le O_t, C_t \le H_t\). The range \(H_t - L_t\) measures how far the price moved during the day and is the basis of the range-based volatility estimators of Chapter 8.

The table shows the last five trading days of Banca Transilvania (TLV) in the course data, from 14 to 18 September 2026. Four of the rows look ordinary: the close moves between 33.40 and 35.50 lei and the volume between 346,819 and 1,884,988 shares. The bar of 17 September 2026 is different. Open, high, low and close are all equal to 33.40 lei, the same value as the close of the previous day, while the volume is 1,084,380 shares.

A flat bar is normal for an illiquid share on a day with a single trade, or for a holiday filled in with the previous price. With more than a million shares traded, a single price for the whole day is very unlikely, so the bar is suspect and has to be checked against the exchange. Simple checks of this kind, the inequalities between the four prices, flat bars and zero volumes, find many data errors before they reach any statistic.

Corporate Actions and the Adjusted Close

Slide 11 in the PDF

On the day of a corporate action the closing price \(P_t\) jumps, although the value of the holding does not change. The adjusted price removes these jumps. Each event \(k\), on day \(t_k\), has an adjustment factor \(F_{t_k}\). For a cash dividend \(D\), \(F = 1 - D/P_{t-1}\): the price falls by about \(D\) on the ex-dividend day, and scaling the earlier prices by \(F\) removes this fall. For an \(m\)-for-\(n\) split, in which \(n\) old shares become \(m\) new ones, \(F = n/m\); a consolidation is a split with \(m < n\), so its factor is greater than one and scales the earlier prices up.

The adjusted price multiplies each past price by the factors of all later events:

\[P^{\text{adj}}_t = P_t \prod_{k:\, t_k > t} F_{t_k}.\]
(1.1)

The product in (1.1) runs only over events after day \(t\), so the latest prices remain unchanged and the adjustment works backwards in time. Returns computed from adjusted prices include the dividend and contain no artificial jumps.

The same idea appears directly in the definition of the return. With a dividend \(D_t\) paid on day \(t\), the simple return is \(R_t = (P_t + D_t)/P_{t-1} - 1\) (Franke, Härdle & Hafner, 2019): the investor's gain is the change in price plus the cash received. A return computed from the adjusted close gives, up to a negligible second-order term, the same value.

In brief

  • The traded close \(P_t\) jumps on the day of a corporate action; the value of a holding does not
  • Adjustment factor \(F\) on the event day
    • cash dividend \(D\): \(F = 1 - D/P_{t-1}\)
    • split \(m\)-for-\(n\) (\(m\) new shares for \(n\) old): \(F = n/m\); a consolidation is a split with \(m < n\)
  • Adjusted close: every earlier price is multiplied by the factors of all later events, \(P^{\text{adj}}_t = P_t \prod_{k:\, t_k > t} F_{t_k}\)
    • \(t_k\): the day of event \(k\); the product runs over all events after day \(t\), so the last prices stay unchanged
    • returns computed from adjusted prices include the dividend and have no artificial jumps
  • With dividends, the simple return is \(R_t = (P_t + D_t)/P_{t-1} - 1\), with \(D_t\) the dividend paid on day \(t\) Franke, Härdle & Hafner (2019)

Worked Example: Adjusted Prices

Slide 12 in the PDF

Consider a share quoted at 100 that pays a dividend of 2 on day 5 and has a 2-for-1 split on day 10. The dividend factor is \(F_5 = 1 - 2/100 = 0.98\) and the split factor \(F_{10} = 1/2 = 0.50\). A price before day 5 is followed by both events, so it is multiplied by \(0.98 \times 0.50 = 0.49\), and 100 becomes 49. A price between days 5 and 9 is followed only by the split and is multiplied by 0.50. From day 10 onwards no event follows, and the adjusted price equals the quoted price.

The example shows a property of the adjusted series that is easy to forget: it changes every time a new event occurs. After the next dividend, every earlier adjusted price is scaled again. Two downloads of the same adjusted series made at different dates therefore differ, and a result can be reproduced only if the download date is kept together with the data.

Indices face the same choice. A price index ignores dividends, so its level falls when the constituents go ex-dividend. A total return index reinvests the dividends in the index and measures what an investor holding the constituents would have earned. The BET and BET-TR comparison shows how large the difference becomes over a decade.

In brief

  • A share trades at 100; on day 5 it pays a cash dividend of 2; on day 10 it splits 2-for-1
  • Adjustment factors
    • dividend: \(F_5 = 1 - 2/100 = 0.98\); split: \(F_{10} = 1/2 = 0.50\)
  • Adjusted prices
    • before day 5: \(P^{\text{adj}}_t = P_t \times 0.49\), so 100 becomes 49
    • between days 5 and 9: \(P^{\text{adj}}_t = P_t \times 0.50\); from day 10: \(P^{\text{adj}}_t = P_t\)
  • The adjusted series changes every time a new event happens: always keep the download date
  • Indices: a price index ignores dividends; a total return index reinvests them

        Three Biases That Make Results Look Too Good

        Slide 16 in the PDF

        Some errors do not lie in individual prices but in the way the sample is chosen or used. Survivorship bias arises when the sample contains only the firms or funds that still exist. Firms with poor results were delisted, merged or went bankrupt, so they are missing, and the average return of the survivors overstates what an investor could have earned (Brown et al., 1992). An example is the current composition of the BET traced back to 2000: the companies that left the index in the meantime are not in the sample.

        Look-ahead bias is the use of information that was not available at the date of the decision. A typical case is an annual report published in March and used in a backtest for decisions taken in January. The backtest then trades on numbers the investor could not yet know. Database dates often record the period to which a figure refers, not the date of its publication, which makes this error easy to commit.

        Data snooping is trying many rules on the same data and reporting the best one. Even if no rule works, the best of many looks significant by chance. Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016) conclude that hundreds of published predictors of returns are probably false discoveries. All three biases overstate returns, and none of them shows up in the descriptive statistics of the final sample, which look perfectly normal. They can only be prevented by the design of the study.

        In brief

        • Survivorship bias: only firms or funds that still exist are in the sample

        From Prices to Returns

        The statistical methods of this course are applied to returns, not to prices. This section gives the reasons: returns have no unit, so they can be compared across assets, and they fluctuate around a stable level, while prices follow a trend. The second property is made precise by the notion of weak stationarity and checked formally with the augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF) test on the S&P 500.

        Why Returns, Not Prices?

        Slide 19 in the PDF

        A price has a unit and a scale; a return has neither. A change of 1 leu is large for a share quoted at 2 lei and small for one quoted at 500 lei. A return expresses the change relative to the starting price, so returns of different assets, in different currencies and over different periods can be compared directly. The investor's question is also relative: by how much did my wealth change? That is exactly a return.

        The second reason is statistical. Prices follow a trend and do not return to a fixed level, so their mean and variance depend on the period observed. Returns fluctuate around a stable level. The concept behind this distinction is weak stationarity: a series is weakly stationary if its mean is constant, its variance is constant and the covariance between two observations depends only on the distance between them, the lag, not on the date.

        Most statistical methods of the course assume at least weak stationarity, because only then can averages over time estimate fixed population quantities. A sample mean of prices estimates nothing stable; a sample mean of returns estimates the expected return. The next two frames show the difference on data and test it formally.

          A Formal Check: the ADF Test (1/2)

          Slide 21 in the PDF

          The augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF) test (Dickey & Fuller, 1979) asks whether a series has a unit root, that is, whether it behaves like a random walk. The null hypothesis \(H_0\) is a unit root, so the series is non-stationary; the alternative \(H_1\) is that the series is stationary. The test regresses the daily change of the series on its level on the previous day:

          \[\Delta y_t = \alpha + \beta t + \gamma\, y_{t-1} + \sum_{j=1}^{p} \delta_j\, \Delta y_{t-j} + \varepsilon_t .\]
          (1.2)

          In (1.2), \(y_t\) is the series tested, the log price or the log return, and \(\Delta y_t = y_t - y_{t-1}\) is its change from day \(t-1\) to day \(t\). The constant \(\alpha\) and the linear trend \(\beta t\) allow for drift and trend; the \(p\) lagged changes, with coefficients \(\delta_j\), absorb short-term autocorrelation, and \(\varepsilon_t\) is the error. The key coefficient is \(\gamma\). If \(\gamma = 0\), today's change does not depend on yesterday's level: the series has no level to return to, which is a unit root. If \(\gamma < 0\), a high level is followed on average by a fall and a low level by a rise, so the series is pulled back towards its level and is stationary.

          The test rejects \(H_0\) when the \(t\) statistic of \(\hat\gamma\) lies below a critical value. The critical values do not come from the Student distribution but from the Dickey–Fuller tables, because under \(H_0\) the regressor \(y_{t-1}\) is itself non-stationary and the \(t\) statistic has a non-standard distribution, shifted to the left. The critical value also depends on whether the constant and the trend are included.

          In brief

          • ADF test (augmented Dickey–Fuller) Dickey & Fuller (1979): is there a unit root (a random walk) in the series?
            • \(H_0\): unit root, the series is non-stationary; \(H_1\): the series is stationary
          • The test regresses the daily change of the series on its previous level
            \[ \Delta y_t = \alpha + \beta t + \gamma\, y_{t-1} + \sum_{j=1}^{p} \delta_j\, \Delta y_{t-j} + \varepsilon_t \]
          • Notation
            • \(y_t\): the tested series (log price or log return); \(\Delta y_t = y_t - y_{t-1}\): its change from day \(t-1\) to day \(t\)
            • \(\alpha\): constant; \(\beta t\): linear trend; \(\delta_j\): coefficients of the \(p\) lagged changes, which absorb short-term autocorrelation; \(\varepsilon_t\): error
            • \(\gamma = 0\): the series does not return to a level (unit root); \(\gamma < 0\): it is pulled back (stationary)
          • Reject \(H_0\) when the \(t\)-statistic of \(\hat\gamma\) is below the critical value (Dickey–Fuller tables, not Student)

          Simple and Log Returns

          A return can be defined as a relative change of the price or as the change of its logarithm. The two definitions give almost the same number for a typical day and very different numbers for a crash or over a long horizon. This section defines both, measures the gap between them, illustrates it on the most extreme days of Bitcoin and the S&P 500, and gives a rule for choosing between them.

          The Simple Return

          Slide 23 in the PDF

          The simple (net) return from \(t-1\) to \(t\) is the relative change of the price (Franke, Härdle & Hafner, 2019; Tsay, 2010):

          \[R_t = \frac{P_t - P_{t-1}}{P_{t-1}} = \frac{P_t}{P_{t-1}} - 1 .\]
          (1.3)

          The ratio \(1 + R_t = P_t/P_{t-1}\) is the gross return: the value at \(t\) of one unit invested at \(t-1\). From here on, \(P_t\) denotes the closing price of an index or the adjusted price of a stock, so that dividends and splits are already taken into account.

          The simple return is bounded below: \(R_t \ge -1\). The bound expresses limited liability: the price cannot fall below zero, so a shareholder cannot lose more than the amount invested. For example, with \(P_{t-1} = 100\) and \(P_t = 110\), \(R_t = 10\%\). This is the number an investor sees on an account statement when the fund reports that it "gained 10%".

          In brief

          • Simple (net) return from \(t-1\) to \(t\) Franke, Härdle & Hafner (2019), Tsay (2010)
            • \(R_t = \dfrac{P_t - P_{t-1}}{P_{t-1}} = \dfrac{P_t}{P_{t-1}} - 1\)
            • gross return: \(1 + R_t = P_t / P_{t-1}\)
          • Bounded below: \(R_t \ge -1\) (limited liability: you cannot lose more than you invested)
          • Example: \(P_{t-1} = 100\), \(P_t = 110\): \(R_t = 10\%\)
          • It is what an investor sees on the account statement: “the fund gained 10%”
          • Notation: from here on \(P_t\) is the close of an index or the adjusted close of a stock

          The Log Return

          Slide 24 in the PDF

          The log return, also called the continuously compounded return, is the change of the logarithm of the price:

          \[r_t = \ln\frac{P_t}{P_{t-1}} = \ln P_t - \ln P_{t-1} = \ln(1 + R_t), \qquad R_t = e^{r_t} - 1 .\]
          (1.4)

          The name comes from compounding. One leu invested at a rate \(r\) compounded \(m\) times during the period grows to \((1 + r/m)^m\), and as \(m \to \infty\) this tends to \(e^{r}\). The log return is therefore the rate that, compounded continuously over the period, turns \(P_{t-1}\) into \(P_t\). With \(P_{t-1} = 100\) and \(P_t = 110\), \(r_t = \ln 1.1 = 9.53\%\), below the simple return of 10% on the same prices.

          Unlike the simple return, the log return is unbounded: \(r_t \in (-\infty, +\infty)\), and a total loss, \(P_t = 0\), corresponds to \(r_t = -\infty\). Log returns are the default in the statistics of financial markets. The main reason, shown in the next section, is that they add up over time, so that the return over a long period is the sum of the daily returns. Their range over the whole real line also suits statistical models better.

          In brief

          • Log return (continuously compounded return)
            • \(r_t = \ln\dfrac{P_t}{P_{t-1}} = \ln P_t - \ln P_{t-1} = \ln(1 + R_t)\)
            • back to the simple return: \(R_t = e^{r_t} - 1\)
          • Name: \(e^{r}\) is the growth of 1 leu at rate \(r\) compounded continuously over the period
          • Unbounded: \(r_t \in (-\infty, +\infty)\); a total loss (\(P_t = 0\)) is \(r_t = -\infty\)
          • Example: \(P_{t-1} = 100\), \(P_t = 110\): \(r_t = \ln 1.1 = 9.53\%\)
          • Log returns are the default in statistics of financial markets: they add up over time (next section)

          How Far Apart Are \(r\) and \(R\)?

          Slide 25 in the PDF

          The relation between the two returns follows from the Taylor expansion of the logarithm around zero:

          \[r = \ln(1 + R) = R - \frac{R^2}{2} + \frac{R^3}{3} - \dots\]
          (1.5)

          The logarithm is concave, so its graph lies below its tangent at zero, the line \(r = R\). Hence \(r < R\) for every \(R \neq 0\), for gains as well as for losses. For small changes the first correction term, \(-R^2/2\), gives almost the whole gap.

          For a typical daily change of \(R = 1\%\), \(r = 0.995\%\), and the approximation \(R - R^2/2\) also gives 0.995%. The gap \(R - r\) is 0.5 basis points; a basis point (bp) is 0.01 percentage points. For a large change of \(R = 10\%\), \(r = 9.531\%\), while \(R - R^2/2 = 9.500\%\), and the gap \(R - r\) is 46.9 basis points. For daily data the two returns are practically equal; for large changes and long horizons they differ, and the gap grows with the square of the change.

          In brief

              Multi-Period and Portfolio Returns

              Investors hold assets for months or years and usually hold several of them. This section builds the return over \(k\) periods from daily returns, links cumulative returns to wealth, and computes the return of a portfolio. The two aggregation rules of the previous section, sums over time for log returns and weighted averages across assets for simple returns, are applied to real data: four markets over 2015–2026 and a portfolio of two BVB stocks.

              Returns over \(k\) Periods

              Slide 31 in the PDF

              Over \(k\) periods ending at \(t\), from \(P_{t-k}\) to \(P_t\), the gross simple returns multiply and the log returns add:

              \[1 + R_t(k) = \frac{P_t}{P_{t-k}} = (1 + R_t)(1 + R_{t-1}) \cdots (1 + R_{t-k+1}), \qquad r_t(k) = \ln\frac{P_t}{P_{t-k}} = r_t + r_{t-1} + \dots + r_{t-k+1} .\]
              (1.6)

              Both identities in (1.6) follow from writing \(P_t/P_{t-k}\) as a product of \(k\) one-period ratios; taking logarithms turns the product into a sum. Here \(R_t(k)\) and \(r_t(k)\) denote the simple and the log return over the \(k\) periods.

              The additivity of log returns has an important consequence: the mean and the variance of the \(k\)-day log return follow from those of the daily returns. The mean of a sum is the sum of the means, so the \(k\)-day mean is \(k\) times the daily mean; if the daily returns are uncorrelated, the variance of the sum is also \(k\) times the daily variance. This is the basis of the annualisation rules of the next section but one.

              Weekly, monthly or annual returns are computed from the prices at the end of each period, not by averaging the daily returns within it. The average of 21 daily returns is a daily figure, not a monthly return; the monthly log return is the sum of the daily ones, or equivalently the log of the ratio of the two month-end prices.

              In brief

              • Simple: gross returns multiply
                • \(1 + R_t(k) = \dfrac{P_t}{P_{t-k}} = (1 + R_t)(1 + R_{t-1}) \cdots (1 + R_{t-k+1})\)
              • Log: returns add
                • \(r_t(k) = \ln\dfrac{P_t}{P_{t-k}} = r_t + r_{t-1} + \dots + r_{t-k+1}\)
              • \(R_t(k)\), \(r_t(k)\): the simple and the log return over the \(k\) periods that end at \(t\), from \(P_{t-k}\) to \(P_t\)
              • Consequence: the mean and variance of a \(k\)-day log return follow from those of the daily returns
              • Weekly, monthly or yearly returns are computed from the prices at the end of each period, not by averaging

              Cumulative Return and Wealth

              Slide 32 in the PDF

              Starting from wealth \(W_0\), the wealth after \(T\) periods is

              \[W_T = W_0 \prod_{t=1}^{T} (1 + R_t) = W_0 \exp\Big(\sum_{t=1}^{T} r_t\Big),\]
              (1.7)

              where \(\prod_{t=1}^{T}\) is the product of the \(T\) gross returns and \(\exp(x) = e^x\) inverts the logarithm. The two forms in (1.7) are the two identities of (1.6) applied over the whole sample.

              The cumulative simple return \(\text{CSR}_T = W_T/W_0 - 1\) is the investor's gain in percent. The cumulative log return \(\text{CLR}_T = \ln(W_T/W_0) = \sum_t r_t\) is the sum of the log returns, and the two are linked by \(\text{CSR}_T = e^{\text{CLR}_T} - 1\). The cumulative log return is the natural scale for long horizons: it grows linearly when the growth rate is constant, while the cumulative simple return grows exponentially.

              This explains why wealth charts use a logarithmic vertical axis. On a log scale, equal vertical distances correspond to equal percentage changes: a doubling from 100 to 200 and one from 1,000 to 2,000 have the same height. A constant growth rate appears as a straight line, and the slope of the line is the log growth rate.

              In brief

              • Wealth after \(T\) periods, starting from \(W_0\): \(W_T = W_0 \prod_{t=1}^{T} (1 + R_t) = W_0 \exp\Big(\sum_{t=1}^{T} r_t\Big)\)
              • \(\prod_{t=1}^{T}\): the product of the \(T\) gross returns; \(\exp(x) = e^x\) undoes the logarithm
              • Cumulative simple return: \(\text{CSR}_T = W_T/W_0 - 1\)
                • what the investor gained, in percent
              • Cumulative log return: \(\text{CLR}_T = \ln(W_T/W_0) = \sum_t r_t\)
                • link: \(\text{CSR}_T = e^{\text{CLR}_T} - 1\)
              • Log scale on charts: equal vertical distances are equal percentage changes

                              Portfolio Returns

                              Slide 34 in the PDF

                              A portfolio holds \(n\) assets with weights \(w_1, \dots, w_n\), \(\sum_i w_i = 1\), fixed at the start of the period, where \(w_i\) is the share of wealth invested in asset \(i\). Let \(R_i\) and \(r_i\) be the simple and the log return of asset \(i\) over the period. Since wealth is additive across assets, the value of the portfolio at the end of the period is \(\sum_i w_i (1 + R_i)\) times its initial value, and the portfolio return is exactly the weighted average of the simple returns:

                              \[R_p = \sum_i w_i R_i, \qquad r_p = \ln\Big(\sum_i w_i e^{r_i}\Big) \neq \sum_i w_i r_i .\]
                              (1.8)

                              The log return of the portfolio, in (1.8), is the logarithm of a weighted sum and not a weighted sum of logarithms. For example, with 60% in asset 1, with \(R_1 = 10\%\), and 40% in asset 2, with \(R_2 = -5\%\), the portfolio return is \(R_p = 0.6 \times 10\% + 0.4 \times (-5\%) = 4.0\%\), and the exact portfolio log return is \(r_p = \ln(1 + R_p) = 3.92\%\). The weighted log returns give \(0.6 \times 9.53\% + 0.4 \times (-5.13\%) = 3.67\%\), which is too small. For weights between zero and one the weighted log return is always too small, by the convexity of the exponential (Jensen's inequality).

                              The weights do not stay fixed. When one asset rises more than the other its share of wealth grows, so a portfolio with fixed weights has to be rebalanced, by selling part of the winners and buying the losers. The formula \(R_p = \sum_i w_i R_i\) uses the weights at the start of each period, so a portfolio that is rebalanced daily applies it day by day.

                              In brief

                              • Portfolio with weights \(w_1, \dots, w_n\), \(\sum_i w_i = 1\), set at the start of the period
                                • \(R_i\), \(r_i\): the simple and the log return of asset \(i = 1, \dots, n\) over the period; \(w_i\): the share of wealth in asset \(i\)
                                • simple: \(R_p = \sum_i w_i R_i\), exact
                                • log: \(r_p = \ln\big(\sum_i w_i e^{r_i}\big) \neq \sum_i w_i r_i\)
                              • Example: 60% in asset 1 (\(R_1 = 10\%\)), 40% in asset 2 (\(R_2 = -5\%\))
                                • \(R_p = 0.6 \times 10\% + 0.4 \times (-5\%) = 4.0\%\); exact \(r_p = \ln(1 + R_p) = 3.92\%\)
                                • weighted log returns: \(0.6 \times 9.53\% + 0.4 \times (-5.13\%) = 3.67\%\), too low
                              • Weights drift when prices move: a fixed-weight portfolio must be rebalanced

                              Annualisation

                              Statistics computed from daily data are daily figures, while returns and risks are usually quoted per year. This section gives the rules for annualising the mean and the volatility, defines the compound annual growth rate (CAGR) and shows that, unlike volatility, the mean return is estimated very imprecisely, however frequent the data.

                              Annualising the Mean and the Volatility

                              Slide 37 in the PDF

                              Let \(A\) be the number of observations per year: about 252 for an exchange, 365 for crypto, 52 for weekly data and 12 for monthly data. Let \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) be the mean and the standard deviation of the returns at the frequency of the data, for example daily. Since log returns add up over a year, the annual mean is the sum of \(A\) daily means, and the same rule is used for the mean of simple returns, as an arithmetic annualisation. The volatility, the standard deviation of the returns, scales with the square root of \(A\):

                              \[\mu_{\text{year}} = A\,\mu, \qquad \sigma_{\text{year}} = \sqrt{A}\,\sigma .\]
                              (1.9)

                              The second rule in (1.9) is the square-root-of-time rule. It holds when the returns are uncorrelated and have constant variance: then \(\Var(r_1 + \dots + r_A) = A\,\sigma^2\), because all the covariances vanish, and the square root gives \(\sqrt{A}\,\sigma\). With positive autocorrelation the annual variance is larger than \(A\sigma^2\), and with negative autocorrelation smaller; this is the idea behind the variance ratio tests of Chapter 7.

                              For the S&P 500, the daily standard deviation is 1.12%, so the annual volatility is \(1.12\% \times 15.87 = 17.7\%\), where \(15.87 = \sqrt{252}\). For Bitcoin, which trades every day, the factor is \(\sqrt{365} = 19.10\), which gives 66.7%. The exchange factor \(\sqrt{252}\) would give only 55.4% and understate Bitcoin's risk by more than 11 percentage points. This is why the frequency rule of the data section matters.

                              In brief

                              • \(A\) = number of observations per year: about 252 for exchanges, 365 for crypto, 52 for weekly, 12 for monthly data
                              • \(\mu\), \(\sigma\): the mean and the standard deviation of the returns at the frequency of the data (for example, daily)
                              • Mean: log returns add, so \(\mu_{\text{year}} = A\,\mu\)
                                • the same rule is used for the mean of simple returns (arithmetic annualisation)
                              • Volatility = standard deviation of returns: \(\sigma_{\text{year}} = \sqrt{A}\,\sigma\), the square-root-of-time rule
                                • valid when returns are uncorrelated with constant variance: then \(\Var(r_1 + \dots + r_A) = A\,\sigma^2\), and the square root gives \(\sqrt{A}\,\sigma\)
                              • S&P 500: daily \(\sigma = 1.12\%\), so \(\sigma_{\text{year}} = 1.12\% \times 15.87 = 17.7\%\)
                                • Bitcoin: \(\sqrt{365} = 19.10\) gives 66.7%; the stock-market factor \(\sqrt{252}\) would give only 55.4%

                              The Compound Annual Growth Rate

                              Slide 38 in the PDF

                              The CAGR (compound annual growth rate) is the constant annual rate that turns the first price \(P_0\) into the last price \(P_T\) in \(Y\) years:

                              \[\text{CAGR} = \big(P_T / P_0\big)^{1/Y} - 1 ,\]
                              (1.10)

                              with \(Y\) measured in calendar years. Taking logarithms of (1.10) gives \(\ln(1 + \text{CAGR}) = \ln(P_T/P_0)/Y\), the cumulative log return per year. Hence \(\text{CAGR} = \exp(\text{annual mean of log returns}) - 1\).

                              For the S&P 500 over 2015–2026, the annual mean of the log returns is 11.2%, and \(e^{11.2\%} - 1 = 11.9\%\), the same value as the CAGR, 11.9%. The CAGR is also called the geometric mean return, because \(1 + \text{CAGR}\) is the geometric mean of the annual gross returns.

                              The CAGR uses only the first and the last price. It says nothing about the path between them: two investments with the same CAGR can have had very different volatilities and very different falls along the way. The indicators of the performance section measure these differences.

                              In brief

                              • CAGR (compound annual growth rate): the constant yearly rate that turns \(P_0\) into \(P_T\) in \(Y\) years
                                • \(\text{CAGR} = \big(P_T / P_0\big)^{1/Y} - 1\), with \(Y\) in calendar years
                              • Link with log returns: \(\text{CAGR} = \exp(\text{annual mean log return}) - 1\)
                              • S&P 500, 2015–2026: mean log return 11.2% a year, \(e^{11.2\%} - 1 = 11.9\%\), the CAGR is 11.9%
                              • The CAGR uses only the first and the last price: it says nothing about the path in between
                              • Also called the geometric mean return

                              How Precisely Do We Know the Mean?

                              Slide 39 in the PDF

                              The standard error (SE) of the estimated annual mean \(\hat\mu_{\text{year}}\), computed from \(Y\) years of data, is

                              \[\text{SE}(\hat\mu_{\text{year}}) = \frac{\sigma_{\text{year}}}{\sqrt{Y}} ,\]
                              (1.11)

                              where the hat denotes a value estimated from the sample. The formula depends on the number of years, not on the number of observations. The 95% confidence interval is \(\hat\mu_{\text{year}} \pm 1.96\,\text{SE}\), where 1.96 is the 97.5% quantile of the standard Normal distribution.

                              For the S&P 500, with 11.7 years of data, the annual mean of the log returns is 11.2% and its SE is 5.2%. The 95% confidence interval is [1.1%; 21.4%]: the data are compatible with an expected return that is barely positive and with one that is almost twice the estimate. For Bitcoin the estimate is 47.4% with an SE of 19.5%, and the interval, \(47.4\% \pm 1.96 \times 19.5\%\), is [9.2%; 85.6%], very wide.

                              Volatility, in contrast, is estimated precisely from daily data, because every daily observation carries information about the size of the moves. The asymmetry between the two estimates, an imprecise mean and a precise volatility, recurs throughout the course, and it is the reason why ratios that divide a mean by a volatility, such as the Sharpe ratio, inherit the imprecision of the mean.

                              In brief

                              • Standard error of the annual mean: \(\text{SE}(\hat\mu_{\text{year}}) = \sigma_{\text{year}} / \sqrt{Y}\), with \(Y\) years of data
                                • \(\hat\mu_{\text{year}}\): the estimated annual mean (a hat marks an estimate from the sample)
                                • it depends on the number of years, not on the number of observations
                                • 95% confidence interval: \(\hat\mu_{\text{year}} \pm 1.96\,\text{SE}\), with 1.96 the 97.5% quantile of the standard Normal distribution
                              • S&P 500, 11.7 years: annual mean log return 11.2%, SE 5.2%
                                • 95% CI (confidence interval): [1.1%, 21.4%]
                              • Bitcoin: 47.4% \(\pm\) \(1.96 \times 19.5\%\)
                                • the interval is [9.2%, 85.6%]: very wide
                              • Volatility, by contrast, is estimated precisely from daily data

                              Descriptive Statistics of Returns

                              A return series is described first by its location and spread, then by the shape of its distribution: asymmetry, the weight of the tails and the extremes. This section defines these statistics, computes them for the indices, Bitcoin and five BVB stocks, compares the histograms with the Normal distribution and shows that volatility changes over time.

                              Location and Spread

                              Slide 42 in the PDF

                              Consider a sample \(r_1, \dots, r_n\) of daily log returns, in percent. The mean \(\bar r = \frac1n \sum_{t=1}^{n} r_t\) is the average daily return. The variance \(\hat\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n-1} \sum_{t=1}^{n} (r_t - \bar r)^2\) uses the divisor \(n - 1\), which makes it unbiased, and the standard deviation is \(\hat\sigma = \sqrt{\hat\sigma^2}\). In finance, the standard deviation of returns is called volatility.

                              The quantile \(q_\alpha\) is the value below which a fraction \(\alpha\) of the returns lies. The median is \(q_{0.5}\). The 1% quantile \(q_{0.01}\) is the basis of the value at risk (VaR): VaR 1% is \(\text{VaR}_{0.01} = -q_{0.01}\), the loss exceeded on 1% of days, written as a positive number. VaR and its backtesting are the subject of Chapter 10.

                              The minimum and the maximum are the worst and the best day of the sample. They should always be reported with their dates, because a date identifies the event behind an extreme value and is the quickest way to tell a genuine crash from a data error.

                              In brief

                              • Sample \(r_1, \dots, r_n\) of daily log returns (in %)
                              • Mean: \(\bar r = \frac1n \sum_{t=1}^{n} r_t\), the average daily return
                              • Variance: \(\hat\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n-1} \sum_{t=1}^{n} (r_t - \bar r)^2\); standard deviation \(\hat\sigma = \sqrt{\hat\sigma^2}\)
                                • in finance the standard deviation of returns is called volatility
                              • Quantile \(q_\alpha\): the value below which a fraction \(\alpha\) of returns lie
                                • median \(q_{0.5}\); the 1% quantile \(q_{0.01}\) is the basis of VaR 1% (Chapter 10)
                              • Minimum and maximum: the worst and best day, always with their dates

                              Shape: Skewness and Kurtosis

                              Slide 43 in the PDF

                              The skewness measures the asymmetry of the distribution:

                              \[\hat S = \frac{\frac1n \sum_t (r_t - \bar r)^3}{\hat\sigma^3}, \qquad \hat K = \frac{\frac1n \sum_t (r_t - \bar r)^4}{\hat\sigma^4} .\]
                              (1.12)

                              The third power in (1.12) keeps the sign of each deviation, so large losses push \(\hat S\) below zero and large gains above zero. A symmetric distribution has \(S = 0\); \(S < 0\) indicates a longer left tail, with large losses more frequent than large gains of the same size.

                              The kurtosis \(\hat K\) uses the fourth power, so the extreme days dominate it. The Normal distribution has \(K = 3\), and the excess kurtosis \(\hat K - 3\) is therefore zero for the Normal distribution. A positive excess kurtosis indicates heavy tails: extreme days more frequent than under the Normal distribution with the same mean and standard deviation.

                              Both statistics are sensitive to isolated extreme days: one crash can change the skewness from positive to negative and double the kurtosis. They should therefore be reported together with the extremes. Heavy tails and negative skewness are stylised facts of returns, regularities found across markets and periods (Cont, 2001); the formal tests are in Chapter 2.

                              In brief

                              • Skewness: \(\hat S = \frac1n \sum_t (r_t - \bar r)^3 / \hat\sigma^3\)
                                • the cube keeps the sign of each deviation, so large losses push \(\hat S\) below 0 and large gains above 0
                                • \(S = 0\): symmetric; \(S < 0\): a longer left tail, large losses more frequent than large gains
                              • Kurtosis: \(\hat K = \frac1n \sum_t (r_t - \bar r)^4 / \hat\sigma^4\); excess kurtosis \(\hat K - 3\)

                                      Performance Indicators

                                      Investors compare investments not only by how much they grew but also by how much risk they carried and how deep their falls were. This section defines the standard risk-adjusted indicators, the Sharpe, Sortino and Calmar ratios, the drawdown and volatility drag, applies them to the assets of the chapter and shows that each of them is an estimate with a sizeable error.

                                      Why One Number Is Not Enough

                                      Slide 49 in the PDF

                                      Return alone rewards risk-taking. A leveraged position, financed partly with borrowed money, multiplies both gains and losses, so it can show a high return for years, until a fall wipes it out. An indicator that looks only at return cannot distinguish a skilled manager from one who simply took more risk.

                                      An investor therefore asks three questions. How much did the investment grow? The answer is the CAGR. How large were the fluctuations? The answers are the volatility and the downside deviation. How deep was the largest fall? The answer is the maximum drawdown (MDD). Risk-adjusted indicators combine these answers by dividing a return by a measure of risk: the Sharpe ratio divides by volatility, the Sortino ratio by downside deviation, the Calmar ratio by the maximum drawdown.

                                      All these indicators are estimates from a single sample, and each has an estimation error. A ranking of assets by an indicator is informative only if the differences between them are large relative to these errors. This point recurs in every frame of the section.

                                      In brief

                                      • Return alone rewards risk taking: a leveraged bet can show a high return for years
                                      • Three questions an investor asks
                                        • how much did it grow? (CAGR)
                                        • how bumpy was the ride? (volatility, downside deviation)
                                        • how deep was the worst fall? (maximum drawdown)
                                      • Risk-adjusted indicators divide a return by a risk measure: Sharpe, Sortino, Calmar
                                      • All are estimates from one sample: each has an estimation error

                                      The Sharpe Ratio

                                      Slide 50 in the PDF

                                      The Sharpe ratio (Sharpe, 1966; Sharpe, 1994) is the excess return per unit of total risk:

                                      \[\text{SR} = \frac{\mu - r_f}{\sigma}, \qquad \text{SR}_{\text{year}} = \sqrt{A}\,\text{SR}_{\text{day}} ,\]
                                      (1.13)

                                      where \(\mu\) is the mean return, \(r_f\) the risk-free rate and \(\sigma\) the volatility. The annual ratio follows from (1.9): the numerator is multiplied by \(A\) and the denominator by \(\sqrt{A}\), so the ratio is multiplied by \(\sqrt{A}\).

                                      In this chapter the Sharpe ratio uses the mean of daily simple returns and \(r_f = 0\). The reason is practical: the assets are quoted in lei, euro and dollars, each currency with its own risk-free rate, and setting \(r_f = 0\) for all keeps the comparison simple. The ratio is then a mean return per unit of volatility rather than a strict excess return. For the S&P 500 the daily Sharpe ratio is 0.046, and the annual one is 0.72.

                                      The Sharpe ratio does not depend on leverage. Borrowing at the risk-free rate to double a position doubles both the excess return and the volatility, so the ratio stays the same. Two investments with the same Sharpe ratio thus offer the same return per unit of risk, and an investor can reach any desired level of risk with either. William F. Sharpe, shown on the slide, received the Nobel Prize in Economics in 1990.

                                      In brief

                                      • Sharpe ratio Sharpe (1966), Sharpe (1994): excess return per unit of total risk
                                        • \(\text{SR} = \dfrac{\mu - r_f}{\sigma}\); \(\mu\) mean return, \(r_f\) risk-free rate, \(\sigma\) volatility
                                        • from daily to annual: \(\text{SR}_{\text{year}} = \sqrt{A}\,\text{SR}_{\text{day}}\)
                                      • Here: mean of daily simple returns, \(r_f = 0\)
                                        • the assets are in RON, EUR and USD, each with its own risk-free rate
                                        • S&P 500: daily SR \(0.046\), annual SR \(0.72\)
                                      • Same Sharpe ratio, same return per unit of risk, whatever the leverage

                                      The Sharpe Ratio Is an Estimate

                                      Slide 51 in the PDF

                                      For returns that are i.i.d. (independent and identically distributed), Lo (2002) gives the standard error of the estimated Sharpe ratio \(\widehat{\text{SR}}\):

                                      \[\text{SE}(\widehat{\text{SR}}) \approx \sqrt{\frac{1 + \text{SR}^2/2}{n}} ,\]
                                      (1.14)

                                      per period, where \(n\) is the number of returns; the annual standard error is obtained by multiplying by \(\sqrt{A}\), the number of returns per year. For daily data the per-period ratio is small, so the term \(\text{SR}^2/2\) hardly matters, and the annual standard error is about \(\sqrt{A/n} = 1/\sqrt{Y}\): around 0.3 with 12 years of data, whatever the asset.

                                      For the S&P 500 over 2015–2026, \(\text{SR} = 0.72\) with a standard error of 0.29, and the 95% confidence interval is [0.15; 1.30]. The interval excludes zero, but it is compatible both with a modest and with an exceptionally good risk-return trade-off. Like the mean, the Sharpe ratio is known only roughly after more than a decade of data.

                                      The real error is larger than (1.14) suggests, because heavy tails and volatility clustering violate the i.i.d. assumption. A second source of error is selection: choosing the best of many strategies overstates the Sharpe ratio of the strategy chosen, and Bailey & López de Prado (2014) propose a correction for this, the deflated Sharpe ratio.

                                      In brief

                                      • Standard error for i.i.d. (independent, identically distributed) returns Lo (2002)
                                        • \(\text{SE}(\widehat{\text{SR}}) \approx \sqrt{(1 + \text{SR}^2/2)/n}\) per period; annual: multiply by \(\sqrt{A}\)
                                        • \(\widehat{\text{SR}}\): the Sharpe ratio estimated from the sample; \(n\): the number of returns; \(A\): returns per year
                                        • roughly \(1/\sqrt{Y}\) for the annual Sharpe ratio: about \(0.3\) with 12 years of data
                                      • S&P 500, 2015–2026: \(\text{SR} = 0.72\), SE \(0.29\)
                                        • 95% CI [\(0.15\), \(1.30\)]

                                        The Sortino Ratio

                                        Slide 53 in the PDF

                                        Investors fear losses, not gains, but volatility treats deviations in both directions alike. The downside deviation below a threshold \(\tau\), here \(\tau = 0\), counts only the days below the threshold:

                                        \[\sigma_D = \sqrt{\frac1n \sum_t \min(R_t - \tau, 0)^2}, \qquad \text{Sortino} = \frac{\mu - \tau}{\sigma_D} .\]
                                        (1.15)

                                        In (1.15), \(\min(R_t - \tau, 0)\) is the shortfall below the threshold on day \(t\), zero on the days above it, and \(n\) counts all the days of the sample, not only those below the threshold. The Sortino ratio (Sortino & van der Meer, 1991) divides the mean return in excess of the threshold by the downside deviation and is annualised like the Sharpe ratio.

                                        For a symmetric distribution centred near the threshold, about half of the squared deviations fall below it, so \(\sigma_D \approx \sigma/\sqrt2\) and the Sortino ratio is about \(\sqrt2 \approx 1.4\) times the Sharpe ratio. For the S&P 500 over 2015–2026 the Sharpe ratio is 0.72 and the Sortino ratio 1.02, close to this rule. A Sortino ratio well above 1.4 times the Sharpe ratio would indicate that the losses are smaller than the gains; well below, that they are larger.

                                        In brief

                                        • Investors dislike losses, not gains: the volatility treats both the same
                                        • Downside deviation below a target \(\tau\) (here \(\tau = 0\))
                                          • \(\sigma_D = \sqrt{\frac1n \sum_t \min(R_t - \tau, 0)^2}\), only days below the target count
                                          • \(\min(R_t - \tau, 0)\): the shortfall below the target on day \(t\), zero on days above it; \(n\): all days of the sample
                                        • Sortino ratio Sortino & van der Meer (1991): \(\text{Sortino} = \dfrac{\mu - \tau}{\sigma_D}\), annualised like the Sharpe ratio
                                        • For a symmetric distribution \(\sigma_D \approx \sigma/\sqrt2\), so Sortino \(\approx 1.4 \times\) Sharpe
                                        • S&P 500, 2015–2026: Sharpe \(0.72\), Sortino \(1.02\)

                                        Drawdown and Maximum Drawdown

                                        Slide 54 in the PDF

                                        The drawdown is the fall from the highest price reached so far:

                                        \[\text{DD}_t = \frac{P_t}{\max_{s \le t} P_s} - 1 \le 0, \qquad \text{MDD} = \min_t \text{DD}_t ,\]
                                        (1.16)

                                        where \(\max_{s \le t} P_s\) is the highest price over all days \(s\) up to and including day \(t\). The drawdown is zero at every new high and negative in between. The maximum drawdown (MDD) is the largest fall from a peak to the following trough. It should be reported with its dates: the peak, the trough and the recovery to the old peak, if it occurred.

                                        The depth of a drawdown matters more than it seems, because of the recovery rule: after a loss \(x\), the gain needed to return to the peak is \(x/(1 - x)\). A loss of 10% needs 11%, 20% needs 25%, 50% needs 100% and 80% needs 400%. Deep falls are much harder to recover from than their size suggests.

                                        Unlike volatility, the MDD depends on the path of the prices, and it grows with the length of the sample: the longer the history, the more chances for a deep fall (Magdon-Ismail et al., 2004). Two MDDs are comparable only over periods of the same length.

                                        In brief

                                        • Drawdown: the fall from the highest price reached so far
                                          • \(\text{DD}_t = \dfrac{P_t}{\max_{s \le t} P_s} - 1 \le 0\); zero at a new maximum
                                          • \(\max_{s \le t} P_s\): the highest price over all days \(s\) up to day \(t\)
                                        • MDD (maximum drawdown): \(\text{MDD} = \min_t \text{DD}_t\), the worst peak-to-trough fall
                                          • also report the dates: peak, trough and recovery (back to the old peak)
                                        • A loss \(x\) needs a gain \(x/(1 - x)\) to recover
                                          • 10% needs 11%; 20% needs 25%; 50% needs 100%; 80% needs 400%
                                        • The MDD depends on the path and grows with the length of the sample Magdon-Ismail et al. (2004)

                                            The Calmar Ratio

                                            Slide 57 in the PDF

                                            The Calmar ratio measures growth per unit of the largest fall:

                                            \[\text{Calmar} = \frac{\text{CAGR}}{|\text{MDD}|} .\]
                                            (1.17)

                                            It is popular among fund managers because the MDD is a loss the investor actually bears, while volatility is an abstract measure of dispersion. For the S&P 500 over 2015–2026, the CAGR is 11.9% and the MDD −33.9%, so the Calmar ratio is \(11.9/33.9 = 0.35\).

                                            The denominator of (1.17) is determined by a single event, the deepest fall of the sample. One more crash, or one less, changes the ratio much more than it would change the volatility, so the Calmar ratio is even more unstable than the Sharpe ratio. Since the MDD also grows with the length of the sample, Calmar ratios should only be compared over the same period.

                                            In brief

                                            • Calmar ratio: growth per unit of the worst fall, \(\text{Calmar} = \dfrac{\text{CAGR}}{|\text{MDD}|}\)

                                            Volatility Drag

                                            Slide 58 in the PDF

                                            For a log return with mean \(m\) and variance \(\sigma^2\), the simple return has mean approximately \(m + \sigma^2/2\); for normally distributed log returns this is exact, since \(\E[e^{r}] = e^{m + \sigma^2/2}\). Hence the growth rate of wealth, the mean log return, lies below the mean simple return:

                                            \[\mu_{\text{log}} \approx \mu_{\text{arith}} - \frac{\sigma^2}{2} ,\]
                                            (1.18)

                                            where \(\mu_{\text{arith}}\) is the mean of the simple returns and \(\mu_{\text{log}}\) the mean of the log returns. The difference \(\sigma^2/2\) is volatility drag (Booth & Fama, 1992). It comes from the concavity of the logarithm, through Jensen's inequality, and it is the same effect as in the example of +10% followed by −10%.

                                            Consider two assets with the same arithmetic mean, 10% per year. With \(\sigma = 5\%\), the drag is 0.12%, the growth rate 9.88% per year, and after 10 years 1 leu becomes 2.68. With \(\sigma = 30\%\), the drag is 4.50%, the growth rate 5.50% per year, and after 10 years 1 leu becomes only 1.73. The same average return produces very different wealth.

                                            The drag grows with the square of volatility, so doubling the volatility quadruples the drag. For low-volatility assets it is negligible; for volatile ones it becomes the main difference between the average return and the actual growth of wealth.

                                            In brief

                                            • For a log return with mean \(m\) and variance \(\sigma^2\), the simple return has mean \(\approx m + \sigma^2/2\)
                                              • so the growth rate is \(\mu_{\text{log}} \approx \mu_{\text{arith}} - \sigma^2/2\)
                                              • \(\mu_{\text{arith}}\): mean of the simple returns; \(\mu_{\text{log}}\): mean of the log returns, the growth rate of wealth
                                              • the gap \(\sigma^2/2\) is the volatility drag Booth & Fama (1992); it comes from the concavity of \(\ln\) (Jensen's inequality)
                                            • Two assets with the same arithmetic mean, 10% a year
                                              • \(\sigma = 5\%\): drag 0.12%, growth 9.88% a year; after 10 years 1 leu becomes 2.68
                                              • \(\sigma = 30\%\): drag 4.50%, growth 5.50% a year; after 10 years 1 leu becomes 1.73
                                            • Doubling the volatility quadruples the drag

                                                          An Open Question

                                                          Slide 65 in the PDF

                                                          Do Sharpe ratios persist over time? If a BVB stock had a high Sharpe ratio in 2015–2020, did it also have a high one in 2021–2026? The answer matters in practice. If past Sharpe ratios do not persist, ranking funds or stocks by their past Sharpe ratio, a common practice, is of little use to an investor choosing for the future.

                                                          The question is open for the BVB for statistical reasons. The samples are short, the number of liquid stocks is small, and a Sharpe ratio estimated on half of the chapter's sample has a standard error of about 0.4, by (1.14). With errors of this size, true persistence and pure noise are hard to tell apart.

                                                          Related results warn against optimism: many return regularities published in the literature do not hold up on new data (Harvey, Liu & Zhu, 2016). AI tools can speed up a study of this kind, from the search of the literature to the code, but they do not replace its verification (Wang et al., 2023).

                                                            • Answer: \(0.6/0.4 = 150\%\)

                                                          Section summary

                                                          From a single price series, the chapter has derived the quantities used in the rest of the course: returns of two kinds, their aggregation, annualised means and volatilities, the moments and extremes of their distribution, and risk-adjusted indicators with their standard errors. Two findings carry forward. The distribution of daily returns has heavy tails and time-varying volatility, the subject of Chapter 2 and of Chapters 8 and 9. And the mean return, with every indicator built on it, is estimated with an error that more frequent data cannot reduce.
