The previous chapters established two facts that seem to contradict each other. Daily S&P 500 returns are almost uncorrelated, so tomorrow's return cannot be anticipated from today's (Chapter 7). Their squares, however, are strongly correlated: the autocorrelation of \(r_t^2\) is 0.31 at lag 1 and still 0.053 at lag 50 (Chapter 8). Large moves follow large moves, of either sign. The daily standard deviation of the index was 0.42% in 2017, but 3.52% between September 2008 and March 2009 and 3.70% between February and May 2020.

This chapter turns the observation into a model. The return is written as an almost constant mean plus a shock whose size, the conditional volatility, depends on the past. The ARCH model of Engle (1982) makes today's variance a function of the squares of recent shocks, and the GARCH(1,1) model of Bollerslev (1986) adds yesterday's variance and obtains, with three parameters, a memory of months. The models are estimated by maximum likelihood, with Normal, Student-t or skewed-t innovations, and extended with the leverage effect (GJR-GARCH, EGARCH).

Applied to six markets, from the S&P 500 to Bitcoin and two stocks of the Bucharest Stock Exchange, the model shows clear differences: equity indices have highly persistent and strongly asymmetric volatility, while Bitcoin has volatility without mean reversion and without a leverage effect. The chapter ends with what makes the model useful in practice: volatility forecasts over several horizons, compared out of sample with EWMA, and a VaR 1% that changes from one day to the next.

            Why Model the Conditional Variance?

            Slide 8 in the PDF

            In risk management, the value at risk (VaR), the loss exceeded with a given probability, and the expected shortfall (ES), the average loss beyond the VaR, need tomorrow's volatility, not the average volatility of the last 20 years. A VaR computed with a constant variance is too large in calm years and too small in crises, exactly when it matters; Chapter 10 measures this failure with backtesting.

            The same need arises elsewhere. The weights of a minimum-variance portfolio depend on variances and covariances that change over time. The price of an option depends on the volatility expected until maturity, and hence on a volatility forecast over a given horizon.

            In statistical inference, tests and confidence intervals that assume a constant variance can be wrong when the variance changes: the usual standard errors understate the uncertainty in turbulent periods. This is why Chapter 7 needed random walk tests that remain valid under heteroskedasticity.

            In brief

            • Risk management: VaR (value at risk) and ES (expected shortfall) need tomorrow's volatility, not the average volatility of the last 20 years
              • a constant-variance VaR is too large in calm years and too small in crises (Chapter 10)
            • Portfolios: weights depend on variances and covariances, which change over time
            • Derivatives: option prices depend on the volatility expected until maturity
            • Inference: tests and confidence intervals that assume a constant variance can be wrong
              • this is why Chapter 7 needed tests that remain valid under heteroskedasticity

            Stylised Facts a Volatility Model Must Reproduce

            Slide 9 in the PDF

            The first fact is volatility clustering, measured by the autocorrelation function (ACF) of squared returns (Chapter 8). For the S&P 500, the autocorrelation of \(r_t^2\) is 0.31 at lag 1 and still 0.053 at lag 50: positive and slowly decaying, the sign of a long memory in the size of returns. The second fact is heavy tails: the kurtosis of daily S&P 500 returns is 13.7, against 3 for the Normal distribution (Chapter 2).

            The third fact is the leverage effect: price falls raise volatility more than rises of the same size (Christie, 1982). The fourth is mean reversion of volatility: after a turbulent period volatility does not stay high forever, but returns towards a long-run level. The fifth is the almost complete absence of autocorrelation in returns themselves (Chapter 7).

            A volatility model must reproduce all five facts at once: uncorrelated returns with correlated squares, heavy tails, asymmetry and mean reversion. The list serves as a yardstick for the rest of the chapter. GARCH(1,1) explains the clustering, the mean reversion and part of the tails; Student-t innovations explain the rest of the tails; the GJR-GARCH and EGARCH models explain the asymmetry.

            In brief

            • Volatility clustering: the ACF (autocorrelation function) of \(r_t^2\) is positive and decays slowly (Chapter 8)
              • S&P 500: autocorrelation of \(r_t^2\) at lag 1: \(0.31\); at lag 50: still \(0.053\)
            • Heavy tails: kurtosis above 3, the value of the Normal distribution (Chapter 2)
              • S&P 500 daily returns: kurtosis \(13.7\)
            • Leverage effect: falls raise volatility more than rises of the same size Christie (1982)
            • Mean reversion of volatility: after a storm, volatility returns to a long-run level
            • Almost no autocorrelation in \(r_t\) itself (Chapter 7)

            Daily S&P 500 returns move from a standard deviation of 0.42% in a calm year to more than 3.5% in crises, and turbulent periods last for months. Squared returns are autocorrelated over dozens of lags, returns have a kurtosis of 13.7, and falls raise volatility more than rises.             Conditional Mean and Conditional Variance

            The section defines the two conditional quantities the chapter works with, the mean and the variance of the return given past information, and writes the decomposition shared by all models of the chapter. A numerical example shows why the unconditional variance describes no particular day and why a changing variance produces heavy tails.

            Conditioning on the Past

            Slide 10 in the PDF

            Let \(\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\) be the information available at the end of day \(t-1\), here the past returns. The conditional mean \(E[X \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\) is the best forecast of \(X\) given this information, in the mean squared error sense (Chapter 4). For the return, the conditional mean is \(\mu_t = E[r_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\). Chapter 7 showed that for daily returns the past hardly helps to forecast the mean, so \(\mu_t\) can be taken as constant, \(\mu_t = \mu\).

            The conditional variance is

            \[\sigma_t^2 = \mathrm{Var}(r_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}) = E[(r_t - \mu_t)^2 \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}],\]

            and its square root, \(\sigma_t\), is the conditional volatility. It is known at the end of day \(t-1\), because it depends only on the past, and it changes from one day to the next.

            A variance that is not constant is called heteroskedasticity. Conditional heteroskedasticity means something more precise: the variance changes as a function of past information, although the unconditional variance, that of the stationary distribution of returns, can be a constant. The unconditional variance is a single number; the conditional variance is a process, which follows the market from calm periods to turbulent ones.

            In brief

            • \(\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\): the information available at the end of day \(t-1\) (past returns)
              • conditional expectation \(E[X \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\): the best forecast of \(X\) given the past (Chapter 4)
            • Conditional mean: \(\mu_t = E[r_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\)
              • Chapter 7: for daily returns, \(\mu_t\) is almost constant: \(\mu_t = \mu\)
            • Conditional variance: \(\sigma_t^2 = \mathrm{Var}(r_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}) = E[(r_t - \mu_t)^2 \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\)
              • \(\sigma_t\) is the conditional volatility: known at \(t-1\), it changes from day to day
              • heteroskedasticity: a variance that is not constant

            The Basic Decomposition

            Slide 11 in the PDF

            All models in this chapter have the form

            \[r_t = \mu + \varepsilon_t, \qquad \varepsilon_t = \sigma_t z_t.\]
            (9.1)

            The innovations \(z_t\) are i.i.d. with mean 0 and variance 1; their distribution can be Normal, Student-t or another. The shock \(\varepsilon_t\) is the return minus its mean, and the volatility \(\sigma_t\) depends only on the past. The models differ only in the equation that links \(\sigma_t\) to the past and in the distribution of \(z_t\).

            Three consequences follow from this form. Since \(\sigma_t\) is known at \(t-1\) and \(z_t\) is independent of the past, \(E[\varepsilon_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}] = \sigma_t E[z_t] = 0\): the shocks are a martingale difference. By the law of iterated expectations, \(\mathrm{Cov}(\varepsilon_t, \varepsilon_{t-k}) = E[\varepsilon_{t-k} E(\varepsilon_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1})] = 0\), so the shocks are uncorrelated. On the other hand, \(\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}) = \sigma_t^2\) is predictable, and \(\varepsilon_t^2\) is correlated with \(\varepsilon_{t-1}^2\), because \(\sigma_t\) depends on past shocks. The shocks are uncorrelated, but not independent.

            In the terms of Chapter 7, the RW1 hypothesis, with i.i.d. increments, is rejected, while the RW3 hypothesis, with uncorrelated increments, still holds. The decomposition reconciles the two facts from the start of the chapter: a market in which the return cannot be predicted can have a predictable risk.

            In brief

            • Every model of this chapter has the form \(r_t = \mu + \varepsilon_t\),   \(\varepsilon_t = \sigma_t z_t\)
              • \(z_t\): the innovations, i.i.d. with mean 0 and variance 1 (Normal, Student-t, …)
              • \(\varepsilon_t\): the shock (the return minus its mean); \(\sigma_t\) depends only on the past
            • Consequences
              • \(E[\varepsilon_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}] = \sigma_t E[z_t] = 0\): the shocks are a martingale difference, hence uncorrelated
              • \(\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}) = \sigma_t^2\): the variance is predictable
              • the \(\varepsilon_t\) are uncorrelated but not independent: \(\varepsilon_t^2\) is correlated with \(\varepsilon_{t-1}^2\)
            • In the terms of Chapter 7: RW1 (i.i.d. increments) is rejected, RW3 (uncorrelated increments) is kept

            Worked Example: Conditional and Unconditional Variance

            Slide 12 in the PDF

            Consider a market with two kinds of days: calm days, with conditional variance \(\sigma_t^2 = 0.5\), and turbulent days, with \(\sigma_t^2 = 4.5\), each with probability \(1/2\), and a conditional mean of 0. The law of total variance splits the unconditional variance into the mean of the conditional variances plus the variance of the conditional means:

            \[\mathrm{Var}(r_t) = E[\mathrm{Var}(r_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1})] + \mathrm{Var}(E[r_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]).\]

            Step by step, \(\mathrm{Var}(r_t) = 0.5 \times 0.5 + 0.5 \times 4.5 + 0 = 2.50\), so the unconditional standard deviation is \(\sqrt{2.50} = 1.58\%\). On a calm day, however, \(\sigma_t = 0.71\%\), and on a turbulent day \(\sigma_t = 2.12\%\). The average of 1.58% describes neither day: the unconditional variance is an average, while the conditional variance tells us what kind of day we are in.

            The example also shows where heavy tails come from. If \(z_t\) is Normal, \(r_t\) is a mixture of Normal distributions with different variances, and \(E[r_t^4] = 3E[\sigma_t^4]\). The kurtosis is then \(3E[\sigma_t^4]/(E[\sigma_t^2])^2\), which exceeds 3 whenever \(\sigma_t\) varies, by Jensen's inequality. In the example, \(E[\sigma_t^4] = (0.25 + 20.25)/2 = 10.25\), and the kurtosis is \(3 \times 10.25/6.25 = 4.92\). Conditional heteroskedasticity creates kurtosis even with Normal innovations.

            In brief

            • A market has calm days (\(\sigma_t^2 = 0.5\)) and stormy days (\(\sigma_t^2 = 4.5\)), each with probability \(1/2\); the mean is 0
              • law of total variance: \(\mathrm{Var}(r_t) = E[\mathrm{Var}(r_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1})] + \mathrm{Var}(E[r_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}])\)
              • step by step: \(0.5 \times 0.5 + 0.5 \times 4.5 + 0 = 2.50\), so the unconditional standard deviation is \(1.58\%\)
            • The unconditional variance is an average; the conditional variance tells which day we are in
              • a calm day: \(\sigma_t = 0.71\%\); a stormy day: \(\sigma_t = 2.12\%\); the average \(1.58\%\) fits neither
            • A mixture of Normal distributions with different variances has heavy tails: conditional heteroskedasticity creates kurtosis

            Section summary

            The conditional mean of daily returns is almost constant, while the conditional variance \(\sigma_t^2\) changes from day to day. The decomposition (9.1) produces shocks that are uncorrelated but dependent, and the mixture of calm and turbulent days produces heavy tails. The next section presents the first model for \(\sigma_t^2\), the ARCH model.

            The ARCH Model

            The ARCH model makes today's variance a linear function of the squares of recent shocks. The section presents its origin, the definition and properties of the ARCH(1) model, a comparison of simulated paths, and the ARCH(\(q\)) extension together with its main limitation: to reproduce the long memory of volatility, the model needs many lags.

            1982: Robert Engle and ARCH

            Slide 13 in the PDF

            Engle (1982) introduced the ARCH model (autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity), in which today's variance depends on the squares of yesterday's shocks. The first application was not to financial markets but to the variance of United Kingdom inflation; the paper was written during Engle's visit to the London School of Economics.

            The contribution was recognised by the 2003 Nobel Prize in economics, "for methods of analyzing economic time series with time-varying volatility (ARCH)", shared with Clive Granger, honoured for cointegration. Engle's Nobel lecture (Engle, 2004) describes the path from the original model to its use in risk management. The photographs show Robert F. Engle in 2022 and the Stockholm Concert Hall, where the Nobel Prizes are awarded.

            The idea changed the status of the variance in econometrics. Before, the variance was treated as a constant or estimated on rolling windows, without a model of its dynamics. ARCH turned the conditional variance into a parametric object, which is estimated by maximum likelihood, tested and forecast.

            In brief

            • Engle (1982): ARCH (autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity): today's variance depends on yesterday's squared shocks
              • first application: the variance of inflation in the United Kingdom; written while Engle was visiting the London School of Economics
            • Nobel Prize 2003: “for methods of analyzing economic time series with time-varying volatility (ARCH)”

            ARCH(1): Definition

            Slide 14 in the PDF

            The ARCH(1) model keeps the decomposition \(\varepsilon_t = \sigma_t z_t\) of (9.1) and specifies the conditional variance:

            \[\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\,\varepsilon_{t-1}^2,\]
            (9.2)

            with \(\omega > 0\) and \(\alpha \ge 0\). In words, today's variance is a constant plus a fraction \(\alpha\) of the square of yesterday's shock. The parameter \(\omega\) is the variance after a zero shock, and \(\alpha\) measures the reaction to yesterday's shock; the restrictions ensure a positive variance. Because the shock enters squared, a large shock yesterday, positive or negative, raises today's variance.

            The name "autoregressive" comes from a rewriting. Let \(v_t = \varepsilon_t^2 - \sigma_t^2 = \sigma_t^2(z_t^2 - 1)\), the surprise in the squared shock; \(E[v_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}] = 0\), because \(E[z_t^2] = 1\). Substituting \(\sigma_t^2 = \varepsilon_t^2 - v_t\) in the variance equation gives \(\varepsilon_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\,\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + v_t\): the squared shocks follow an AR(1) model, with an error \(v_t\) that is a martingale difference, but heteroskedastic.

            The dynamics of the squares follow. When the fourth moment exists, the ACF of \(\varepsilon_t^2\) is \(\rho(k) = \alpha^k\), as for any AR(1). The model therefore produces volatility clustering, but the autocorrelation decays geometrically at rate \(\alpha\): for \(\alpha = 0.5\) it is already \(0.5^5 \approx 0.03\) at lag 5, whereas for the S&P 500 the autocorrelation of \(r_t^2\) is still 0.053 at lag 50.

            In brief

            • \(\varepsilon_t = \sigma_t z_t\),   \(\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\,\varepsilon_{t-1}^2\),   \(\omega > 0\), \(\alpha \ge 0\)
              • in words: today's variance = a constant plus a share \(\alpha\) of yesterday's squared shock
              • \(\omega\): the variance after a zero shock; \(\alpha\): the reaction to yesterday's shock; both keep the variance positive
              • a large shock yesterday, of either sign, raises today's variance
            • “Autoregressive”: \(\varepsilon_t^2\) follows an AR(1) (autoregressive) model
              • write \(\varepsilon_t^2 = \sigma_t^2 + v_t\) with \(v_t = \sigma_t^2(z_t^2 - 1)\), \(E[v_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}] = 0\)
              • then \(\varepsilon_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\,\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + v_t\): an AR(1) for the squared shocks
            • Hence the ACF of \(\varepsilon_t^2\) is \(\rho(k) = \alpha^k\) when the fourth moment exists: clustering, but a fast decay

            ARCH(1): Properties

            Slide 15 in the PDF

            ARCH(1) shocks have mean zero and are uncorrelated, \(\mathrm{Cov}(\varepsilon_t, \varepsilon_{t-k}) = 0\) for \(k \ne 0\), like any martingale difference. The unconditional variance follows by taking the mean of the variance equation. If the process is stationary, \(\bar\sigma^2 = E[\varepsilon_t^2] = E[\sigma_t^2] = \omega + \alpha\bar\sigma^2\), so \(\bar\sigma^2 = \omega/(1 - \alpha)\), a finite and positive quantity only for \(\alpha < 1\).

            With Normal innovations and \(3\alpha^2 < 1\), the kurtosis of the shocks is

            \[K = \frac{E[\varepsilon_t^4]}{(E[\varepsilon_t^2])^2} = 3\,\frac{1 - \alpha^2}{1 - 3\alpha^2} > 3.\]

            The innovations are Normal, yet the returns are heavy-tailed (Franke, Härdle & Hafner, 2019), for the reason of the example in the previous section: \(\varepsilon_t\) is a mixture of Normal distributions with random variances. If \(\alpha \ge 1/\sqrt{3} = 0.577\), the fourth moment is infinite, although the variance remains finite for \(\alpha < 1\).

            The worked example uses \(\omega = 0.5\) and \(\alpha = 0.5\). The unconditional variance is \(\bar\sigma^2 = 0.5/0.5 = 1.00\), and the kurtosis is \(K = 3 \times 0.75/0.25 = 9.0\). After a shock \(\varepsilon_{t-1} = 2\), two unconditional standard deviations, today's variance is \(\sigma_t^2 = 0.5 + 0.5 \times 4 = 2.50\), and \(\sigma_t = 1.58\): a single two-standard-deviation shock lifts today's variance to 2.5 times its unconditional level.

            In brief

            • Mean and autocorrelation: \(E[\varepsilon_t] = 0\), \(\mathrm{Cov}(\varepsilon_t, \varepsilon_{t-k}) = 0\) for \(k \ne 0\)
            • Unconditional variance (if \(\alpha < 1\)): take expectations in \(\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\varepsilon_{t-1}^2\)
              • \(\bar\sigma^2 = E[\varepsilon_t^2] = \omega + \alpha\bar\sigma^2\), so \(\bar\sigma^2 = \omega/(1 - \alpha)\)
            • Kurtosis with Normal \(z_t\) (if \(3\alpha^2 < 1\)): \(K = \dfrac{E[\varepsilon_t^4]}{(E[\varepsilon_t^2])^2} = 3\,\dfrac{1 - \alpha^2}{1 - 3\alpha^2} > 3\)
              • Normal innovations, but heavy-tailed returns Franke, Härdle & Hafner (2019)
              • if \(\alpha \ge 1/\sqrt{3} = 0.577\), the fourth moment is infinite
            • Worked example: \(\omega = 0.5\), \(\alpha = 0.5\)
              • \(\bar\sigma^2 = 0.5/0.5 = 1.00\); \(K = 3 \times 0.75/0.25 = 9.0\); after a shock \(\varepsilon_{t-1} = 2\): \(\sigma_t^2 = 0.5 + 0.5 \times 4 = 2.50\), \(\sigma_t = 1.58\)

                                ARCH(\(q\)) and Its Limits

                                Slide 17 in the PDF

                                The ARCH(\(q\)) model includes \(q\) lags: \(\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha_1\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \dots + \alpha_q\varepsilon_{t-q}^2\), with \(\alpha_i \ge 0\), stationary if \(\sum_i \alpha_i < 1\). The coefficient \(\alpha_i\) measures the reaction to the shock of \(i\) days ago. The effect of a shock lasts exactly \(q\) days, whereas volatility clustering in real data lasts months; to reproduce it, \(q\) must be large, with many parameters constrained to be non-negative.

                                The presence of ARCH effects is tested with Engle's Lagrange multiplier test (ARCH-LM): the regression of \(\hat\varepsilon_t^2\) on its own \(q\) lags, with the statistic \(TR^2\), which has a \(\chi^2(q)\) distribution if there are no ARCH effects (Chapter 8). A rejection shows that the squared residuals are predictable from their past.

                                GARCH(1,1): Definition

                                Slide 19 in the PDF

                                The GARCH(1,1) model keeps the decomposition \(r_t = \mu + \varepsilon_t\), \(\varepsilon_t = \sigma_t z_t\) of (9.1), with the conditional variance

                                \[\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\,\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta\,\sigma_{t-1}^2,\]
                                (9.3)

                                with \(\omega > 0\), \(\alpha \ge 0\) and \(\beta \ge 0\), restrictions that keep the variance positive. Each parameter has a role. \(\alpha\) is the reaction to yesterday's news, the size of the jump in variance after a shock. \(\beta\) is the memory of the variance, the part of yesterday's level that is kept today. The sum \(\alpha + \beta\) is the persistence, and \(\omega\) fixes, together with it, the long-run level.

                                Substituting \(\sigma_{t-1}^2\), \(\sigma_{t-2}^2\), … repeatedly in the equation gives

                                \[\sigma_t^2 = \frac{\omega}{1 - \beta} + \alpha\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\beta^j\,\varepsilon_{t-1-j}^2.\]

                                GARCH(1,1) is thus an ARCH(\(\infty\)) with geometric weights: all past shocks matter, and the shock of \(j + 1\) days ago receives the weight \(\alpha\beta^j\). With \(\beta\) close to 1 the weights decay slowly, and three parameters produce the memory of months that ARCH(\(q\)) obtained only with dozens of lags.

                                The shape of the weights is the same as that of the exponentially weighted moving average of Chapter 8. The difference is the constant \(\omega/(1 - \beta)\), which pulls the variance towards a long-run level; the exact link with EWMA comes at the end of the section.

                                In brief

                                • \(r_t = \mu + \varepsilon_t\),   \(\varepsilon_t = \sigma_t z_t\),   \(\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\,\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta\,\sigma_{t-1}^2\)
                                  • \(\omega > 0\), \(\alpha \ge 0\), \(\beta \ge 0\): the variance stays positive
                                • Reading the parameters
                                  • \(\alpha\): the reaction to yesterday's news (size of the jump after a shock)
                                  • \(\beta\): the memory of the variance (how much of yesterday's level is kept)
                                  • \(\alpha + \beta\): the persistence; \(\omega\) sets the long-run level
                                • By recursion, an ARCH(\(\infty\)) with geometric weights
                                  • \(\sigma_t^2 = \dfrac{\omega}{1 - \beta} + \alpha\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\beta^j\,\varepsilon_{t-1-j}^2\): all past shocks matter, with decaying weights
                                  • the shock of \(j + 1\) days ago gets the weight \(\alpha\beta^j\)

                                Stationarity, Long-Run Variance and Kurtosis

                                Slide 20 in the PDF

                                As for ARCH, let \(v_t = \varepsilon_t^2 - \sigma_t^2\) be the surprise in the squared shock. Substituting in (9.3) gives \(\varepsilon_t^2 = \omega + (\alpha + \beta)\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + v_t - \beta v_{t-1}\): the squared shocks follow an ARMA(1,1) model (autoregressive moving average). The autoregressive part has coefficient \(\alpha + \beta\), so the ACF of \(\varepsilon_t^2\) decays like \((\alpha + \beta)^k\) at lag \(k\). With a small \(\alpha\) and \(\alpha + \beta\) close to 1, the autocorrelations of the squares are moderate but decay very slowly, as for the S&P 500.

                                The process is covariance stationary if \(\alpha + \beta < 1\). Then the unconditional variance, also called the long-run variance, is

                                \[\bar\sigma^2 = E[\sigma_t^2] = \frac{\omega}{1 - \alpha - \beta},\]
                                (9.4)

                                and the corresponding annualised volatility is \(\sqrt{252\,\bar\sigma^2}\), with 252 trading days per year. The denominator \(1 - \alpha - \beta\) is small when the persistence is close to 1, which makes \(\bar\sigma^2\) sensitive to small errors in the parameters.

                                With Normal innovations and \((\alpha + \beta)^2 + 2\alpha^2 < 1\), the kurtosis of the shocks is

                                \[K = 3\,\frac{1 - (\alpha + \beta)^2}{1 - (\alpha + \beta)^2 - 2\alpha^2} > 3.\]

                                For \(\alpha = 0.10\) and \(\beta = 0.88\), \((\alpha + \beta)^2 + 2\alpha^2 = 0.9804\) and \(K = 6.06\). The condition for a finite fourth moment is close to its limit: a slightly larger \(\alpha\), at the same persistence, makes the kurtosis infinite.

                                In brief

                                • As for ARCH: \(\varepsilon_t^2 = \omega + (\alpha + \beta)\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + v_t - \beta v_{t-1}\), an ARMA(1,1) for the squared shocks
                                  • \(v_t = \varepsilon_t^2 - \sigma_t^2\): the surprise in the squared shock, as for ARCH(1)
                                  • ARMA: autoregressive moving average; the ACF of \(\varepsilon_t^2\) decays like \((\alpha + \beta)^k\) at lag \(k\)
                                • Covariance stationary if \(\alpha + \beta < 1\); then the unconditional (long-run) variance is
                                  • \(\bar\sigma^2 = E[\sigma_t^2] = \dfrac{\omega}{1 - \alpha - \beta}\),   annualised volatility \(\sqrt{252\,\bar\sigma^2}\) for 252 trading days
                                • Kurtosis with Normal \(z_t\), if \((\alpha + \beta)^2 + 2\alpha^2 < 1\): \(K = 3\,\dfrac{1 - (\alpha + \beta)^2}{1 - (\alpha + \beta)^2 - 2\alpha^2} > 3\)
                                  • \(\alpha = 0.10\), \(\beta = 0.88\): \((\alpha + \beta)^2 + 2\alpha^2 = 0.9804\), \(K = 6.06\)

                                Persistence and Half-Life

                                Slide 21 in the PDF

                                The variance forecast \(h\) days ahead, derived in Section 11, has the form

                                \[E[\sigma_{t+h}^2 \mid \mathcal{F}_t] - \bar\sigma^2 = (\alpha + \beta)^{h-1}(\sigma_{t+1}^2 - \bar\sigma^2),\]
                                (9.5)

                                where \(E[\cdot \mid \mathcal{F}_t]\) is the forecast made at the end of day \(t\) and \(\bar\sigma^2\) is the long-run variance. The deviation of the variance from its long-run level shrinks by the factor \(\alpha + \beta\) every day. The persistence therefore says how fast the model forgets a shock.

                                The half-life is the number of days after which half of a variance shock has gone. From \((\alpha + \beta)^h = 1/2\),

                                \[h_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 0.5}{\ln(\alpha + \beta)}.\]
                                (9.6)

                                For \(\alpha + \beta = 0.90\), the half-life is 6.6 days; for 0.98 it is 34.3 days; for 0.99 it is 69.0 days. Small changes in persistence near 1 matter a great deal: moving from 0.98 to 0.99 doubles the half-life.

                                For daily stock returns, typical estimates are \(\alpha\) around 0.05–0.15 and \(\beta\) around 0.80–0.93, with \(\alpha + \beta\) close to 1. The reaction to one day's news is small and the memory is long; Section 7 confirms this pattern in six markets.

                                In brief

                                • Forecast of the variance \(h\) days ahead (derived in Section 11): \(E[\sigma_{t+h}^2 \mid \mathcal{F}_t] - \bar\sigma^2 = (\alpha + \beta)^{h-1}(\sigma_{t+1}^2 - \bar\sigma^2)\)
                                  • \(E[\cdot \mid \mathcal{F}_t]\): the forecast made at the end of day \(t\); \(\bar\sigma^2\): the long-run variance
                                  • a deviation from the long-run variance shrinks by the factor \(\alpha + \beta\) each day
                                • Half-life: the number of days after which half of a variance shock is gone
                                  • \((\alpha + \beta)^{h} = 1/2 \;\Rightarrow\; h_{1/2} = \dfrac{\ln 0.5}{\ln(\alpha + \beta)}\)
                                  • \(\alpha + \beta = 0.90\): 6.6 days; \(0.98\): 34.3 days; \(0.99\): 69.0 days: small changes near 1 matter a lot
                                • Daily equity returns: \(\alpha\) around 0.05–0.15, \(\beta\) around 0.80–0.93, \(\alpha + \beta\) close to 1 (Section 7)

                                IGARCH and the EWMA Link

                                Slide 23 in the PDF

                                The IGARCH model (integrated GARCH) (Engle & Bollerslev, 1986) is the limiting case \(\alpha + \beta = 1\). Variance shocks no longer die out: the shrinking factor in (9.5) is 1, so there is no half-life and no finite unconditional variance. The variance forecast no longer reverts to a long-run level: with \(\omega = 0\) it is the same for every horizon, and with \(\omega > 0\) it grows linearly with the horizon, \(E_t[\sigma_{t+h}^2] = \sigma_{t+1}^2 + (h - 1)\omega\).

                                The exponentially weighted moving average (EWMA) of Chapter 8, \(\sigma_t^2 = \lambda\sigma_{t-1}^2 + (1 - \lambda)r_{t-1}^2\), is an IGARCH with \(\omega = 0\), \(\alpha = 1 - \lambda\) and \(\beta = \lambda\). The RiskMetrics methodology uses \(\lambda = 0.94\) for daily data. The two methods have opposite advantages: EWMA has nothing to estimate but no mean reversion; GARCH has three parameters to estimate, and its volatility reverts to \(\bar\sigma\).

                                Question for the class: an analyst forecasts next year's volatility with EWMA right after a crash. Where does the analyst go wrong? Answer: EWMA keeps today's crisis level for the whole year, while GARCH lets it decline towards the long-run level, at the speed set by the persistence. Over a long horizon, the gap between the two forecasts is large precisely after a large shock.

                                In brief

                                • IGARCH (integrated GARCH) Engle & Bollerslev (1986): \(\alpha + \beta = 1\)
                                  • shocks to the variance never die out: no half-life, no finite unconditional variance
                                  • with \(\omega = 0\) the forecast of the variance is the same for every horizon; with \(\omega > 0\) it grows by \(\omega\) per day
                                • EWMA (exponentially weighted moving average, Chapter 8): \(\sigma_t^2 = \lambda\sigma_{t-1}^2 + (1 - \lambda)r_{t-1}^2\)
                                  • this is IGARCH with \(\omega = 0\), \(\alpha = 1 - \lambda\), \(\beta = \lambda\); RiskMetrics uses \(\lambda = 0.94\) for daily data
                                  • EWMA: nothing to estimate, but no mean reversion; GARCH: three parameters, and volatility returns to \(\bar\sigma\)

                                Estimation by Maximum Likelihood

                                The parameters of a GARCH model are estimated by maximum likelihood. The section writes the conditional log-likelihood, shows on simulated data how its shape changes with the sample size, estimates GARCH(1,1) for the S&P 500 with a direct implementation and with a dedicated package, and explains why the standard errors must be computed robustly when the innovations are not Normal.

                                The Likelihood of a GARCH Model

                                Slide 24 in the PDF

                                The returns of a GARCH model are dependent, so their joint density is not a product of marginal densities. It does, however, factor into conditional densities: with \(f\) a density, \(T\) the number of returns and \(\theta\) the parameter vector, \(f(r_1, \dots, r_T) = f(r_1)\prod_{t=2}^{T} f(r_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1})\). With Normal innovations, each factor is a Normal density, \(r_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1} \sim N(\mu, \sigma_t^2)\).

                                Conditioning on the first observation and taking logs, the conditional log-likelihood for \(\theta = (\mu, \omega, \alpha, \beta)\) is

                                \[\ell(\theta) = -\frac12\sum_{t=2}^{T}\Big[\ln(2\pi) + \ln\sigma_t^2(\theta) + \frac{(r_t - \mu)^2}{\sigma_t^2(\theta)}\Big].\]
                                (9.7)

                                Each day adds two penalties: one for a large \(\sigma_t^2\) and one for a large squared error relative to \(\sigma_t^2\). The first pushes the variance down, the second up, and the maximum balances the two. The variances \(\sigma_t^2(\theta)\) are computed recursively with (9.3), starting from an initial value \(\sigma_1^2\), for example the sample variance.

                                The maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) is \(\hat\theta = \arg\max_\theta \ell(\theta)\), subject to \(\omega > 0\), \(\alpha, \beta \ge 0\) and \(\alpha + \beta < 1\). Because \(\sigma_t^2(\theta)\) depends on the parameters through a recursion, there is no closed-form formula for \(\hat\theta\); a numerical optimisation algorithm searches for the maximum (Franke, Härdle & Hafner, 2019).

                                In brief

                                • The returns are dependent, so we factor the joint density into conditional densities
                                  • \(f\): a probability density; \(T\): the number of returns; \(\theta\): the vector of parameters; \(\ell\): the log-likelihood
                                  • \(f(r_1, \dots, r_T) = f(r_1)\prod_{t=2}^{T} f(r_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1})\), and \(r_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1} \sim N(\mu, \sigma_t^2)\) for Normal innovations
                                • Conditional log-likelihood with \(\theta = (\mu, \omega, \alpha, \beta)\):
                                  • \(\ell(\theta) = -\dfrac12\sum_{t=2}^{T}\Big[\ln(2\pi) + \ln\sigma_t^2(\theta) + \dfrac{(r_t - \mu)^2}{\sigma_t^2(\theta)}\Big]\)
                                  • in words: each day adds a penalty for a large \(\sigma_t^2\) and a penalty for a squared error that is large relative to \(\sigma_t^2\)
                                  • \(\sigma_t^2(\theta)\) is computed recursively from a starting value \(\sigma_1^2\) (for example the sample variance)
                                • MLE (maximum likelihood estimation): \(\hat\theta = \arg\max_\theta \ell(\theta)\), under \(\omega > 0\), \(\alpha, \beta \ge 0\), \(\alpha + \beta < 1\)
                                  • no closed form: a numerical optimiser searches for the maximum Franke, Härdle & Hafner (2019)

                                        Quasi-Maximum Likelihood

                                        Slide 28 in the PDF

                                        Quasi-maximum likelihood estimation (QMLE) maximises the Normal likelihood (9.7) even if \(z_t\) is not Normal. Bollerslev & Wooldridge (1992) showed that the estimates remain consistent if the mean \(\mu\) and the conditional variance \(\sigma_t^2\) are correctly specified. The reason is that the derivatives of the Normal log-likelihood have mean zero whenever the first two conditional moments are right, whatever the rest of the distribution.

                                        The classical standard errors are wrong, however, because the equality between the Hessian and the variance of the derivatives, which holds when the assumed distribution is the true one, no longer holds. The robust "sandwich" standard error combines the two matrices, \(A^{-1}BA^{-1}\), where \(A\) is the Hessian and \(B\) is the variance of the log-likelihood derivatives. When the innovations are Normal, \(A\) and \(B\) coincide and the formula reduces to the classical one.

                                        Several practical rules follow. Report the robust standard errors, which are the default in the arch package. Use at least 1000–2000 daily observations, and express returns in percent, to avoid numerical problems caused by a very small \(\omega\). An estimate on the boundary, with \(\alpha + \beta = 1\), has no ordinary standard error and is reported as IGARCH. QMLE is consistent but not efficient: a better-fitting innovation distribution gives more precise estimates, the subject of the next section.

                                        In brief

                                        • QMLE (quasi-maximum likelihood estimation): maximise the Normal likelihood even if \(z_t\) is not Normal
                                          • the estimates are still consistent if \(\mu\) and \(\sigma_t^2\) are correctly specified Bollerslev & Wooldridge (1992)
                                          • but the classic SE are wrong: use the robust (“sandwich”) SE
                                        • Practical rules
                                          • report robust SE (the default of arch)
                                          • use at least 1000–2000 daily observations; returns in % avoid numerical problems with tiny \(\omega\)
                                          • an estimate on the boundary (\(\alpha + \beta = 1\)) has no usual standard error: report it as IGARCH
                                        • A better innovation distribution gives more efficient estimates: next section

                                        Heavy-Tailed Innovations

                                        GARCH with Normal innovations explains only part of the kurtosis of returns. The section introduces Student-t and skewed-t innovations, compares them on the S&P 500 with likelihood ratio tests and checks the fit of the tails with QQ plots of the standardised residuals.

                                        Student-t and Skewed-t Innovations

                                        Slide 29 in the PDF

                                        A GARCH with Normal innovations explains part of the heavy tails through the variation of \(\sigma_t\), but not all of them. S&P 500 returns have a kurtosis of 13.7, while the standardised residuals \(\hat z_t = \hat\varepsilon_t/\hat\sigma_t\) of a Normal GARCH(1,1) have a kurtosis of 4.7. If the variance model is correct, the remaining heavy tails must come from the distribution of \(z_t\).

                                        The first choice is the standardised Student-t distribution with \(\nu > 2\) degrees of freedom (Bollerslev, 1987), from Chapter 2. If \(t_\nu\) is a Student-t variable with \(\nu\) degrees of freedom, \(z_t = t_\nu\sqrt{(\nu - 2)/\nu}\) has variance 1, as (9.1) requires. A small \(\nu\) means heavy tails: the kurtosis is \(3 + 6/(\nu - 4)\) for \(\nu > 4\), and \(\nu \to \infty\) gives the Normal distribution. The parameter \(\nu\) is estimated jointly with \((\mu, \omega, \alpha, \beta)\), by maximising the log-likelihood with the Student-t density in place of the Normal one.

                                        The second choice is the skewed-t distribution of Hansen (1994), which adds an asymmetry parameter \(\lambda \in (-1, 1)\); \(\lambda < 0\) means a left tail longer than the right one. The three models are nested, Normal \(\subset\) t \(\subset\) skewed t, so they can be compared with likelihood ratio tests or with the AIC and BIC criteria (Chapter 6).

                                        In brief

                                        • GARCH with Normal \(z_t\) explains only part of the kurtosis: S&P 500 returns \(13.7\), standardised residuals \(\hat z_t = \hat\varepsilon_t/\hat\sigma_t\): \(4.7\)
                                          • the remaining heavy tails must come from the distribution of \(z_t\)
                                        • Standardised Student-t with \(\nu > 2\) degrees of freedom (Chapter 2) Bollerslev (1987)
                                          • \(t_\nu\): a Student-t variable with \(\nu\) degrees of freedom; \(z_t = t_\nu\sqrt{(\nu - 2)/\nu}\) has variance 1
                                          • a small \(\nu\) means heavy tails: kurtosis \(3 + 6/(\nu - 4)\) for \(\nu > 4\); \(\nu \to \infty\): the Normal distribution
                                          • \(\nu\) is estimated together with \((\mu, \omega, \alpha, \beta)\)
                                        • Skewed t Hansen (1994): one more parameter \(\lambda \in (-1, 1)\); \(\lambda < 0\): a longer left tail
                                          • nested models: Normal \(\subset\) t \(\subset\) skewed t; compare them with likelihood-ratio tests or AIC/BIC (Chapter 6)

                                                          Asymmetry and the Leverage Effect

                                                          GARCH(1,1) responds to the size of a shock, not to its sign. The section presents the explanations of the leverage effect, the two standard asymmetric models, GJR-GARCH and EGARCH, the news impact curve as a tool for comparing them, and measures the asymmetry in six markets.

                                                          The Leverage Effect

                                                          Slide 37 in the PDF

                                                          The leverage effect of Section 1, documented by Christie (1982), has two explanations. The leverage explanation proper: a lower share price raises the firm's debt-to-equity ratio, so the share becomes riskier. The volatility feedback explanation: news of higher risk raises the return investors require, and prices fall at once. Causality runs in opposite directions in the two explanations, but both link price falls to higher volatility.

                                                          GARCH(1,1) cannot capture the effect, because \(\sigma_t^2\) depends on \(\varepsilon_{t-1}^2\), not on the sign of \(\varepsilon_{t-1}\). Signs that something is missing have already appeared: the QQ plot of Section 6 had a left tail that was too deep, and the skewed-t distribution gave \(\hat\lambda < 0\).

                                                          The tool for comparing models in this respect is the news impact curve (NIC) of Engle & Ng (1993): the variance \(\sigma_t^2\) as a function of yesterday's shock \(\varepsilon_{t-1}\), with \(\sigma_{t-1}^2\) fixed at the unconditional variance. For GARCH(1,1), the curve is the parabola \(\omega + \alpha\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta\bar\sigma^2\), symmetric around zero; a model with a leverage effect has a curve that is steeper on the left than on the right.

                                                          In brief

                                                          • The leverage effect of Section 1 Christie (1982) has two explanations
                                                            • leverage explanation: a lower share price raises the debt-to-equity ratio, so the equity becomes riskier
                                                            • volatility feedback: news of higher risk lowers prices at once
                                                          • GARCH(1,1) cannot see it: \(\sigma_t^2\) depends on \(\varepsilon_{t-1}^2\), not on the sign of \(\varepsilon_{t-1}\)
                                                            • the QQ plot of Section 6 and the skewed t (\(\hat\lambda < 0\)) already pointed to the left tail
                                                          • NIC (news impact curve) Engle & Ng (1993): \(\sigma_t^2\) as a function of \(\varepsilon_{t-1}\), with \(\sigma_{t-1}^2\) fixed at the unconditional variance

                                                          GJR-GARCH and EGARCH

                                                          Slide 38 in the PDF

                                                          The GJR-GARCH model of Glosten, Jagannathan & Runkle (1993) adds a term that is active only after negative shocks:

                                                          \[\sigma_t^2 = \omega + (\alpha + \gamma\,I_{t-1})\,\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta\sigma_{t-1}^2,\]
                                                          (9.8)

                                                          where \(I_{t-1} = 1\) if \(\varepsilon_{t-1} < 0\) and \(I_{t-1} = 0\) otherwise. The variable \(I_{t-1}\) thus flags a negative shock yesterday, and \(\gamma\) is the extra reaction to bad news. After good news, the slope in \(\varepsilon_{t-1}^2\) is \(\alpha\); after bad news it is \(\alpha + \gamma\); a leverage effect means \(\gamma > 0\). For symmetric \(z_t\), half of the shocks are negative, so the persistence is \(\alpha + \beta + \gamma/2\), not \(\alpha + \beta\).

                                                          The EGARCH model (exponential GARCH) of Nelson (1991) works with the log of the variance:

                                                          \[\ln\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\big(|z_{t-1}| - E|z_{t-1}|\big) + \gamma z_{t-1} + \beta\ln\sigma_{t-1}^2.\]
                                                          (9.9)

                                                          The term \(|z_{t-1}| - E|z_{t-1}|\) is the size of yesterday's standardised shock above its mean (\(E|z| = \sqrt{2/\pi}\) for Normal \(z\)), and \(\gamma z_{t-1}\) depends on the sign. Because the model describes \(\ln\sigma_t^2\), the variance stays positive without sign restrictions on the parameters. A leverage effect here means \(\gamma < 0\): a negative \(z_{t-1}\) raises \(\ln\sigma_t^2\). The persistence is \(\beta\).

                                                          The two models measure the same asymmetry in different ways: GJR through different slopes of a parabola, EGARCH through a linear reaction in \(z_{t-1}\) on a log scale. Both, as well as threshold GARCH, are covered in Franke, Härdle & Hafner (2019), Section 13.2.

                                                          In brief

                                                          • GJR-GARCH Glosten, Jagannathan & Runkle (1993): \(\sigma_t^2 = \omega + (\alpha + \gamma\,I_{t-1})\,\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta\sigma_{t-1}^2\), \(I_{t-1} = 1\) if \(\varepsilon_{t-1} < 0\), else 0
                                                            • \(I_{t-1}\): an indicator of a negative shock yesterday; \(\gamma\): the extra reaction to bad news
                                                            • good news: slope \(\alpha\); bad news: \(\alpha + \gamma\); leverage effect: \(\gamma > 0\)
                                                            • persistence \(\alpha + \beta + \gamma/2\) for symmetric \(z_t\) (half of the shocks are negative)
                                                          • EGARCH (exponential GARCH) Nelson (1991): \(\ln\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\big(|z_{t-1}| - E|z_{t-1}|\big) + \gamma z_{t-1} + \beta\ln\sigma_{t-1}^2\)
                                                            • \(|z_{t-1}| - E|z_{t-1}|\): the size of yesterday's standardised shock above its average (\(E|z| = \sqrt{2/\pi}\) for Normal \(z\))
                                                            • the logarithm keeps \(\sigma_t^2 > 0\) without sign restrictions on the parameters
                                                            • leverage effect: \(\gamma < 0\) (a negative \(z_{t-1}\) raises \(\ln\sigma_t^2\)); persistence: \(\beta\)
                                                          • Textbook treatment of both, and of the threshold GARCH: Franke, Härdle & Hafner (2019), Section 13.2

                                                              Checking a Fitted Model

                                                              Slide 41 in the PDF

                                                              If the model is correct, the standardised residuals \(\hat z_t = (r_t - \hat\mu)/\hat\sigma_t\) are close to i.i.d., with mean 0 and variance 1. Each diagnostic test checks one consequence of this property. The Ljung–Box (LB) statistic (Ljung & Box, 1978), \(Q(m) = T(T+2)\sum_{k=1}^{m}\hat\rho_k^2/(T-k)\), where \(\hat\rho_k\) is the sample autocorrelation at lag \(k\), has a \(\chi^2(m)\) distribution under the hypothesis of no autocorrelation. Applied to \(\hat z_t\) with \(m = 10\), it checks whether autocorrelation in the mean remains (Chapter 7).

                                                              Applied to the squares \(\hat z_t^2\), together with the ARCH-LM test, the same statistic checks whether ARCH effects remain (Chapter 8). The QQ plot of \(\hat z_t\) checks the assumed distribution, as in Section 6. The sign bias test of Engle & Ng (1993) regresses \(\hat z_t^2\) on \(I_{t-1}\), \(I_{t-1}\hat\varepsilon_{t-1}\) and \((1 - I_{t-1})\hat\varepsilon_{t-1}\), where \(I_{t-1}\) flags a negative shock. The joint test \(TR^2 \sim \chi^2(3)\) asks whether the sign and size of past shocks still predict the variance; a rejection means the model lacks asymmetry.

                                                              The logic of diagnostics is asymmetric. A test without rejection does not prove that the model is correct: it only shows that this test finds no departure. A rejection, on the other hand, shows where the model fails and what must be added.

                                                              In brief

                                                              • If the model is right, the standardised residuals \(\hat z_t = (r_t - \hat\mu)/\hat\sigma_t\) are close to i.i.d. with mean 0 and variance 1

                                                                • \(Q(10)\) on \(\hat z_t^2\) and the ARCH-LM test: no ARCH effects left (Chapter 8)
                                                                • QQ plot of \(\hat z_t\): is the assumed distribution right? (Section 6)

                                                                • joint test \(T R^2 \sim \chi^2(3)\): a rejection means the sign of past shocks still predicts the variance (asymmetry is missing)
                                                              • A passed diagnostic does not prove the model right; a failed one shows where it is wrong

                                                                        Volatility Forecasts

                                                                        The section derives multi-step GARCH forecasts and the variance of the return over several days, applies them to the S&P 500 on 18 September 2026, shows the term structure of volatility at three different moments and compares GARCH and EWMA forecasts out of sample, with the QLIKE loss function and the Diebold–Mariano test.

                                                                        Multi-Step Forecasts

                                                                        Slide 45 in the PDF

                                                                        The one-day forecast is known exactly at the end of day \(t\): \(\sigma_{t+1}^2 = \omega + \alpha\varepsilon_t^2 + \beta\sigma_t^2\). For two days, writing \(E_t\) for the mean conditional on the information at the end of day \(t\), \(E_t[\sigma_{t+2}^2] = \omega + \alpha E_t[\varepsilon_{t+1}^2] + \beta\sigma_{t+1}^2 = \omega + (\alpha + \beta)\sigma_{t+1}^2\), because \(E_t[\varepsilon_{t+1}^2] = \sigma_{t+1}^2\). The unknown future shock is replaced by its mean, that is, by its variance.

                                                                        Repeating the argument and subtracting the long-run variance \(\bar\sigma^2 = \omega/(1 - \alpha - \beta)\) gives, by induction, the forecast (9.5), \(E_t[\sigma_{t+h}^2] = \bar\sigma^2 + (\alpha + \beta)^{h-1}(\sigma_{t+1}^2 - \bar\sigma^2)\), where \(h\) is the horizon in days. The forecast converges to the long-run variance, from above after a turbulent period and from below after a calm one, at the speed set by \(\alpha + \beta\).

                                                                        The variance of the return over \(H\) days is the sum of the daily forecasts, because returns are uncorrelated:

                                                                        \[\mathrm{Var}_t(r_{t+1} + \dots + r_{t+H}) = \sum_{h=1}^{H} E_t[\sigma_{t+h}^2],\]
                                                                        (9.10)

                                                                        not \(H\sigma_{t+1}^2\). The square-root-of-time rule, \(\sigma_{t+1}\sqrt{H}\), assumes a constant variance at tomorrow's level. It gives values that are too large in a crisis, when the variance is expected to fall, and too small in a calm period, when the variance is expected to rise.

                                                                        In brief

                                                                        • One step: \(\sigma_{t+1}^2 = \omega + \alpha\varepsilon_t^2 + \beta\sigma_t^2\) is known at the end of day \(t\)
                                                                          • two steps: \(E_t[\sigma_{t+2}^2] = \omega + \alpha E_t[\varepsilon_{t+1}^2] + \beta\sigma_{t+1}^2 = \omega + (\alpha + \beta)\sigma_{t+1}^2\), since \(E_t[\varepsilon_{t+1}^2] = \sigma_{t+1}^2\)
                                                                        • By induction: \(E_t[\sigma_{t+h}^2] = \bar\sigma^2 + (\alpha + \beta)^{h-1}(\sigma_{t+1}^2 - \bar\sigma^2)\)
                                                                          • \(E_t\): the expectation given the information at the end of day \(t\); \(h\): the horizon in days
                                                                          • the forecast converges to the long-run variance; the speed is set by \(\alpha + \beta\)
                                                                        • Variance of the \(H\)-day return: the sum of the daily forecasts (returns are uncorrelated)
                                                                          • \(\mathrm{Var}_t(r_{t+1} + \dots + r_{t+H}) = \sum_{h=1}^{H} E_t[\sigma_{t+h}^2]\), not \(H\sigma_{t+1}^2\)
                                                                          • the square-root-of-time rule \(\sigma_{t+1}\sqrt{H}\) is too high in a crisis and too low in a calm period

                                                                          How to Evaluate a Volatility Forecast

                                                                          Slide 48 in the PDF

                                                                          The true variance \(\sigma_t^2\) is never observed, so a forecast \(h_t\) is compared with a noisy proxy, here \(r_t^2\). The proxy is unbiased, \(E_{t-1}[r_t^2] = \sigma_t^2\) when \(\mu \approx 0\), but very noisy, because \(r_t^2 = \sigma_t^2 z_t^2\) and \(z_t^2\) varies a lot from day to day. Hence a regression of \(r_t^2\) on the forecast has a low \(R^2\) even for a perfect forecast (Andersen & Bollerslev, 1998): a low \(R^2\) does not mean the model is weak.

                                                                          The QLIKE (quasi-likelihood) loss function (Patton, 2011) is

                                                                          \[L(r_t^2, h_t) = \frac{r_t^2}{h_t} + \ln h_t,\]
                                                                          (9.11)

                                                                          up to a constant, where \(h_t\) is the variance forecast for day \(t\) made at the end of day \(t-1\); the smaller value is better. It is, up to constants, minus the Normal log-likelihood (9.7). Patton (2011) showed that QLIKE ranks forecasts correctly even when a noisy but unbiased proxy of the variance is used. It penalises forecasts that are too low more than forecasts that are too high, a property suited to risk management.

                                                                          The evaluation is out of sample: from 2015, each forecast uses only data up to the previous day, and the models are re-estimated every 250 days. The Diebold–Mariano (DM) test (Diebold & Mariano, 1995) asks whether the mean loss difference between two forecasts is zero. Its statistic is a \(t\) ratio of this mean difference, computed with heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation consistent (HAC) standard errors.

                                                                          In brief

                                                                          • The true \(\sigma_t^2\) is never observed: we compare the forecast \(h_t\) with a noisy proxy, here \(r_t^2\)
                                                                            • \(E_{t-1}[r_t^2] = \sigma_t^2\) (with \(\mu \approx 0\)), but \(r_t^2\) is very noisy: low \(R^2\) even for a perfect forecast Andersen & Bollerslev (1998)
                                                                          • QLIKE loss Patton (2011): \(L(r_t^2, h_t) = \dfrac{r_t^2}{h_t} + \ln h_t\) (up to a constant); smaller is better
                                                                            • \(h_t\): the variance forecast for day \(t\), made at the end of day \(t-1\)
                                                                            • ranks forecasts correctly even with a noisy proxy; it is minus the Normal log-likelihood
                                                                            • penalises forecasts that are too low more than forecasts that are too high
                                                                          • Out of sample: from 2015, each forecast uses only data up to the day before; the models are re-estimated every 250 days

                                                                                VaR 1% from a GARCH Model

                                                                                The section combines the volatility forecast with the quantile of the innovations to obtain a VaR that changes from one day to the next.                                                                                 Conditional VaR

                                                                                Slide 50 in the PDF

                                                                                The VaR at level \(\alpha\) is the loss exceeded with probability \(\alpha\): \(\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha = -q_\alpha(r_{t+1})\), where \(q_\alpha\) is the \(\alpha\)-quantile of the return (Chapter 6). In this section \(\alpha\) is the VaR level, not the GARCH parameter. With \(\alpha = 1\%\) we obtain the VaR 1%, a loss exceeded, on average, on one day in a hundred.

                                                                                With \(r_{t+1} = \mu + \sigma_{t+1}z_{t+1}\), the quantile of the return is \(\mu + \sigma_{t+1}q_\alpha(z)\), because \(\mu\) and \(\sigma_{t+1}\) are known at the end of day \(t\). Then

                                                                                \[\mathrm{VaR}_{t+1} = -\big(\mu + \sigma_{t+1}\,q_\alpha(z)\big).\]
                                                                                (9.12)

                                                                                For Normal innovations, \(q_{0.01} = -2.326\). For standardised t innovations, \(q_{0.01} = t_\nu^{-1}(0.01)\sqrt{(\nu - 2)/\nu}\), where \(t_\nu^{-1}(0.01)\) is the 1% quantile of the Student-t distribution with \(\nu\) degrees of freedom, and the square root rescales it to variance 1. The VaR changes every day with \(\sigma_{t+1}\): it is large in turbulent periods and small in calm ones.

                                                                                The worked example uses the GARCH(1,1)-t model for the S&P 500 on 18 September 2026, with \(\hat\mu = 0.079\), \(\sigma_{t+1} = 0.714\) and \(\hat\nu = 6.26\). The innovation quantile is \(q_{0.01}(z) = -3.100 \times 0.825 = -2.557\), and the VaR 1% \(= -(0.079 + 0.714 \times (-2.557)) = 1.75\%\). With the Normal quantile, the same computation gives 1.58%. Over 10 days, with the variance from the example of the previous section, 5.67, and without \(\mu\), we obtain about \(2.557 \times \sqrt{5.67} \approx 6.09\%\); the computation is an approximation, because the sum of ten GARCH-t returns does not have exactly the t distribution of a single day.

                                                                                    An Open Question

                                                                                    Slide 53 in the PDF

                                                                                    Is Bitcoin volatility becoming more like the volatility of an equity index? Over the full sample, the contrast is clear. Bitcoin is an IGARCH, without mean reversion, with extremely heavy-tailed innovations (\(\hat\nu = 3.18\)) and no leverage effect. The S&P 500 has mean-reverting volatility and a strong leverage effect. Since 2024, exchange-traded funds (ETFs) on Bitcoin have brought investors from the equity market into the crypto market, which could bring the dynamics of the two markets closer.

                                                                                    Earlier work has studied related questions. Pele et al. (2023) ask whether crypto assets are becoming an alternative asset class, and Pele, Lazar & Dufour (2017) treat volatility and entropy as information about market risk.

                                                                                    The question remains open because several changes overlap in time: regime changes, the crypto crash of 2022 and the launch of the ETFs. The answer depends on the chosen window, and a change in the GARCH parameters may reflect any of these events. AI tools can speed up such a study, but its results must be checked (Wang et al., 2023).

                                                                                    What to Check

                                                                                    Slide 55 in the PDF

                                                                                    Several errors are common in AI-generated GARCH estimates. The first concerns units: returns must be expressed in percent, not in decimals, otherwise the optimiser may stop at a wrong \(\omega\), very close to zero. The second concerns persistence: for GJR it is \(\alpha + \beta + \gamma/2\), not \(\alpha + \beta\), and for EGARCH it is \(\beta\); code that reports \(\alpha + \beta\) for GJR understates the memory.

                                                                                    Boundary estimates, with \(\alpha + \beta = 1\), have no half-life and no long-run volatility; they are reported as IGARCH, not as infinite values or as huge numbers produced by dividing by almost zero. Calendars differ: Bitcoin trades 7 days a week, the S&P 500 five, so each series is annualised with its own frequency, and daily persistences are not directly comparable without this correction.

                                                                                    Rolling windows overlap, so consecutive estimates share most of their data and are not independent evidence. Finally, every cited paper must exist: its DOI must be checked, because invented references with plausible titles are a known failure of language models.

                                                                                    In brief

                                                                                    • Units: returns in % (not decimals), otherwise the optimiser may stop at a wrong \(\omega\)
                                                                                    • The persistence of GJR is \(\alpha + \beta + \gamma/2\), not \(\alpha + \beta\); for EGARCH it is \(\beta\)
                                                                                    • Estimates on the boundary (\(\alpha + \beta = 1\)): no half-life, no long-run volatility; do not report infinite numbers
                                                                                    • Calendars: Bitcoin trades 7 days a week, the S&P 500 five; annualise each series with its own frequency
                                                                                    • Rolling windows overlap: consecutive estimates are not independent evidence
                                                                                    • References: every cited paper must exist; check the DOI

                                                                                    Key Takeaways

                                                                                    Slide 57 in the PDF

                                                                                    Daily returns have an almost unpredictable mean and a predictable variance, \(r_t = \mu + \sigma_t z_t\). GARCH(1,1), with three parameters in the variance equation, captures volatility clustering in every series of the chapter, with persistence \(\alpha + \beta\) close to 1. The models are estimated by maximum likelihood, reported with robust standard errors, and the tails are described with Student-t or skewed-t innovations.

                                                                                    The S&P 500 and the DAX need asymmetry: for these indices, GJR-GARCH and EGARCH clearly improve the model, while for the BET the effect is weak. Bitcoin has no leverage effect and is an IGARCH.

                                                                                    GARCH forecasts revert to the long-run level, and GARCH beats EWMA out of sample where volatility reverts to the mean. The VaR 1% \(= -(\mu + \sigma_{t+1}q_{0.01}(z))\) depends both on the volatility model and on the quantile of the innovations: EWMA with the Normal quantile is exceeded more than twice too often, GARCH-t much less often.

                                                                                    In brief

                                                                                    • Returns: unpredictable mean, predictable variance: \(r_t = \mu + \sigma_t z_t\)
                                                                                    • GARCH(1,1) with three parameters captures volatility clustering in all our series; \(\alpha + \beta\) is close to 1
                                                                                    • Estimate by maximum likelihood, report robust SE; Student-t or skewed-t innovations for the tails
                                                                                    • The S&P 500 and the DAX need asymmetry (GJR, EGARCH); for the BET it is weak (BIC prefers the symmetric GARCH); Bitcoin does not need it, and is an IGARCH
                                                                                    • Forecasts revert to the long-run level; GARCH beats EWMA out of sample where volatility mean-reverts
                                                                                    • VaR 1% = \(-(\mu + \sigma_{t+1}q_{0.01}(z))\): the volatility model and the quantile both matter

                                                                                    Key Formulas

                                                                                    Slide 58 in the PDF

                                                                                    The table collects the chapter's formulas. ARCH(\(q\)) and GARCH(1,1), (9.3), give the conditional variance, and the long-run variance, (9.4), is the constant divided by one minus the persistence. The half-life, (9.6), turns the persistence into days.

                                                                                    GJR-GARCH, (9.8), and EGARCH, (9.9), add the leverage effect. The Normal log-likelihood, (9.7), is the function maximised in estimation and, up to constants, the QLIKE loss function, (9.11), used to evaluate forecasts. The multi-step forecast, (9.5), and the conditional VaR, (9.12), are the two formulas that turn the model into a risk tool.

                                                                                    All forecast formulas depend on the persistence through powers of \(\alpha + \beta\), and the long-run variance depends on it through the denominator \(1 - \alpha - \beta\). When the persistence is close to 1, small errors in its estimate produce large errors in long-horizon forecasts.

                                                                                    In brief

                                                                                    QuantityFormula
                                                                                    ARCH(\(q\))\(\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \sum_{i=1}^{q}\alpha_i\varepsilon_{t-i}^2\),   \(\bar\sigma^2 = \omega/(1 - \sum\alpha_i)\)
                                                                                    GARCH(1,1)\(\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta\sigma_{t-1}^2\),   \(\bar\sigma^2 = \omega/(1 - \alpha - \beta)\)
                                                                                    Half-life\(h_{1/2} = \ln 0.5/\ln(\alpha + \beta)\)
                                                                                    GJR-GARCH\(\sigma_t^2 = \omega + (\alpha + \gamma I_{t-1})\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta\sigma_{t-1}^2\)
                                                                                    EGARCH\(\ln\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha(|z_{t-1}| - E|z_{t-1}|) + \gamma z_{t-1} + \beta\ln\sigma_{t-1}^2\)

                                                                                    QLIKE\(L = r_t^2/h_t + \ln h_t\)

Only negative shocks raise the variance.
